UNTILabout thirty years ago, Michael Sendivogius (1566-1636) was chiefly recognized for
his remarkable life as a nobleman of Slavic origin who supposedly possessed the secret of
transmuting base metals into gold.1 During his lifetime he was reputedly caught up in
remarkable escapades with emperors and kings, involving him inimprisonment and torture,
and hewas thus awell-known personality throughout Europe. He retained this reputation in
the nineteenth century; in 1867,the Polish artist Jan Matejko painted a famous incident-
Sendivogius holding a transmuted gold coin in front ofking Sigismund IIIVasa and his
court. Even today, it is possible to buy in Poland a slide strip entitled, Jak Mistrz S~dziw6j
latal, or How Maestro Sendivogius flew, which describes his escape from a burning tower in
which he was imprisoned. With biographical interest focused on his colourful adventures, it
is scarcely surprising that Sendivogius has not been recognised as having contributed
significantly to philosophy or science although historians ofchemistry have expressed some
interest in him, because of a work entitled De Lapide Philosophorum [On the Philosophers'
Stone], and also known asNovum Lumen Chymicum (translated into English asA New Light of
Alchemy). This isarguably attributable toSendivogius and had awide readership throughout
Europe during the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. Until recently, opinions expressed
by historians, even Poles, have not been sympathetic. For example, in 1954 Emilian
Ostachowski wrote:
One could therefore divide alchemists into twogroups. In the firstgroup oneought to
include those who practised the art ofalchemy forfinancial reasons only, and in t!Ie
other-those whose activity was prompted by the thirst for knowledge. S~DZIWOJ
ought to be reckoned rather among the first group, forhe did not enrich the science
with any valuable contribution.2
In his encyclopedic History of Chemistry, Partington included Sendivogius among the major
chemists ofhis time, but concluded that: 'Even the existence ofSendivogius is doubtful.,3
In more recent studies however, scholarly research has confirmed that Sendivogius did
exist, and that hewas the author ofseveral short works, which had asignificant influence on
the development ofcertain branches ofnatural philosophy throughout Europe during the
seventeenth and eighteenth centuries.4 Sendivogius was born near Sq,czin southern Poland
on 2 February 1566, and died in 1636. He studied at various universities including Leipzig,
Vienna and Altdorf. He served asboth an alchemist and adiplomat at the courts ofthe King
ofPoland, Sigismund IIIVasa (at Cracow), and Emperors Rudolph IIand Ferdinand IIat
Prague and Vienna. His best known work, the De Lapide Philosophorum, which was first
published in Prague in 1604, went through more than fiftyeditions in fivelanguages before
1797.The aim ofthe present article is to elucidate Sendivogius' chemical theory. A second
article will show how the theory played an important role in the development ofchemical
sciences, giving a new perspective on the long and complex history ofideas which preceded
the formal recognition ofoxygen at the end ofthe eighteenth century.

THE BACKGROUND TO SENDIVOGIUS' WRITINGS
In order to set the scene for a detailed analysis ofSendivogius' writings, it is necessary to
discuss two issues briefly: the 'state ofthe art' with respect to nitre and its role in chemistry
and chemical philosophy, and the interpretation ofalchemical writings in general.
During the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, potassium nitrate, otherwise known as
saltpetre ornitre, played an important roleinthe development ofcertain aspects ofchemical
philosophy because ofitswide variety ofproperties, its ready accessibility and its numerous
applications. Quercetanus (Du Chesne), in his work translated into English in 1591as The
True &perfect spagerikepreparation ofMinerals, animalles, and vegiatables with theyrusesummarized
the properties and uses ofnitre as follows:

verie profitable against many infirmities, and worketh twocontrarie effectes,the first

Thus nitre was used asacomponent ofcooling mixtures, medicines, gunpowder and aliquid
which could dissolve metals. It is therefore not surprising that it appealed to chemical
philosophers as a subject ofsome interest.
Indeed, nitre had featured inthework ofoneofthe most important ofthese philosophers,
Philippus Theophrastus Aureolus Bombast von Hohenheim (1493-1541), otherwise known
asParacelsus. In hisinterpretation ofmatter, heaccepted, inaccordance with contemporary
ideas on matter theory, the existence ofthe four Aristotelian primary elements: earth, fire,
air and water, out ofwhich all matter iscomposed. To these he added three principles: salt,
sulphur, and mercury. He believed that a combination ofthe Aristotelian elements with his
three principles would provide abetter frame ofreference forexplaining natural phenomena.
The introduction ofsalt as aprimary principle brought with it afresh perspective on awide
range of substances which had been generally classified as salts. Paracelsus drew special
attention tothese, including nitre. Through hishermetico-occult vision ofthe world interms
of his salt-sulphur-mercury theory, he elevated the role of nitre from merely that of a
versatile substance with many practical applications, to one with a more profound
significance. Paracelsus wrote of the vital role of nitre as a salt in living organisms.
Furthermore, hedrew parallels between hisobservations ofthe behaviour ofgunpowder and
ofthunder and lightning. From these, he deduced that ifan 'earthy' nitre and sulphur were
responsible for the explosive violence of man-made gunpowder, then an 'aerial' nitre and
sulphur must be responsible for the God-made phenomena ofthunder and lightning.6

The other background issue to a consideration of Sendivogius' works is the problem of
the interpretation of alchemical texts, due to the nature of alchemy itself, to the limited
audience forwhich they were intended and tothe terminology they employed.7 Alchemy, by
its very nature, involved a physical, or practical aspect and a metaphysical, or theoretical
aspect. The practical aspect related tothe manipulation and handling ofsubstances whereas
the metaphysical aspect related toexperiences ofthe mind only.8The language used in each
approach was frequently similar, and this therefore raises the question ofwhether a given
passage relates to an actual physical process or a spiritual process. Alchemists frequently
employed a complex and obscure style ofwriting, in order to communicate only to the true
adepts of alchemical knowledge, or the 'sons of Hermes' as they were sometimes known.
These were the alchemists who had studied the writings of the ancient philosophers and
were thus deemed worthy ofsharing the great responsibility ofpossessing the knowledge of
how to make the universal elixir, or the Philosopher's Stone. Further interpretive problems
arise from the fact that alchemists frequently used many names to denote one substance.
Conversely, they could use one name to denote different substances. This is because they
named things as they pleased in local terms, often according to source, properties, or
purpose.

SUMMARY AND CONCLUSION
Three aspects ofSendivogius' writings have been given particular attention: (a) hisinterest
in air and its role in life processes and combustion; (b) the Central Salt, or nitre, as the
substance which contains within itself that part of air whichis necessary for the above
mentioned processes, and (c)theCentral Salt, ornitre, fromwhich the universal solvent can
be prepared, and hence the Philosophers' Stone.
The theory, aswehave seen,was supported bySendivogius' ownpractical expertise and
experience. Unlike Paracelsus, Quercetanus and Palissy, who had written before him,53
Sendivogius repeated his ideas on nitre and its vital role in the universe, several times and
with reference tovarying situations-this givesgrounds forbelieving that heconsidered these
ideas to constitute a major contribution to the understanding ofnatural processes.
Although thelanguage and terminology used byhim wasbynomeans unambiguous and
isfrequently difficult tointerpret on account ofits complex style, there issufficient evidence
toshow that hewas the author and originator ofagrand newtheory which was based on the
chemistry of'Central Nitre' or potassium nitrate. (See Figure I).
For many centuries, alchemy had been preoccupied with metallic transmutation.
Paracelsus had already redefined its objective as primarily the preparation of chemical
remedies to cure all manner of diseases. Sendivogius, stimulated in part by Paracelsian
teachings, played a major role in directing attention to a part of the air involved in
generation, growth, and the maintenance of life. The principle hinted at in the Tabula
Smaragdina) supposedly known tothe writers ofthe classic texts ofalchemy, and searched for
in vain by alchemists through the centuries, was for Sendivogius the 'aerial food' or the
Central Nitre. (See Figure 2.)
