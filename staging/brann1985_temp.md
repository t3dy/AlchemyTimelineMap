As Girolamo indicated in his version ofpeculiarly alchemical melancholy, however, the

The program ofalchemical mysticism embraced two basic principles which could also be
found at the heart of non-alchemical mysticism: firstly, a principle of inward spiritual
catharsis or purification by afluid and fire-like cosmic spirit which descends to the soul from
a transcendent source and simultaneously cleanses it and lifts it to a higher state ofspiritual
being; and, secondly, a principle of ascent by dialectical negation, the dynamic process of
which ismarked atevery stage by t\vOcontradictory passions: melancholy sorrow for the loss
of what has been left behind, and rapturous joy instigated by the alacritous anticipation of
what is still to come. The apologists of alchemy realized that if they were to succeed in
reversing the common prejudice ofits opponents, who held their art to be at best a fruitless
vanity and at worst a diabolically inspired art of seduction, they needed to downplay the
materialistic motives behind their behavior and thrust into the forefront oftheir speculations
the spiritual motives underlying their extrinsic alchemical transmutations. Accordingly,
they established as a cornerstone of their occult operations the axiom that success in the
transmutation of base metals into gold is dependent upon a corresponding inward
transmutation of the alchemical operator's soul into "spiritual gold". Much as Christian
mystics in general began their purgative ascent to Heaven with amelancholy "dark night of
the soul", so did the medieval and Renaissance alchemical mystics in particular begin their
purgative journey with an experience ofmelancholy "darkness" or nigredo, corresponding to
lead among the metals and to the planet Saturn among the planets.

The footing for alchemical theory in medieval and Renaissance mystical speculation can
be illustrated many times over, since the image of purification by fire was a frequently
employed literary device of the mystical writers. Among the Christian fathers, for example,
St. Ambrose (d. 397) maintained that only by passing through the "fire" (ignis) of worldly
tribulation are we enabled to test the true purity ofour divine and immortal souls; just as
gold and silver are tested by fire, and precious stones are cleansed of their filth and
illuminated by fire, likewise does the spiritual fire enkindled and brought to full flame by the
tribulations of earthly adversity "cleanse the soul" of its contaminations.7 Picked up by St.
Benedict and incorporated into his rules for a monastic life, which he imagined to be a kind
ofpurgative crucible for the transmutation ofthe souls ofmonks from soft and base "lead" to
hard and pure "gold",8 the image of catharsis by fire was given one of its most illustrious

The question remains, nevertheless, ofhow a view ofmelancholy as a positive auxiliary

ideas needed to come into association which belonged to separate medieval traditions. The
first, formulated apart from medieval medical theory by the patristic and medieval
theologians, comprised a mystical elaboration of St. Paul's appeal (II Cor. 7:10) to a

medical theory before it could be applied to religious theory, recognized in the cold and dry
humor of melancholy a combustible character which could materially assist in the
conversion of sorrow from its diabolical into its divine form.
Prior to their re-evaluation of "melancholy sorrow", the medieval mystical theorists
needed to distinguish the concept of sorrow from another concept with which it had
commonly been identified bythe theologians, that ofsloth (acedia) among the seven principal
or "capital" vices.11It should be evident that there islittle to salvage from avice portrayed

outwardly resembling but inwardly distinguishable passion ofsorrow (tristitia), initsvicious

Albertus' image of melancholia jumosa was likewise a favored device of the mystical
theorists. Sometimes, as in the case of the French mysticJean Gerson (d. 1429), this image
seemed to convey more of a negative than positive signification. In a treatise describing
mystical meditation Gerson qualified his definition of meditatio cordis as follows:
Meditation is a powerful and vehement application or attentiveness of the mind in
the act of fruitfully searching out or discovering something. I use the word
"fruitfully" (fructuose) with deliberation, lest meditation incline to suspicion, to
curiosity, or to melancholic stupor.21
In his major mystical work, the De monte contemplationis, Gerson elaborated on the theme of
meditation, together with its attending danger ofstultifying melancholy, by comparing the
act of meditatio cordis to the enkindling of a piece of wood. The lighted wood first begins to
burn with considerable smoke (fumus), after which the flame (ignis) becomes brighter and the
quantity ofsmoke becomes reduced; finally, after the wood has been thoroughly reduced to
charcoal, it emits a "pure, brilliant, and lucid fire". A parallel sequence takes place in
mystical meditation, characterized by a spiritual form of combustibility which, in its first
stage, produces a kind of smoke which is "obscure and greatly vexing" and "bitter to
endure". In spite of these unpleasant feelings, however, the soul of the solitary mystic is
required to pass through this dark and bitter "smoke ofdiscontent with its past life" (fumum
displicentiae de vita sua praeterita) before it is ready to attain to the higher stages of
contemplation signified by "pure love which isfree ofsmoke" (amor purus sinejumo).22 Gerson
was anxious, however, that the "dark night of the soul" indicated in these passages not be
confused with melancholy.
The condition ofmelancholy, Gerson maintained, isnot a healthy and licit outgrowth of
the meditative process, but an unhealthy and illicit sign ofits sabotage by the Devil. Though

concurred with their non-alchemical colleagues that humoural melancholy, with its
potential for combustibility, iscapable ofacting as asuitable natural auxiliary for that form
of purgative sorrow which is "following God". The Christian alchemists, that is to say,
confirmed the insight ofthe non-alchemical mystics that the theological doctrine oftristitia
secundum Deum is variantly expressible as melancholia secundum Deum, the equivalent of dark
and tortuous nigredo which isthe essential first stage ofthe alchemist inhis ardent pursuit of
Lapis-Christ.

The present volume is, as the title proclaims, a selection from those publications (about
70, apart from reviews) in various periodicals and books. They are intended to "illustrate
aspects ofmedieval intellectual and scientific endeavor"-the seven liberal arts, especially
the quadrivium (arithmetic, geometry, music and astronomy); alchemy; medicine as an art;
star-science, particularly in relation to man; and bibliophilia ofthe late 13th, the 14th and
15th centuries.
