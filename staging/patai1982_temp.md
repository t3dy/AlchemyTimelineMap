I. INTRODUCTORY
IN the writings ofHellenistic, Arab, and European alchemists one finds constant references
to and quotations from numerous suppositious ancient practitioners of the Hermetic Art.
Alchemical expertise and secret knowledge were attributed to Biblical figures beginning
with Adam, to Egyptian kings, to Persian satraps, to Greek gods, heroes, and philosophers,
and to many other mythical and historical personages. While the origins of these tradi-
tions are elusive, the claim itself that the "Royal Art" was founded by gods, kings, and
nobles can easily be understood: it reflects a desire on the part of its adepts to endow
alchemy with a respected, prestigious, not to say glorious, ancestry.
Hard historical facts give an entirely different picture. They show that the first actual
non-fictitious alchemists lived-at least as far as can be ascertained on the basis of the
extant literary documents-in Hellenistic Egypt. And the earliest among them was a
woman, known as Maria Hebraea, Maria the Hebrew, or Maria the Jewess, who, as will be
shown, deserves to be styled the founding mother ofwestern alchemy.
II. WHEN DID MARIA LIVE?
The earliest, most detailed, and most reliable information about Maria the Jewess is
contained in the writings of Zosimus of Panopolis.
Zosimus himself is a rather shadowy figure. Nothing is known of his life, despite the
fact that he left behind numerous Greek alchemical treatises.! However, most scholars
agree that Zosimus lived in the late third and early fourth centuries A.D., that he was from
Panopolis in the Thebais but resided inAlexandria, and that hewasby far the most import-
ant of the early alchemical authors.2
For the most part, Zosimus' writings consist of extensive quotations from other, older
alchemical authorities. The ones whom he quotes most frequently are (Pseudo-) Demo-
critus to whom he refers as "the Philosopher", and Maria the Jewess whom he calls in most
casessimply Maria, although occasionally he givesher the accolade ofstyling her "the divine
Maria" .3 Zosimus considered the Jews in general the repositories of alchemical wisdom.
The sacred art of the Egyptians, he wrote, and the power of gold which resulted from it,
were revealed only to the Jews, by fraud, and they made it known to the rest ofthe world.4
Zosimus held that the Jews' knowledge ofalchemy was greater and more reliable than that
of any other people, including the Egyptians. In one remarkable section entitled "A Dis-

III. MARIA'S INSTRUMENTS
From the numerous references Zosimus makes to Maria one can piece together apicture
of her contribution to the theory and practice of Hellenistic alchemy. To begin with al-
chemical apparatus, there are several which she either invented, or whose description she
provided-from the way Zosimus quotes her it is impossible to say which of these two
alternatives was the case.
Maria constructed and described numerous ovens, apparatus for cooking and distilling,
made of metal, clay, and glass. She connected, tightened, and calked the various parts
these instruments comprised by means of fat, wax, starch-paste (size), fatty clay, and the
"clay ofthe philosophers".l1 She considered glass vessels especially useful, because "they
see without touching", and permit the safe manipulation of dangerous materials, such as
mercury, which she describes as "the deadly poison, since it dissolves gold, and the most

a vessel under a plate into which were placed vaporizable substances capable of attacking
metals, while over it was an inverted cup in which the vapours were condensed to liquid. On
the plate itself were placed the metals to be treated.17

V. HER DOCTRINES

namely her doctrines about the nature ofnature which underlay allher alchemical work and
which she herself proffered in numerous enigmatic axioms as the basis ofthe procedures she
describes and recommends.
From what Zosimus says on the subject one gathers that Maria held that all substances
or bodies found in nature were basically one. This, of course, was the alchemical doctrine

feminine principle, earth, the regions under the earth, and evil itself, are interpolated

applied to Muslim alchemical schools which were ignorant of, or unconcerned about,
Christian dogma. Nor can one assume that Maria, who, asweshall seeanon, was a devoted
and fervent Jewess,had Christian dogma inmind when coining her famous axiom. It seems
much more likely that wehave here an encapsulated and purposely enigmatic reference to a

tradition that she, on one occasion at least, shrieked enthusiastically when expressing the
essence of her insight into a fundamental alchemical procedure (p. 182).
Line 2. "She fixes the fugacious matter with the double gum", refers to the volatile
material which, Maria taught, must be fixed and which she did fix with the use of the white
gum and the red gum (pp. 182,184).
Line 3. The "tubes" into which Maria ties "three powerful substances" are the three
tubes of the tribikos invented or described by her (p. 179).
Line 4. "Binds a band in three hours" refers to the alchemical tradition according to
which Maria performed the "Great Work" in three hours, binding together, or uniting,
various substances (p. 191).
Line 5. "Daughter ofPluto" is an allusion to Maria's ability to make gold, and thereby
becoming, as it were, the daughter of Pluto (Hades) to whom Greek myth attributes
fabulous riches. "Unites love's affinities" refers to Maria's teaching according to which the
transmutation consists of amarriage, a union, ofmale and female substances (pp. 181, 183,
190, 192).
Line 6. "Delights in things roasted" refers to the burning ofthe tetrasomia, the copper,
or the sulphur prescribed and described by Maria (pp. 180, 183, 186, 192). Things "sown"
refers to the mysterious white herb on the mountain (PP.I91-2). "Assembled in threes"
refers either to the tribikos (see above, line 3), or to the three kinds of "divine water", the
whitened, yellowed, and blackened one(p. 181), or to Maria's doctrine according to which
two must become three (p. 182).
