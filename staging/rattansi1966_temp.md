within a fundamentally Aristotelian framework, that is, a framework which
assumed contact-action as the basis of efficient causation, ruled out action at
a distance thr~ugh sympathy-antipathy and the world-soul, and was in-

means.33 The Aquinian view, in other words, had the corollary that the
extraordinary use of "natural" la\vs to produce extraordinary effects in nature

the superhuman effort needed to produce marvellous effects through a know-

which men could aspire. Raleigh \vas not concerned to stress this "scholastic-

The comparison of Raleigh's views on alchemy and natural magic ,vith
those of James I and Bacon displays the wide spectrum of opinions held on
these topics in early seventeenth-century England, and the manner in which
these opinions were connected with more general attitudes to the study of
natural philosophy.
They differed about the aims which were to guide the study of nature.
For James, too great a prying into the secrets of nature was dangerous, and
he was satisfied with a knowledge which would serve limited utilitarian
purposes. Raleigh did ~ot wish to harness the study of nature to any broad
social ends. He favoured knowledge above utility and thought it right that
those who had won knowledge through hard work and expense should keep it
secret. Bacon, on the other hand, saw no conflict between experiments of
"light" and experiments of "use", and envisioned a new science of nature
which would be devoted to human welfare and would transform human life.
They differ, too, in their allegiance to the major traditions .within which
the study of nature was pursued in their own times, and the procedures and
explanatory models characteristic of each of these traditions. James' position
is basically aligned with the "scholastic Aristotelian" one, and Raleigh's with
the "high" Platonism of the Renaissance. Only Bacon wished to break with
both traditions, and formulate radically novel methods and explanations.
Their responses to alchemy and natural magic are linked with these basic
commitments. James set narrow bounds to the "natural"; whatever trans-
gressed those bounds was "demonic". Against that view, Raleigh sought to
claim a wide area of operation for natural magic. Bacon, while convinced
that "there remaineth at this day a world ofinventions and sciences unknown"
comparable with "the new-found world of land", 70 believed they would be
discovered not by the discredited methods of the alchemists and magicians,
but through a new and "purified" natural magic.
Of these three thinkers, it is Bacon who points most significantly at the
new science of nature which was to emerge in a coherent form by the mid-
century. We cannot here examine the wide gap which was to separate his
aspirations and his achievement. While wishing to reject the two traditions
which he saw as dominating the natural philosophy of his own time, "the
philosophy of the Grecians and the philosophy of the Alchemists", his own
work retained a neo-Aristotelian flavour in its search for "forms", while he
was forced to rely on natural-magical authors for much of his data.71
