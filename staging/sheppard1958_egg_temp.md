THE ORIGINSOFEGGSYMBOLISM
THOUGHof prime importance in alchemy the Egg symbol was by no means
confined to that art. Of great antiquity, it probably appeared for the first time
in the many creation myths of the countries in which the first great civilizations
emergedl; it occurs, for example, in several forms as a World Egg in the mythol-
ogies ofEgypt, where alchemy itself was later to crystallize out ofan amalgam of
early technology, philosophy and Gnostic beliefs.
The similarities between many ofthe creation myths sointrigued mythologists
that they made frequent attempts to trace their origins to a single country,
though it cannot be said that these efforts were very successful. Comparative
mythology is an amorphous subject, based upon racial and lingual classifications
which hang together by slender threads, and Lobeck2 was probably near the
truth when he said of a common origin for the World Egg, "quaerere ludicrum
est". The racial migrations whicl:toccurred in proto-historic times are in most
cases conjectural, though Guha has shown convincingly that the population of
parts of India by the early Proto-Australoid race from the Mediterranean was
responsible for the occurrence today of features which are not common to other
areas of the sub-continent-features such as totemistic beliefs and creation
myths involving the World Egg3. Thus, elements of uncertain but great antiq-
uity (pre-4ooo B.C.) eventually became distorted or transformed into the
mythical forms met with today in the Sanskrit Puranas and in popular Hindu
folklore. From India the Proto-Australoid race moved further east to Melanesia
and Polynesia4, where we find also the myths ofcreation from an Egg.
Linguistic researches, too, point to the same movement eastwards from the
Mediterranean, for Przyluski has traced the movement of the Austric language
along the path followed by the Proto-Australoidss. But to infer in general that
the World Egg had a single origin in the eastern Mediterranean would be un-
warranted, for it can hardly account for the presence of the Egg in the myths of
those other countries more widely separated and unrelated ethnically.

The origins ofprimitive symbolism are better sought in the functioning ofthe
primitive mind. Given the same simple surroundings and the same undeveloped
mentality, the human mind, like the body, must have everywhere passed through
similar stages of development. There seems to be no valid reason, then, why
primitive men in different lands should not have evolved the same theories
concerning the origin and the working of the universe. The explanation lies
in the formulation ofbeliefs; true belief rests upon perception or upon inferences
verified by perception, whereas false belief seems to depend upon imagination.
From earliest times "picture-thinking" has been of inestimable value in aiding
man to comprehend what cannot be reproduced as precepts but only imagined
by constructing a picture out of concepts. Reason expresses itself here by
imagination; and in the comprehension of the cosmic process imagination seized
upon the creative process commonly observed within the animal world. By this
means the World Egg most probably arose as a universal symbol of creation.
With the elaborate alchemical symbolism of later times the process of
adoption was a complex one. The formulation oftrue symbols, as distinct from
mere signs, isattributed by Jung to the" collective unconscious", that part ofthe
unconscious mind which has been progressively conditioned by the impressions
and experiences received by our fore-runners6. As a result of this conditioning,
the unconscious mind projects primordial images, or archetypes, which are
manifested as symbols appearing in dreams or in hallucinations; thus, the
Philosopher's Egg of alchemy becomes a symbol of the primordial chaos out of
which the living world emerged. In the words of Jung7, it is "the chaos appre-
hended by the artifax, the prima materia containing the captive world-soul.
Out of the egg-symbolized by the round cooking-vessel-will rise the eagle or
phoenix7a, the liberated soul, which is ultimately identical with the Anthropos
who was imprisoned in the embrace of Physis". Regarded psychologically,
then, the Philosopher's Egg takes its place in an interpretation of alchemy as a
symbolic redemption of the soul of man according to Orphic-inspired Gnostic
conceptions of regeneration.
However, in alchemy the Egg symbol occurs in such different forms and con-
texts that in the early texts, at any rate, it was probably a conscious representa-
tion ofan egg, ie. in the terminology ofJung, was asign-a representation ofthe
real thing. In later works only would it appear that a true symbol arose by the
psychological projection described.

STAGES IN THE MANIFESTATION OF THE SYMBOL
Throughout the long history of alchemy there appear to be three distinct
stages of development of the sYmbol, each succeeding form gathering to itself
greater complexity of expression and meaning. These phases are, in order of
appearance :-
(i) A simple literal expression, accompanied, if at all, by only the simplest
pictorial representation.
(ii) The fashioning of an Hermetic Vessel into a form indicative of its
gestatory function.
(iii) A more complex pictorial representation of the Egg, symbolic of the
mystical aspects ofthe Work. This form ofimagery isconfined almost
entirely to the later adepts and, insofar as the Hermetic Vessel is
concerned, is divorced from practical considerations.
In the descriptions ofthese stages which followit should berealized that each
stage retained and co-existed with previous ones.
LITERAL SYMBOLISM
Early Hellenistic alchemy provides abundant examples of this type8; in
most cases aparallel isdrawn between the parts ofthe Egg and the ingredients of
the Work. The Egg had various arbitrary denominations: to some it was the
image ofthe world, to others stone ofcopper, stone ofArmenia, stone ofEgypt,
etc. Again, the shell was likened to the earth or to various metals, while the
designations of the contents were equally numerous and diverse. Thus, the
white was called gum, fig-juice, tithYmal juice and other vegetable notations;
and the yellow likened to Attic ochre, mineral of hard copper, Cilician saffron

The Chrysopoeia of Kleopatra consists of a single page portraying apparatus
and symbolsll, one of which, a circle, may represent the Egg or cinnabar, the
latter being a term applied at times to substances other than mercuric sulphide.
Assuming that the symbol applied to mercuric sulphide, it is difficult to resist
the light-hearted suggestion that this arose out of the ability of cinnabar to
yield upon heating small globules of mercury which, by coalescence, formed
egg-like droplets! However, the assimilation of the symbol to cinnabar most
likely arose, as Sherwood Taylor suggested to the writer, from the fact that
cinnabar itself iscomposed ofthe yellow (sulphur) and the white (mercury) like
the egg-which, again, corresponds to gold and silver.
In the Letter to Petesios, attributed to Pseudo-Ostanes (first two centuries
A.D.?)12, are given the practical details for preparing a "divine Water". The
author, who resorts to allegory, warns the reader of the true intent ofhis recipe
by placing alchemical signs above words which must be interpreted in a different
sense from the literal. Thus, the probable method of preparing the Divine
Water is as follows: "Taking the eggs of the serpent of oak (sign of mercury
placed above) which dwellsinthe month ofAugust onthe mountains ofOlympus
(sign of cinnabar placed above) or Libanus or Taurus, put them when they are
freshinto aglassvessel ... ". This evidently refers to the preparation ofmercury
from cinnabar and implies the identity ofthe Egg and mercury. The same idea
isexpressed in a Cairo MS.referred to by Stapleton13, wherein Zosimos remarks,
"They (the alchemists) have called this secret 'The Egg' :but all ofthem (really)
mean 'Mercury'."
Earlier inthis Cairo MS.welearn, too, that during his visit to Egypt, Ostanes
informed the Egyptians ofthe eastern belief that eggscontained the same essen-
tial nature as the brain of the pig, regarded by Egyptians as the First Principle
of the Work14•
This identity ofthe Egg with the ingredients ofthe process isshown again in
the description which Sherwood Taylor gave of Zosimos' use of real eggs in
distillation15• The products described correspond closely to those actually
obtained in such a distillation: firstly, a clear distillate called "rain-water";
secondly, a pale golden liquid, "oil of radish"; and finally, a dark yellowish-
green liquid, "castor-oil". As Sherwood Taylor suggested,' the probable aim
was the extraction ofthe life-giving pneuma, in addition to the golden sulphurous
liquids which could be used for tingeing other materials.

THE HERMETIC VESSEL
Following the primary conception of the rebirth of matter from the Egg,
there came the all-important task of bringing about in vitro that which theory
attributed to nature. For this purpose the apparatus was ofno lesssignificance
than the materials themselves; the creation analogy was carried a stage further
by the employment of a vessel of truly symbolic form-a vas mirabile. From
the diagrams in the Greek manuscripts it would appear to be a vessel of thick
glass or earthenware with a round bulb and a short neckl6• In such a form it
persisted throughout the period of Muslim alchemy, finally to be portrayed
symbolically in the later pictorial symbolism illustrating the magnum opus.
LATER PICTORIAL SYMBOLISM
With the transition of alchemy from a dual mystico-practical craft to the
predominantly esoteric quest referred to as Hermetic Philosophy there emerged
in western Europe a wealth of pictorial symbolism. The main concepts of the
earlier alchemists were retained, but their expression wascarried to such extrava-
gant lengths as to make it evident that little practical significance could be
placed on them. Nevertheless, most of the representations were beautifully
executed designs in which the Philosopher's Egg featured prominently, both as
the "creative point" and as the Hermetic Vase.
Among the finest examples of the latter are those which occur in Splendor
Solis, attributed to Salomon Trismosin, a work which "is divided into seven
parts, in which is described the hidden mystery of the old philosophers ... "17.
Of the twenty-two magnificent illustrations in the text, seven depict a pear-
shaped Hermetic Vase; the accompanying description is given in the modern
version which has introductory notes by "J.K."

The source from which this parable derives is evidently the Turba Philoso-

a recent study on the origin ofthe Turba Plessner19has succeeded in identifying
the first nine philosophers mentioned with nine pre-Socratics, whose cosmo-
gonical theories are known from the existing doxographies.
Early in the Turba we learn from Iximidrus (Anaximander) that "fire and
water are enemies between which there isnoconsanguinity, for the fireishot and
dry, but the water is cold and moist. The air, which is warm and moist, joins
them together byits concording medium; between the humidity ofwater and the
heat of fire the air is thus placed to establish peace"20. This, as will be seen,
explains the separation of the Yolk (Fire) from the White (Water).
The assembly approves of this, and Exumdrus (Anaximenes) then discusses
the nature of Air, following which Anaxagoras (same) deals with Pietas and
Ratio (Density and Proportion). Then Pandulfus (Empedocles) is called upon
forhis views: "I signify to posterity", he declares21,"that air isa tenuous matter
of water, and that it is not separated from it. It remains above the dry earth,
to wit, the air hidden in the water, which isunder the earth. If this air did not
exist, the earth would not remain above the humid water". Thus we see how
the Shell (Earth) is separated from the White (Water).
Pandulfus (Empedocles) summarizes the discussion as follows: "The province
ofthe air is, therefore, to fillup and to make separation between diverse things,
that is to say, water and earth, and it is constituted a peace-maker between
hostile things, namely, water and fire, dividing them, lest they destroy one
another"22. The assembly requests an example, whereupon he proceeds to
illustrate the findings by reference to the structure of an egg-in terms almost

these heconceived asseparating out from asubstrate, the Non-Limited (Apeiron).

There are many interesting examples of the Egg as the Hermetic Vase;
the Commentariorum Alchymiae of Libavius27 represents the Vase incorporating
pictorially the stages of the magnum opus. An illustration and description of
this will be found in Professor Read's Prelude to Chemistry28.
Contemporary with Libavius was the German Michael Maier who, more than
any ot~er alchemist, was responsible for the strangest collection of mystical
symbolism, much of which was couched allegorically in the figures of classical
mythology. The present writer has discussed earlier in this Journal29 the
representation of a swordsman taking aim at the Egg with his sword; it is
intended to symbolize the production of the Philosopher's Stone from the Egg
as a result of the application of heat (symbolized by the sword) and recalls a
similar theme in the Tractatus Micreris, wherein an allegory attributed to
Ostanes requires that the Egg be pierced with a fiery sword30
The magnum opus, the alchemical process resulting in the production of the
Philosopher's Stone, lent itself readily to a variety ofsymbolic expressions ofthe
Egg or Vase. The stages of the opus are well known and derive from the series
of colour changes observed in Hellenistic alchemy the initial ingredients when

In the pictorial representations of the magnum opus the proximate materials
were placed in the Egg, or Vase, which was depicted in various ways to sym-
bolize the union of opposites. This union was best expressed in terms of sexual
dualism, opposite principles being depicted often as King (male-sophic sulpnur)
and Queen (female-sophic mercury). Their conjunction might be regarded as
a marriage, in which case the container was a palace "constructed by the hands
of many craftsmen the king and his spouse must be quite naked when they
are joined together that their seed may !lot be spoiled by being mixed with
any foreign matter"33.
But this compulsory confinement would to some writers best be symbolized
as imprisonment; the bride and bridegroom "were shut up for ever in a close
prison, which, however, was as pellucid and transparent as glass", the narrator
informs us in The Golden TractM. Or, as in the Philosophia Reformata of Daniel
Mylius, the conjunction takes place within a glass tomb, the conjoined bodies
becoming an hermaphroditic corpse which blackens and putrefies35•
From the conjunction of opposites was born the hermaphrodite or "rebis"
(two-thing), which, in the diagram from Viatorium Spagyricum38, isseen standing
on the winged globe of chaos (the'Egg), accompanied by the seven planets and
the dragon.
In the portrayal of the magnum opus, alchemical symbolism attained its
richest expression. As we have seen, the Egg played a variety ofroles through-
out the long history of alchemy; from a mere sign, of practical significance, it
soon evolved into a true symbol, psychologically formulated, and appropriately
took its place among the symbols of a quest which, too, had evolved from a
practical craft into a predominantly esoteric quest.
