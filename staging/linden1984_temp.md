Besides their traditional preoccupations with metallic transmutation and salubrious
elixirs, seventeenth-century alchemical authors were keenly interested in setting forth the
religious and ethical implications ofthe art.7 They did so chiefly by devising or reaffirming
intricate systems of correspondence and analogy between chemical processes and
interactions occurring within their alembics and spiritual transformations taking place
within their own hearts and souls. In each case the desired end was purification and
perfection: the attainment ofthe philosopher's stone or the moral and spiritual regeneration
ofa believer whose soul, through God's grace, has been fitted for salvation.
Although itwas particularly prominent inworks ofRenaissance alchemical authors, this
analogical system was, of course, based on the Mediaeval association of the philosopher's
stone with Christ.8 Extending back at least as far as Raymond Lull and the thirteenth
century but also deriving inpart from the Scriptural metaphor ofChrist ascornerstone,9 this
analogy is largely responsible for the infusion ofreligious and ethical content in sixteenth-
and seventeenth-century alchemical writing. It is, as well, the basis for the ingeniously
parabolic and mystical mode ofsuch discourse: what Luther in the epigraph from Table Talk
refers tofondly as alchemy's "allegory and secret signification". Like Christ, the lapis isseen
as the agent ofhealing, the deliverer from sin and baseness, the rewarder ofmerit, the author
of grace and salvation, and the creator of new heavens and a new earth "in that great &
generall refining day"-to borrow a phrase from Thomas Tymme's description of the
Second Coming. 10 The successive stages in the preparation of the philosopher's stone are
likened toChrist's Nativity, Crucifixion and Resurrection; and, by acurious extension ofthe
analogy, the two major events in the world's past and future, the Creation and the Last
Judgment, are often described in terms ofalchemical process. In its most extreme form, this
analogical mode ofthought leads toadirect identification ofChrist, orhis attributes, or God,
with the master alchemist who creates, directs, and will some day end the world and the
course ofhuman history.

On the basis of this evidence it seems probable that seventeenth-century poets who, in
their imagery, combined alchemy with eschatology and millenarianism were introduced to
this trope through its occurrence in a number of popular and contemporary alchemical
treatises. Appearing in the verse ofAnglican and Puritan alike, the imagery which resulted
from this fusion-like the alchemical ideas from which they derive-has a common basis in
analogy: God as chemist; Christ as gold, tincture, mineral, or philosopher's stone; salvation
as transmutation; the sinful self as base metal, clay, or vicious tincture; death as putrefactio;
moral purification as calcinatio; the world's creation as alchemical proceSs cast in a
"Mosaical" format; the LastJudgment as alchemical process patterned on selected biblical
prophecy-all attempt to convey Christian meaning through recourse to alchemy's splendid
obscurities, its "allegory and secret signification". An outgrowth of the Mediaeval and
Renaissance mind's propensity for drawing intricate correspondences between macrocosm
and microcosm, spiritual and physical, the divine and the human, these analogies and the
poetic conceits which proceed from them have special affinity with the thought and style of
works by Paracelsus, Jacob Boehme, and their numerous English adherents. Although the
combining of alchemy with eschatology and millenarianism is common in writings by the
so-called radical Puritans (among whom Paracelsus and Boehme were especially
influential), this practice is not limited to those who, in the words of the Presbyterian
Thomas Hall, were of the "Familistical-Levelling-Magical Temper" .59 The poets who
employed metaphors based upon these models were equally diverse in their theological and
political views.

before "the great and dreadful day of the Lord", Malachi 4 :5-6; the "restorer of all things", according to
