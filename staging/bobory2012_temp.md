This special fascicle has developed from the proceedings of an interna-
tional workshop, convened in Budapest in July 2010 with the aim of
exploring interactions between some of the lesser known sites of al-
chemical practice.' Participants were concerned not only to share new
research—often based on previously unstudied, untranslated or mis-
construed sources—but also to evaluate the marginal status of alchemy
in the historiography of these regions.
Once consigned to the fringes of academia as a mystical or esoteric
practice, or, at best, a primitive precursor to "modern" chemistry,
alchemy has emerged in recent decades as a central theme in early
modern science, medicine and culture. Peeling back earlier preconcep-
tions, scholars have revealed the ubiquity of alchemy in early modern

Following a historical and linguistic trajectory from the Greek-speak-
ing world of Egypt and Byzantium to the Islamic lands, and thence to
Christian Europe, alchemy had made the leap from Arabic into Latin
by the mid twelfth century. Unlike medicine or astrology, alchimia was
thus a relative newcomer to the intellectual life of the Latin West. This
combination of novelty and perceived antiquity presented challenges

alchemy's intellectual credentials. In early modern Europe, the largely
pseudo-epigraphic works of distinguished medieval authorities were
reinforced by sources from farther afield, as seekers after alchemical
wisdom increasingly looked both outwards and back. To its European

The contributors to this fascicle consider examples from regions in
Europe and beyond. Rather than purely local studies, each essay explores
contacts between different zones, asking how alchemical knowledge
was communicated. In doing so, they construe alchemy in a broad
sense, recognizing that the boundaries between disciplinary territories
are often as fiuid as their geographical counterparts: from Johannes
Bureus's quest to reconcile the Scandinavian runic language with Para-
celsian and Neoplatonic philosophy, to the practical melding of
medical, chemical and folk traditions in Johann David Ruland's East
European Dreckapotheke.
In her contribution, Jennifer Rampling sets the scene by examining
the rhetoric of pan-European unity so prevalent in early modern
alchemical treatises, often illustrated by genealogies of alchemists rep-
resenting different national traditions. The theme of the international
conference, or banquet, of philosophers played out not only in edited
collections of authoritative texts, but also in the interchanges of editors
and practitioners at the communication hubs of late sixteenth- and
seventeenth-century Europe. Rampling studies these interactions by
focusing on the reception of works by the famous English alchemist,
George Ripley (d. c. 1490), who claimed to have acquired alchemical

In particular national contexts, early modern scholars were also keen
to establish the credentials of their own heritage within the universal
prisca phibsophia. Hakan Hâkansson traces the efforts of the Swedish
Royal Librarian, Johannes Bureus (1568-1652), to restore Scandinavia
to its rightful place as the wellspring of European learning and culture.
Arguing for the Ancient Goths as genealogical and intellectual forebears
of Greece and Rome, Bureus sought to restore the runic language,
repository of the Goths' ancient wisdom, to its original perfection.
Paradoxically, Bureus found support for his arguments beyond Sweden's
borders, in alchemical and Paracelsian literature, and in the work of
Neoplatonic authors, especially Reuchlin and Agrippa: traditions which
were not widely known or embraced among his Swedish contempo-
raries. Bureus's careful study of Heinrich Khunrath and Gerard Dorn
is evident in his recreation of the runic alphabet, a "Gothic Cabala"
that, for Bureus, constituted a source of Hermetic knowledge rather
than evidence for its reception.
Rafal Prinke's essay shifts attention eastwards, to the vast territories
of the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth, which also extended south
to border Hungary, Croatia and Armenia. Studies of Polish alchemy
have typically centred on the influential figure of Michael Sendivogius,
whose career peaked farther west, in Prague. Yet the same barrier that
defended Christendom from its foes also offered a porous membrane
through which ancient knowledge might be filtered, encouraging
would-be adepts to look east in search of alchemical knowledge—and
suggesting an alternative path to the better known Greek-Arabic-Latin
trajectory. Arguing that "everything connects," Prinke identifies links
between practitioners of alchemy, medicine and astronomy, throughout
the commonwealth and beyond. By moving the focus away from Send-
ivogius and Alexander von Suchten, he reveals the fainter constellations
that appear only in the absence of these distracting "Pole stars."

The final essay, by Vera Keller, returns to the problem of locating the
"centre" of alchemy in late seventeenth-century Europe. For those will-
ing to look beyond the Old World, new territories to the East and West
offered enticing sources of chemical knowledge, which could in turn
be relayed to princely patrons or communicated via the Republic of
Letters. This appeal was exploited by Johann Otto von Hellwig (1654-
1698), who, through his travels in Europe, Africa and the East Indies,
developed his own persona as a purveyor of global alchemical knowl-
edge. To the Dukes of the land-locked Duchy of Saxe-Gotha-Altenburg,
intelligencers like Hellwig constituted a politically valuable informa-
tion-gathering network, which Keller contrasts with the vision of a
universal yet geographically dispersed "Republic of Alchemy," propa-
gated by groups such as the self-styled "Hermetic Alliance." As Hellwig's
clashes with the Alliance and other critics reveal, the "place" of alchemy
remained unsettled even by the close of the seventeenth century—a
body of knowledge both central and marginal to the early modern
world.
