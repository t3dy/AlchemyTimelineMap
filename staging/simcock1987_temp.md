INTRODUCTION
SOMEpapers ofDr. F. Sherwood Taylor (1897-1956), the founding editor ofAmbix, have
recently been deposited in the Museum ofthe History ofScience, Oxford, ofwhich he was
Curator during the 1940s. They provide a valuable record ofa chemist and historian who

SCIENCE, HISTORY, AND FREEDOM
It was in these same years at Queen Mary College that he turned his attention to wider
aspects ofthe history ofscience, and to writing his first expositions ofgeneral science for the
average reader. The World of Science (1936) was his masterpiece in this genre, and for a long
time it was to be his best-selling book.25 It was an encyclopaedic work of synthesis, and
established him, in the tradition of Sciencefor All, as a mediator between science and the
common man. Like some of his other books seeking to convey difficult technical knowledge
to awide readership, one ofits underlying virtues was the trouble taken tosolicit information
and advice from experts. He combined this with a patient and businesslike readiness to
accommodate the requirements ofhis publishers. For the reader, his more obvious quality
was simply "the gift of making science intelligible"-ofbeing able to explain very complex
things in lucid and direct language.26
The history ofscience, which had been seen by its pioneers as offering a bridge between
science and art, and between science and the general public, was an ideal medium for the
exercise of the same talents. The historical introduction to Inorganic and Theoretical Chemistry
had benefited from the advice of Singer, one ofthe masters ofthis field; and later, in A Short
History of Science, Sherwood Taylor acknowledged the help ofJ.R. Partington, his professor
at Queen Mary College.27 The influence of such men clearly encouraged his historical
ventures outside the world of alchemy.

No one who knew Sherwood Taylor was surprised when he turned to the Roman

alchemists, mystical poets, or Christian saints. Since his Galileo studies he had begun to
observe his Catholic friends and to study some of the great Catholic authors. He had been
reading the Confessions ofSt. Augustine when, standing beside a London by-pass one day, he
heard an inner voice say "Why are you wasting your life?" It was, he later learned, the

canonised and made patron saint of scientists.88 Above all he revered St. Albert's pupil St.
Thomas Aquinas, whose erudite theology was part ofa fully worked-out natural philosophy.

courses at Blackfriars in Oxford, and elucidating his attitude toscience in apaper read to the

A CHEMISTRY OF SPIRIT
In 1930 Sherwood Taylor adopted as his emblem a figure he took from the earliest
manuscript of alchemical symbols-the serpent swallowing its own tail, coiled around a
Greek motto and signifying "the unity ofall things" .101 The Greek, in fact, iswell translated
by his later phrase, "the One that is the All". Even though the priorities of his earlier life
were, ofnecessity, rejected as "materialist", his mature views as aCatholic had grown out of
a lifelong interest in mysticism and alchemy, his studies of which played no small part in
urging him along the road to Rome. So a distinct unity characterised his thinking both as a
historian of alchemy and as a religious philosopher. His alchemists had much in common
with his ideal "natural philosopher" (and thus his ideal self): the "excellence ofthings" was
their ultimate faith too, and philosophically they too attempted "the unification ofall nature
in a single scheme" .102
Albeit only "an interim report", his book The Alchemists has endured because of its
honesty and restraint, and because ofits author's empathy with the subject. 103 It displayed
the approach he commended to historians ofscience: to release the historical character from
the context of retrospective value-judgments and "understand and enter into his point of
view" .104 This too was an extension of his mystical approach to the things of nature: "to
enter into them with understanding". So great was his sympathy with alchemy-both as a
spiritual quest, a refining in the human mind, and as a physical quest, a refining of the
material of creation-that he could be said to have pursued a version of it in his own life.
After all, alchemy was not only "essentially religious" but it relied heavily upon the study of
texts! 105
Of course, Sherwood Taylor insisted (contra Jung) upon the practical nature of
alchemy-that the true alchemists wen~ chemical technicians who devoted long hours to
laboratory work, seeking "to perfect everything in its own nature" .106 But they were not
"materialists" because the horizons of their vision extended beyond the laboratory, where
the manipulation of matter was part of a personal journey towards the universal
mystery-as their twentieth-century successors have occasionally felt. Sherwood Taylor
lived through an age when discoveries in chemical physics inspired Rutherford and Soddy to
announce a "new alchemy". Nuclear reaction verged upon proving the transmutation of
base metals into gold possible, and this was one ofthe things which rekindled interest in the
history of the subject.107 Sherwood Taylor kept an open mind anyway about what the
alchemists may have achieved, believing as he did in "preternatural" events, and conscious
