ALCHEMY AND THE SIXTEENTH CENTURY METALLURGISTS
By ERWINF. LANGE*
CHEMISTRYhas often been portrayed as having evolved from alchemy. The
works of Robert Boyle in the last part of the seventeenth century and those
of Joseph Black, Joseph Priestley, Henry Cavendish, and Antoine Lavoisier
in the eighteenth century are usually cited as being significant in turning the
minds of men from alchemy as an art to chemistry as a science. Not always
mentioned by historians of chemistry is the fact that already in the sixteenth
century there existed a considerable body of chemical knowledge relating to
a number of metals and allied compounds. There also existed during that
century a small coterie of metallurgists who denounced the practices and
frauds of the alchemists. Both groups were interested in the metals, the
alchemists being particularly interested in gold and silver. During that
century a considerable number ofpublications concerning the metals appeared.
One group ofimportant works by the sixteenth-century metallurgists is charac-
terized by the clarity of expression, while the writings of the alchemists are
shrouded in obscurity.
The first metallurgist of note was Vannoccio Biringuccio (I48o-I539), who
was born in Sienna and probably died in Rome. His life is closely associated
with the Petrucci family of Sienna. He spent his last year or two in Rome
as head of the Papal foundry and munitions. Little isknown ofhis education,
but during his lifetime he travelled widely in Italy and Germany visiting
many kinds of mining operations and installations. His fame rests on the
publication ofthe first complete metallurgical book based oncareful observation
of practice and experimentation. The book, De la Pirotechnia, commonly
called the Pirotechnia, was published in I540 a year after the author's death
by Curtio Navo under the auspices of the Pope, the Emperor, and the Venetian
Senate. The Pirotechnia appeared three years before De Revolutionibus Orbium
Celestium of Nicholas Copernicus and De Humani Corporis Fabrica of Andreas
Vesalius and 60 years before the publication of De Magnete of William Gilbert,
three well~known classics leading to the study of modern science.
During the years, anumber ofeditions ofthe Pirotechnia appeared in Italian,
French, English, and finally in German. The English editions, however, were
not satisfactory until the translation of Cyril Stanley Smith and Martha Teach
Gnudi appeared in I942. This work was published by the American Institute
of Mining and Metallurgical Engineers. Because of the limited number of
copies printed, the edition was soon very scarce and was reprinted in I959 by
Basic Books in its collectors series in science, thus making the Pirotechnia
available to all English-speaking scholars.

The importance of the Piroteehnia lies in its freedom from superstition and
its straightforward explanation of a variety of chemical and technological
processes. As might be expected for a book of the sixteenth century, the
language is largely clothed in Aristotelian terms and ideas. The author
described the assaying, reducing and alloying of such metals as gold, silver,
copper, lead, tin, iron, mercury and antimony. He discussed the preparation
of mineral acids, saltpetre and gun powders. Technological processes of
distillation, the casting of bells and cannon balls, the drawing out of wires,
and the minting ofcoins are all described. The last chapter ofthe book, written
with asense ofhumour, refers to cupid's fireoflove asbeing the most powerful.
It isalsoin respect to his views regarding alchemy that Biringuccio elevates
himself, for he is one ofthe first ofthe scientific writers to point out the futility
of attempting to convert metals into gold or silver. In the Piroteehnia, Birin-
guccio devoted no less than 12 pages as well as numerous incidental references
to the problems of alchemy. His attitudes towards alchemy, he said, were
based on the reading of the literature, discussions with many practitioners
of the art, and the conducting of many experiments. He stated that he had
been unable to observe any cases where alchemists became rich because of
the practice, even though many had almost unlimited resources with which
to work. He noted that all of the fathers of the art were dead and not one
was able to enjoy even one extra period of youth, let alone several, and none
had been raised from the dead. He referred to the processes of alchemy as
being possible only with God. He noted that anyone interested in acquiring
gold or silver would be much more successful by following the harder road
of mining. In behalf of the alchemists, Biringuccio pointed out that their
efforts were not entirely in vain, but resulted in useful substances such as
medicinals, colours or dyes, perfumes, and numerous other things.
The best known metallurgist of the sixteenth century is Georg Bauer
(1491-1555), latinized Georgius Agricola and commonly referred to as Agricola.
In contrast to Biringuccio, a practical mining engineer, Agricola was a classical
scholar having attended the Universities ofLeipsic, Bologna, Venice, and possibly
Padua. Among his activites, he taught Greek and Latin and lectured at the
University of Leipsic. Later he was town physician at the mining towns of
Joachimsthal and Chemnitz. At Chemnitz he also served four terms as
Burgomaster.
In 1544, Agricola began the publication of a series of books dealing with

was to bring him fame as the most complete book on the subject ofmetallurgy
published in the sixteenth century. It has been ofconsiderable popular interest
ever since the date of publication, and this popularity has somewhat over-
shadowed the other works on metallurgy during the sixteenth century. De re

De re Metalliea was originally published in Latin in 1556, a year after
the author's death, and went through numerous editions in Latin, German
and Italian. In 1912, an English translation by Herbert C. Hoover, who

Like Biringuccio, Agricola alsocommented concerning his views on alchemy.
In the preface to his work, he reviewed the various authors who had written
on the metallic arts and then mentioned those (the alchemists) who referred
to the metals by avariety ofstrange names which did not belong to the metals.
They often invented several names for the same metal. Concerning the possi-
bility of converting 'base metals to gold, Agricola was dubious; for on the one
hand, many authors had written confidently concerning the art, yet there
was no evidence that any had become rich. He noted that alchemists often
referred to the masters as having acquired great wealth through alchemy
and then for some reason the disciples failed to understand or to follow the
precepts of the masters. The disciples were so numerous that whole towns
could have become filled with gold or silver if the processes were successful.
Agricola commented on the vanity of the alchemists in that they had to resort
to the adding of names of famous philosophers such as Plato or Aristotle to
their manuscripts in order to impress simple people with their learning. In
the De re Metalliea, he referred to the frauds of the alchemists, particularly
those who only colour the metals temporarily, and those who would hide a
piece ofpure gold or silver in a crucible with the flux and then pretend to have
made the desired metal from tin or something similar.

Ercker's book might well be considered an important forerunner of books
dealing with chemical quantitative analysis. Not only did Ercker discuss the
procedures of assaying gold, silver, copper and other metals, but he also des-
cribed the methods by which the assayer could make his instruments and
equipment. His descriptions include procedures for making the analytical
balance, sets of weights and a variety of furnaces. To an analytical chemist,
the description of making and adjusting the analytical balance should be of
interest. Ercker pointed out that it was not always feasible to send the balance
to instrument makers in Nuremberg for repairs or adjustments and therefore
he felt the necessity for describing these procedures. The beam he fashioned
~rom a sword and each step in turn is carefully described to the last step in
which the balance isplaced in the traditional glass case. Likewise, he described
the casting and calibration of the set of weights. He recommended that they
be made of silver or brass, preferably silver.
Ercker's style, like that of the other metallurgists, is clear and direct. He
passed offalchemy with but one terse comment to the effect that the cements
used by the philosophers and alchemists to change copper to silver and silver
to gold he would leave for what they were worth. Ercker said he would only
deal in processes and procedures which had been thoroughly tested and could
be relied upon. He did not wish to raise any false hopes.
In view of the fact that practical scholars and engineers in the field of
metallurgy had already discovered the hopelessness of transmutation as early
as the sixteenth century, one might raise the question why alchemy persisted
as long as it did. The answer is complex and certainly not easy. The metal-
lurgical works referred to in this paper are a reflection of Renaissance culture.
The renaissance is often looked upon as a period of intellectual progress and
enlightenment, and yet it was also a period of tremendous acquiescence to
many superstitious beliefs. The practice of astrology as an art like alchemy
persisted alongside of astronomy as a science. The sixteenth century was
also aperiod in which the belief in witches was rampant. Amid such paradoxes
in thought, alchemy as an art and chemistry as an applied science could
each continue to flourish.
