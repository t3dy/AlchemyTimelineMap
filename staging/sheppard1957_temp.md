among the Alexandrians of the 2nd century A.D. as a result of the mutual
interaction of early chemistry and Gnosticism might be less readily acceptable
if we judge by the concluding remarks in a recent contribution to this Journal:
HIt is also obvious that Hellenic chymeutike has no connexion whatever with

and development of early chemistry (chymeutike) in Greece and Hellenized
Egypt; in the main he follows the argument of Stephanides2 against the

If the thesis is true, the conclusion must be much the same as that of the

condition of chymeutike during the first three centuries of the Christian era,
Zacharias states that HTheir books were more or less affected by religious
mysticism and the tendency to popularize the natural phenomena by comparison
with the proceedings ofhuman life"4. HTheirbooks" were the note-books and
records of the earliest Hellenic chymeutes which had managed to survive the
troublesome times leading up to the establishment of the Byzantine empire.
And it was precisely during this period that there arose the interaction between
chemistry and Gnosticism which was responsible for the combination of the
exoteric and esoteric natures which characterized Western alchemy thenceforth.

Late Western alchemy appears to offer two concomitant themes: the trans-
mutation ofbase metals into gold (which is regarded as the ultimate stage in a
process of perfection, or regeneration), and a quasi-religious conception of the
regeneration of the soul, otherwise regarded as a spiritual perfection. It was
these which gave rise to the two extreme schools ofinterpretation which existed
before the first serious research was undertaken by Berthelot and later scholars.
The one school, materialistic, regarded alchemy as a chemical process at times
veiled allegorically in terms of religious experiences, while the other, mystical,
conceived ofit as aspiritual process couched in the language ofchemistry. The
two· views represented broadly the standpoints of early modern scientific
interpreters and of occultists (principally theosophists) respectively.
The only major English work which attempted to decide between the two
views is that of the late A. E. Waite: The Secret Tradition in Alchemy. Not-

mysticism, his essay must be regarded as a failure-at any rate, as far as
alchemy up to the end ofthe Renaissance is concerned. In brief, he concluded
that only after the beginning ofthe 17th century could alchemy be regarded as

By the 2nd century B.C. Greek rationalism was slowly declining12. The
disputes between the many schools of philosophy had led to an attitude of
scepticism on the part ofthe ordinary man, to whom it seemed that there could
be but one true explanation ofthe mechanism ofnature. Discounting reason,
he was left with recourse to one source only-the gods, from whom he sought
the truth through revelation. .
For such an attitude of mind the Egyptian milieu was particularly favour-
able; the conquest of Egypt by the Persian Cambyses in 525 B.C. led to the
infiitration of Magian beliefs, which, together with the Oriental influences
following the campaign of Alexander the Great in 333-331 B.C., readily lent
themselves to the new outlook. What was now sought in science was a know-
ledge of the hidden relationships between natural objects-the forces acting
among them, and between them and the greater universe-a knowledge which
it was felt could come only from the gods themselves.
Astrology provided the solution; established by the Chaldaeans in the 6th

Ofparticular importance for alchemy was the Stoic conception that animals
and plants reproduce their kinds according to fixed types by virtue of the
pneuma, or Uspirit", operating in each kind in a specific fashion. The principle
by which the pneuma worked in an individual was termed the spermatikos logos,
.or "seminal formula"; it implied that reproduction of a given type took place
from a "seed" through the impelling force ofthe logos, which was regarded as a
rational organizing principle founded in the Divine intelligence. Toextend this
to the mineral world, as did the alchemists, was thus asimple step.
For the Stoics pneuma originated as part of God who was Divine Fire
(undoubtedly inspired by the ideas of Herakleitos). When part of the Divine
Fire condensed and coarsened, the Divine nature was lost and it became the
uelements" air, water and earth, out of which the remaining Divine Fire (as

THE RELATIONSHIP BETWEEN GNOSTICISM AND ALCHEMY
The decline in rationalism and the consequent faith in the power of revela-
tory knowledge which immediately preceded the Christian era was most strongly
marked in the field ofreligious thought.
By the 2nd century A.D. the movement had reached its apogee in the form
ofGnosticism and thereafter declined inthe faceofgrowing Christian opposition.
The origin of Gnosticism is shrouded in pbscurity; Leisegang19 and others
regard it as avast syncretism in which the Orient provided the religious material
and the Greeks the philosophy. Its basic tenet was the belief that true know-
ledge (gnosis) could be attained only through revelation afforded by the Divine
God, an ecstatic experience which came Ilthrough the eyes ofthe soul"20.
The problem which had long confronted man was the origin of evil, and in
attempting to solve this the Gnostics were faced with two questions. If evil
were resident in matter, and hence in man himself, how could the creation ofthe
world be attributed to an infinite, spiritual Godwho ,vas perfect? And further-
more, why should such aCreator permit the existence ofsin, and by what means
can man be delivered from it?
Gnostic works and fragments which .remain to-day are, unfortunately,
relatively few, and to see how the various sects attempted to answ~r these
questions we must turn to the writings of Church Fathers such as Irenaeus
(ca. 133-203 A.D.), Hippolytus (ca. 220 A.D.) and Clement of Alexandria (ca.
155~220 A.D.), all ofwhom were well acquainted with the tenets ofthe Gnostic
heresy which they were actively engaged in refuting.

THE EVIDENCE FOR GNOSTIC IDEAS IN LATER ALCHEMY
After the 5th century, Greek alchemical texts showed little advance upon
the works ofZosimos and his contemporaries; they consisted mainly ofcommen-
taries upon, and compilations of, the works of the earlier schools and are con-
tained in the various manuscripts extant. But following the creation ofIslam,
there arose a Muslim school which, beginning in the 8th century, drew its
inspiration in part from the Alexandrian work transmitted by the Nestorians,
and in part from the ancient chemistry ofthe Harranites.
Among the many texts composed or edited by the Arabs are several that
may be related to the Hermetic tradition that we have just mentioned52• Of
these only a very small number have been shown to derive from Greek originals
-indeed, but a few quotations from the Greek and Latin versions of the

traditional literary manner that presents itself in the Arabic Hermetica? Or
how far can the contents ofthese writings prove that they are the true successors
of their classical ancestors?"
At present the question remains open, though Plessner has shown clearly
one example, at least, in which Greek Hermetic tradition, apparently derived
from Babylonian sources, was transmitted to the Arabs to form part of their

long and detailed analyses ofthe Arabic texts, coupled with acareful comparison
with the Greek Hermetica.
