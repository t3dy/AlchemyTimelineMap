INTRODUCTION
THE vitality, complexity and depth of intellectual activity in the, medieval
Islamic world during the eighth, ninth and tenth centuries is well-known.
Within that world, although many scholars were not ethnic Arabs, most were
Muslim, and all wrote in Arabic, which was an international mode of scientific
communication and a vehicle for synthesis of many cultural traditions.
Alchemy was one of the fields in which disciplinary evolution was
stimulated by the confluence of ancient wisdom, the quest for knowledge, and
the emergence of observation and experimentation as investigative
techniques. However, the written indigenous testimony of medieval
alchemists has traditionally thwarted researchers seeking explicit information
regarding chemical discoveries and procedures. Wrapped in the shrouds of
allegory and speculative metaphysics, alchemy belonged to a conceptual
universe that deliberately defied the conscious, linear and logical mind.
Because alchemy was an activity of a dual nature, looking outward at the
natural world and inward at the psyche, the literature has been considered
recalcitrant and often impenetrable to translators, researchers and historians
of science. 1
However, an explicit description of the specifically external features of the
alchenlicallaboratory in the Middle Ages exists in the form ofa chapter from a
tenth-century Arabic manuscript prepared as a general reference tool for
members of the Samanid court by the scribe Abu ~Abdallah Muhammad ibn
Ahmad ibn Yusuf al-Khwarizmi (d.ca.997).
This paper has a dual purpose: to examine how al-Khwarizmi presented his
alchemical data, and to provide an updated and revised English version, with
footnote commentary, of his entry on alchemy, inasmuch as direct access to a
contemporary account ofa past phase ofscience allows the topic to reveal itself
through its own situated cultural logic.

EARLY CHEMISTRY REVEALED
This particular alchemical text can beconsidered aunique and valuable cultural
artifact, forit isan objective description ofmedieval alchemy written by a non-
alchemist. Unlike the abstruse writing ofthe alchemists themselves, therefore, it

inscrutable.

TAXONOMY FEATURES
The organization and features ofthe taxonomy itself are important not only for
what they reveal in terms of definitions, but also because of the fact that
taxonomic systems of the past are currently considered to be 'one of the most
important resources for understanding the interconnections of science and
culture.,13 Therefore, the reproduction of a genuine medieval classification
scheme allows insight into the conceptual hierarchies, theoretical assumptions
and organizing principles applied by the alchemical practitioners totheir natural
environments.
Al-Khwarizmi chose to organize his taxonomy in three divisions: apparatus,
substances (naturally-occurring and artificial), and operations. Within each
division he limited the scope of his inquiry primarily to definitions and
descriptions, although his authorial voice surfaced occasionally when he made
comments about particular terms. He sustained a scrupulous concern with the
task at hand, with virtually no digressions. In doing so,heprovided uswith hard
evidence of early chemical experimentation and research, recreating an itat de
science that has been little understood.

DISCOURSE ORGANIZATION
As mentioned above, al-Khwarizmi divided his observations into three
categories: first description ofthe alchemical apparatus; second, a listing ofthe
substances that are either the basis foror the result ofchemical operations; and
third, the alchemical operations themselves.
In the first section the author listed and described as technical apparatus
fifteen instruments for smelting, distillation, separation, and heating, including
the athanor, which hedescribed asa self-ventilating oven, because itallowed for
interior air circulation. AI-Khwarizmi provided interesting incidental infor-
mation connecting specific apparatuses with other contemporary crafts, e.g.,
goldsmithing, rose~water making and potash-calcination.
In section two, al-Khwarizmi mentioned the mystical correspondences
between metals and planets, and the factthat 'the practitioners ofthiscraft speak
in symbols' using these correspondences. He added a terse comment about the
variability ofsymbol systems, but stated that there was consistency about the use
ofthe sun to represent gold and the moon silver.
He then explained the alchemical distinction between 'bodies' and 'spirits',
which he understood to be based on their reaction to application ofheat. The
bodies include the seven metals: gold, silver, iron, copper, lead, tin and
quicksilver, whereas the spirits are sulphur, arsenic, mercury and ammonia. He
also listed different kinds of salts, ammonia, borax, vitriols, marcasite,
magnesium, zinc and a number ofother naturally occurring substances which
include gems (e.g., lapis lazuli, turquoise), kohl, ratsbane, colophony, arsenic
and the magnet. This was followed by a listing and description ofman-made
substances, including verdigris, cinnabar and fiveothers.
The third and last section isaclassification ofthirteen alchemical operations,
including the standard ones of subl~mation/distillation, solution (including
softening and waxification), coagulation, and calcination, but also others, which
he labelled roasting, oxidation, lixiviation, amalgamation, fixing, and descen-
sion. The operations he did not mention explicitly are separation, mortification
and conjunction (chemical combination). However, his descriptions of

DISCOURSE STRUCTURE AND METALANGUAGE
The author approached the daunting topic of alchemy with directness and
brevity. He did not mention names offamous alchemists, even though he post-
dates the salient figures ofJabir ibn Hayyan (d. ca. 800-812) and al-Razi
(Rhases) (d. ca. 925), but it was generally not his practice to citesources.20 Nor
did he involve himself in the discussion of the historical, theoretical,
philosophical or mystical aspects of the discipline, focusing exclusively on
concrete descriptions of the apparatus, substances and experimental pro-
cedures.21 Considering the breadth ofhis undertaking, it isunderstandable that
he skirted theoretical issues; the omission of names of prominent alchemists,
which contrasts with the normally ad hominem, personality-centered tradition of
medieval Arabic chronicles, (e.g., al-Nadim) illustrates the essentially data-
based nature of the The Kf:Js to the Sciences. It was not intended as a historical
documentation of scientific traditions, but as a reference handbook for
professional scribes who needed immediately accessible descriptions and
definitions ofterms.
AI-Khwarizmi's discourse structure, in keeping with the minimalist conven-
tions of early Arabic discursive practice for technical prose, was terse, cryptic
and highly characterized by parataxis, with sparse use of subordination
structures or coherent juncture articulation.22 These features, as well as the
consistent and formulaic useofanaphora-reiteration ofidentical word sequences
at the beginning ofsuccessive clauses-imbue the textwith aspecifically medieval
type ofrigor and systematic unity, although sequence ofthought isoccasionally
obscure.
Perhaps the most challenging aspect ofthis particular text isthe difficulty in
reconstructing the original denotation ofthe technical terminology, particularly
in the second section, where al-Khwarizmi identified 'substances' using a great
deal of Persian-based nomenclature. This is an area where both Arabic and
Persian sources had to be consulted, in order to determine accurate or at least
plausible English equivalents.23

CONCLUSION
AI-Khwarizmi's text provides an unprecedented snapshot of the medieval
Islamic alchemical laboratory, stripped of its enigmatic allure and potent
psychological undercurrents, but nonetheless a fascinating place where natural
substances could be transmuted through experimentation and the workings
(whether logical or intuitive) of the human mind. In a paradoxical way the
directness ofhisdiscourse delineates evenmore clearly the potent transformation
analogy that alchemy awakened in the psyches of its adepts. Moreover, his
explicit taxonomy of substances, apparatus, and procedures sheds substantial
light on the classification systems, activities and achievements of medieval
Middle Eastern alchemy.
The testimony ofal-Khwarizmi and others even earlier led Stapleton etal. to
declare in 1927:
