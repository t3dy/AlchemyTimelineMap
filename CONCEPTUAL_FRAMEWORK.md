## § 1: Project Vision

ALCHEMYTIMELINEMAP is an authoritative, interactive portal for the history of alchemy and the chemical sciences from Late Antiquity through the early modern period. It combines a 500-event interactive timeline with a geo-pinned map covering Europe, North Africa, and the Middle East, allowing scholars, students, and independent researchers to explore chemical discoveries, alchemical theories, key texts, and pivotal figures.

The portal is **not** esoteric or mystical. It is a rigorous, provenance-aware historical resource structured for academic browsing, built to the historiographical standards of William R. Newman (*Atoms and Alchemy*, Yale), Michela Pereira (medieval alchemy), and the *Dictionary of Gnosis and Western Esotericism* (Hanegraaff, Brill, 2006).

**The model for every entry is the DGWE:** scholarly precision, chronological depth, named sources, and transparent attention to historiographical disputes.

**What this portal is not:** an esoteric or New Age resource; a guide to alchemical practice; a Wikipedia mirror; AI-generated summaries without scholarly grounding. The tone is always: critical, reportorial, provenance-aware. Scholarly disagreements are named explicitly.

## § 2: Three Constituencies

Every page and event must serve all three simultaneously:

**Scholars:** Need precision, historiographical nuance, named sources, complete bibliography, relational links to primary texts and secondary literature. They will tolerate no vagueness.

**Students:** Need accessible prose, definitions on first use of technical terms (distillation, calcination, transmutation), clear chronological frameworks, and visual scaffolding. They will be lost without context.

**Serious independent researchers:** Need depth, the ability to follow a thread from one event to a related figure to a text to a concept. A user should be able to start at a European city's 14th-century alchemical circle, jump to Roger Bacon, follow links to his texts, discover which concepts he engaged with. Every event must link to at least one person, one text, and ideally one concept or location.

## § 3: Historiographical Principles

These principles are the intellectual foundation of the project, not stylistic preferences.

### 3.1 Alchemy Is Not (Just) Chemistry

Modern historiography distinguishes:

- **Alchemy:** A tradition of texts, practices, and ideas about transformation, spiritual perfection, and manipulation of matter through secret operations. Practitioners believed they were working toward transmutation of base metals into gold. Whether this was literally possible is irrelevant to understanding why they pursued it.
- **Chemistry:** The modern discipline that emerged from alchemy but abandoned transmutational claims, grounding itself in empirical experimentation and atomic theory.

Do not collapse these registers. Historical actors called themselves *alchemists*, not chemists. When discussing their work, use their own language. When discussing modern understanding, signal clearly that analytical categories are retrospective.

### 3.2 The Actor/Analyst Distinction

This is the most critical principle for concept definitions and biography writing.

**ACTOR_TERM:** A term or concept that historical practitioners themselves used and would recognize. Examples: *distillatio*, *calcination*, *transmutatio*, *quintessence*. These require material grounding — what was actually happening in the laboratory, what equipment, what observable results.

**ANALYST_TERM:** A retrospective scholarly category that modern historians use to organize the historical record. Historical actors may not have recognized themselves under this rubric. Examples: *Hermeticism*, *alchemy* (as a modern historiographical category), *esotericism*, *occultism*. These require attention to historiographical disputes about whether the category is coherent.

Every concept definition **must explicitly declare** ACTOR_TERM or ANALYST_TERM at the outset.

### 3.3 Medieval Continuity in Arabic and Latin

The tradition of alchemy is **not** an invention of the European Renaissance. It has continuous roots in:
- Late Antique and Byzantine alchemical texts (Zosimos of Panopolis, 3rd century)
- Arabic alchemical tradition (Jabir ibn Hayyan/Geber, 8th–9th century; al-Razi; the *Emerald Tablet*)
- Latin medieval tradition (Gerard of Cremona translations, 12th century; Roger Bacon, 13th century)

The Renaissance "discovery" of classical alchemy was built on this continuous, unbroken medieval transmission. Never treat the medieval period as a gap or dark age.

### 3.4 Chemical Operations Are Real

When an alchemist describes distillation, sublimation, fermentation, or crystallization, they are describing **actual chemical operations** that produce measurable, observable results. These were genuine advances in practical chemistry. What practitioners *believed* about transmutation was separate from what they could *actually do* with matter.

Separate the operational knowledge (valuable) from the transmutational theory (historically contingent). Both deserve serious treatment, but they are different things.

### 3.5 Provenance on Every Claim

Every substantive assertion must trace to a named source. Integrate citations organically: "As William Newman argues in *Atoms and Alchemy* (2006)..." Do not use footnote brackets — write citations into prose.

### 3.6 Material Culture and Embodied Knowledge

Pamela Smith's framework (*The Business of Alchemy*, Princeton, 2004): alchemists learned through embodied, practical engagement with materials and apparatus. This knowledge was partly tacit — learned by doing, not just by reading. Biographies and concept definitions should reflect this when possible:
- What equipment was involved? (furnaces, retorts, crucibles, glass apparatus)
- What dangers? (toxins, explosions, burns, respiratory hazards)
- What sensory experiences? (heat, smell, color changes in distillates)
- How did practitioners learn and transmit this knowledge?

### 3.7 Geographic Specificity

Every event has a specific location (city ideally; region at minimum) with coordinates. Clustering of events in certain regions (Baghdad 9th century, medieval Iberia, Renaissance Italy, early modern Antwerp) reveals the geography of alchemical knowledge. The map is a primary research tool, not decoration.

### 3.8 Transmission and Misreading

Ideas in this tradition traveled through translation, often with significant distortion. Arabic texts were translated into Latin by scholars who may have misunderstood technical terms; pseudonymous works circulated under invented attributions; texts accumulated layers of commentary that altered original meanings. The transmission history of any major text is itself historically significant.

## § 5: Why Transmutation Matters Historically

Modern readers sometimes assume that because transmutation (turning base metals into gold) is impossible, alchemical beliefs about it were irrelevant or merely symbolic. This is a historiographical error.

Transmutation belief structured centuries of scientific inquiry. Practitioners who believed they could achieve transmutation developed genuinely valuable practical chemistry — distillation techniques, acid production, metal-working processes — as instrumental means toward transmutational ends. The belief was wrong; the chemistry was real.

The historian's task is to take both seriously: the belief system as a historical phenomenon that shaped human behavior, and the operational knowledge as a genuine contribution to what would eventually become chemistry. Neither should be collapsed into the other.

This is what Hanegraaff's Actor/Analyst distinction protects against: explaining away historical beliefs by assimilating them to modern frameworks, rather than understanding them in their own terms.
