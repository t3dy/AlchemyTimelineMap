Leyden and Stockholm Papyri

Type: PRIMARY_SOURCE | Language: None | Composed: ca. 290-310 CE

The Leyden Papyrus X (Papyrus Graecus Holmiensis) and the Stockholm Papyrus, both now housed in European libraries, are the earliest surviving documentary evidence of the alchemical impulse to make gold-like and silver-like substances in the ancient world. F. Sherwood Taylor, in Ambix (1937), estimates that both were written towards the end of the third century CE, though they are believed to draw on older sources. The Leyden papyrus was edited by O. Lagercrantz (Papyrus Graecus Holmiensis, Uppsala, 1913) and by Berthelot (Archeologie et Histoire des Sciences, Paris, 1906).

Content. Together the papyri contain some hundreds of recipes for the preparation (or, as Taylor notes, sometimes the falsification) of gold, silver, asemos (a white silver-like metal), precious stones, and dyestuffs. The recipes range from crude dilution of genuine gold with copper or cadmia, through surface treatments that produce a convincing appearance of gold on base-metal objects, to more sophisticated alloys. A representative recipe instructs the practitioner to heat copper with iron sulphate, alum, and salt -- a process that evolves sulphuric and hydrochloric acids and removes base metals from the surface of the gold, leaving a thin layer of pure gold for polishing. The Leyden papyrus uses the symbols of Sun and Moon for gold and silver, demonstrating that the planet-metal symbolism characteristic of later Greek alchemy was already in use by the late third century CE.

Significance. Sherwood Taylor is emphatic that the papyri are not in the direct tradition of alchemy: they are nowhere cited by alchemical authors, they lack the spiritual dimension always present in true alchemy, and they make no claim to genuine transmutation. Rather, they are the work of practical craftsmen trying to make gold-like and silver-like alloys, or to falsify genuine precious metals. One papyrus does, however, mention the alchemist Demokritos, indicating that its authors were acquainted with the true alchemical tradition. The papyri thus stand between practical metallurgical craft and the emergence of philosophical alchemy, providing the clearest surviving window into the Egyptian workshop culture that formed one of the two proximate roots of Western alchemy.
