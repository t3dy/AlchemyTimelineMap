Bosome Book (George Ripley)

Type: PRIMARY_SOURCE | Language: None | Composed: ca. 1471-1490 (discovered 1574)

The Bosome Book (also styled Besome Book, Vade mecum, Ripley's Secret Bosom Book, or Liber secretus Georgii Riplaei) is a Latin commonplace book of alchemical notes, recipes, and jottings attributed to George Ripley (d. ca. 1490) and described in the manuscript tradition as Ripley's personal working notebook — the private record of his alchemical practice. Jennifer M. Rampling, in the 'Catalogue of the Ripley Corpus' (Ambix 57:2, 2010, CRC 3), notes that this text is the core manuscript collection of Ripleian materials and that its discovery marked a key moment in the formation of the English alchemical canon. The same Rampling, in 'John Dee and the Alchemists' (Studies in History and Philosophy of Science 43:3, 2012, pp.498-508), provides evidence for the Bosome Book's role in the continental reception of Ripley's alchemy.

The Bosome Book was discovered on 20 July 1577 by the English alchemist Samuel Norton (1548–1621), grandson of Thomas Norton (author of the Ordinal of Alchemy), who announced the find in his alchemical treatise, the Key of Alchemie, dedicated to Elizabeth I. Norton described the manuscript as 'the secret bosome booke of Riple, whereby the true grounds are discovered' and set about translating it into English as of February 1574 (old style). Although the original Latin manuscript is no longer extant — only a single later copy survives in British Library MS Harley 2411 — Norton's English translations circulated widely in the second half of the sixteenth century. John Dee owned and transcribed excerpts from these English versions, including two texts entitled 'George Ryppley's bosome booke or Vademecum' and the 'Practise by experiment', preserved in Bodleian Library MS Ashmole 1486 (Part V).

The contents of the Bosome Book are composite and include a substantial body of practical sericonian recipes (procedures based on the dissolution of 'sericon' or minium in vinegar), together with theoretical aphorisms attributed to Ripley's main authority Guido de Montanor (the 'Notable rules from Guido') and several subsidiary practical tracts: the Elixir vitae (on the production of the drinkable, medicinal gold or aurum potabile), the Concordantia Guidonis et Raimondi (Concordantia Guidonis et Raimondi), and the Accurtations of Raymond. These materials were reworked into the Viaticum seu varia practica Georgii Riplaei — a collection of practical extracts that appeared in continental Europe in the early seventeenth century and was printed by Ludwig Combach in the Opera Omnia Chemica (Kassel, 1649). The appearance of these Bosome Book extracts in Prague by ca. 1600 — in the manuscripts of Nicolaus Mai and Simon Budek — is connected by Rampling to the activities of Edward Kelley, who took a proportion of Dee's alchemical library to Bohemia in 1583 and actively distributed copies of Ripleian works among his alchemical patrons and colleagues.
