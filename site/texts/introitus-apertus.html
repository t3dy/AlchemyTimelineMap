Introitus Apertus ad Occlusum Regis Palatium (Open Entrance to the Shut Palace of the King)

Type: TREATISE | Language: None | Composed: 1667 (composed 1651–1654)

Introitus Apertus ad Occlusum Regis Palatium (Open Entrance to the Shut Palace of the King) is an alchemical treatise attributed to 'Eirenaeus Philalethes,' published in Amsterdam in 1667 and soon translated into English (by William Cooper, London, 1669), German, and Spanish. William Newman, in his definitive source-critical study in Ambix (1990), proves that the actual author was the New England alchemist George Starkey (1628–1665), demonstrating through comparison of the Introitus with Starkey's Latin letters to Robert Boyle (3 January and 3 February 1651/2, Royal Society Library) that the processes described in the Introitus are identical to those Starkey independently described to Boyle. Newman shows that the Introitus was composed between 1651 and 1654, was circulated in manuscript before its printed publication, and went through at least eight different Latin editions between 1667 and 1749.

Content and Theory. The Introitus is a systematic exposition of the preparation of the Philosopher's Stone through a series of laboratory operations centered on antimony and mercury. J. F. Hertodt von Todtenfeldt, writing in the Ephemerides of the Academia Naturae Curiosorum, interpreted the symbolic language of the Introitus as describing the preparation of 'regulus martis' (metallic antimony reduced by iron) as the starting material for the Stone — a reading Newman confirms through comparison with Starkey's letters to Boyle. The 'fiery dragon' of the Introitus is iron; the 'magnet' is antimony sulphide; the 'twin doves of Diana' are silver. The treatise uses the elaborate symbolic vocabulary of the alchemical tradition to describe what Newman identifies as genuine, traceable chemical procedures.

Influence and Reception. Newman documents the Introitus's extraordinary reception: the Danish scholar Olaus Borrichius reported in 1697 that Philalethes' Introitus was considered 'by the whole family of chemists' to be among 'their classics'. The work had a profound influence on Isaac Newton's alchemical laboratory practice: Newton transcribed and worked from a 'Key' or 'Clavis' that appears in Starkey's English letter to Boyle (spring 1651) and forms the basis of the Introitus's central procedures. Newton incorporated Philalethan processes into his own alchemical notebooks, making the Introitus one of the most significant conduits between the Hartlib circle's experimental alchemy and Newton's later laboratory work.

Modern Scholarship. Newman's 1990 article represents the definitive proof of Starkey's authorship. Newman subsequently published, with Lawrence Principe, the critical study Alchemy Tried in the Fire (2002), which situates Starkey within the history of 'chymistry' in the seventeenth century and analyzes the Introitus's laboratory procedures in the context of contemporary chemical practice. Principe's The Secrets of Alchemy (2013) discusses Starkey and the Philalethan corpus as exemplifying the transition from traditional alchemy to what contemporaries called 'chymistry' in the later seventeenth century.
