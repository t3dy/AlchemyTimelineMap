Confessio Fraternitatis Rosae Crucis

Type: PRIMARY_SOURCE | Language: None | Composed: 1615 CE

Confessio Fraternitatis R.C., ad Eruditos Europae (Confession of the Fraternity of the Rosy Cross, to the Learned of Europe) is the second of the three foundational Rosicrucian documents, published at Kassel in 1615 as a companion piece to the Fama Fraternitatis. Written in Latin (unlike the German Fama) and addressed explicitly to the learned men of Europe, the Confessio elaborates the theological and cosmological framework of the Brotherhood with greater explicitness than its predecessor. It attacks Aristotelian natural philosophy and promises a new philosophy grounded in direct observation of nature — a Paracelsian and reformist program. The Confessio makes its Lutheran Protestant character more explicit than the Fama, attacking the Pope directly and framing the Brotherhood's reform mission within an apocalyptic Protestant eschatology: God is described as revealing His secrets at the dawning of a new age, and the Brethren are presented as instruments of this divine disclosure. Carlos Gilly, in Cimelia Rhodostaurotica (Amsterdam, 1995), documents the Confessio's place within the manuscript tradition of the Rosicrucian documents and its relationship to the reformist networks of the Tübingen circle. The Confessio is more explicit than the Fama on the alchemical dimensions of the Brotherhood's knowledge: it promises that the Brethren possess the Philosophia Hermética and know how to transmute metals, but insists that transmutation is only a minor aspect of their knowledge and not the primary goal. Lawrence Principe, in The Secrets of Alchemy (2013), notes the Confessio's careful positioning of alchemical knowledge as real but subordinate to the spiritual and theological mission of the Brotherhood — a characteristically Paracelsian subordination of material operations to spiritual goals. The authorship of the Confessio, like that of the Fama, is uncertain; Gilly credits the Tübingen circle including Tobias Hess, Christoph Besold, and Johann Valentin Andreae.
