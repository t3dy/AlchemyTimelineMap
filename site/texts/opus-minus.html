Opus Minus

Type: PRIMARY_SOURCE | Language: Latin | Composed: c. 1267

Opus Minus (The Lesser Work), attributed to Roger Bacon], is a companion treatise to his Opus Majus, addressing mathematical and alchemical philosophy. The work applies mathematical principles to alchemical transformation and discusses the role of measurement and proportion in achieving successful operations. While less comprehensive than the Opus Majus, the Opus Minus develops specific themes concerning the mathematical foundations of alchemy and the importance of understanding numerical relationships and proportions in manipulating matter. The work exemplifies Bacon's commitment to integrating mathematics, natural philosophy, and alchemy into a unified intellectual framework.

Content and Theory

Opus Minus addresses the mathematical principles governing alchemical operations: the proportions of materials that must be combined, the ratios governing the application of heat, and the numerical relationships that characterize successful transformation. Bacon argues that understanding these mathematical principles is essential to achieving reliable results in alchemical work. The treatise demonstrates that alchemical operations, properly understood, are subject to mathematical laws and can be systematized through quantification and measurement. This approach represents an important step toward the mathematization of chemistry in later periods.

Composition and Textual Tradition

Roger Bacon] composed Opus Minus as a companion to his Opus Majus, likely in the 13th century. The work survives in a relatively small number of medieval manuscripts, suggesting more limited circulation than the Opus Majus. The work did not become widely influential in the medieval or early modern periods, though it was known to scholars interested in Bacon's complete writings.

Modern Scholarship

Modern scholarship on Opus Minus has recognized its significance for understanding Bacon's mathematical approach to natural philosophy and alchemy. The work demonstrates Bacon's conviction that rigorous quantification and mathematical analysis were essential to understanding nature, an insight that would become increasingly important in subsequent scientific development.
