Opus Majus

Type: TREATISE | Language: None | Composed: c. 1268

The Opus Majus (Greater Work) is Roger Bacon's monumental treatise composed around 1268, in which he systematically defends the necessity of experimental science and alchemical knowledge to understanding nature. The work addresses Pope Clement IV (to whom it was dedicated) and attempts to persuade the papacy that experimental philosophy deserves patronage and scholarly recognition.

The Opus Majus is organized into seven parts: (1) reasons for errors in philosophy, (2) relationship of theology to the sciences, (3) mathematics as foundation, (4) physics and natural philosophy, (5) mathematics applied to natural phenomena, (6) experimental science, and (7) moral philosophy. Sections on alchemy appear principally in the discussion of experimental science, where Bacon argues that experiential knowledge of material transformation is essential to natural philosophy.

Bacon's defense of alchemy in the Opus Majus is sophisticated and multifaceted. He argues that alchemical operations reveal truths about matter's nature inaccessible through theoretical speculation alone. He defends alchemy against charges of superstition by emphasizing that its operations produce repeatable, observable results. He integrates alchemical knowledge into a broader program of experimental verification: testing theoretical propositions against material reality, which Bacon identifies as the hallmark of true natural philosophy.

The Opus Majus also addresses transmutation directly. Bacon's treatment is careful: he neither endorses transmutation as definitely possible nor dismisses it as impossible, but treats it as an open question requiring further investigation. This cautious stance—treating transmutation as a legitimate object of inquiry rather than settled fact or obvious superstition—was influential on later medieval attitudes toward alchemy.

The transmission of the Opus Majus was limited (no printed edition until the 19th century), yet its influence among university scholars was significant. Scholars engaged with copies, and its arguments defending alchemy's legitimacy within natural philosophy circulated in scholastic circles. The work stands as a crucial moment when alchemy gains serious intellectual advocacy from a major medieval authority.
