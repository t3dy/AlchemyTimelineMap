Concordantia Guidonis et Raimondi (Ripley)

Type: TREATISE | Language: None | Composed: ca. 1471-1490

The Concordantia Guidonis et Raimondi (Concordance between Guido and Raymond) is a short Latin prose treatise attributed to George Ripley, Canon of Bridlington (d. ca. 1490), which attempts to reconcile the conflicting positions of his two primary authorities — the pseudo-Lullian Testamentum (attributed to Ramon Lull) and the Scala philosophorum of Guido de Montanor — on the crucial question of the alchemical ferment: whether gold or silver must be added to the philosopher's stone as a 'ferment' to allow it to transmute other metals. Jennifer M. Rampling, in her 'Catalogue of the Ripley Corpus' (Ambix 57:2, 2010), describes this text as 'highly compatible' in alchemical contents with the Medulla Alchimiae (CRC 16, 1476) and notes that one Latin version (Type I) was included in the Bosome Book (CRC 3), suggesting a possible fifteenth-century dating; however, no surviving copy is earlier than the mid-sixteenth century.

The doctrinal problem that the Concordantia addresses was of central importance to Ripley's alchemical philosophy: the pseudo-Lullian texts (including the Testamentum) required that a noble metal (gold or silver) be added as a ferment to complete the stone; but the Scala philosophorum of Guido implied that the stone could be completed without such a ferment, from the philosophical mercury alone. Rampling's analysis in 'Establishing the Canon' (Ambix 55:3, 2008) shows that Ripley ultimately followed the pseudo-Lullian position on fermentation while adopting Guido's twelve-step structural framework for the Compound — and that the Concordantia represents Ripley's theoretical justification for this selective synthesis. The text opens with the statement: 'Forasmuch as Raymond speaking of the ferment of the stone...' and closes with the attribution 'whose theorick this is' followed by the name George Ripley, making it unusual among Ripleian texts in carrying an explicit self-attribution in the manuscript tradition. The Concordantia circulated mainly in an English translation but was later translated back into Latin (Type II) and was printed by Ludwig Combach in the Opera Omnia Chemica (Kassel, 1649); the German translation by Cardilucius appeared in the Magnalia Medico-chymica continuata (1680).
