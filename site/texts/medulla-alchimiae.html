Medulla Alchimiae (Marrow of Alchemy)

Type: TREATISE | Language: None | Composed: 1476 CE

Medulla Alchimiae (Marrow of Alchemy) is a Latin alchemical treatise composed by George Ripley, Canon of Bridlington (ca. 1415–ca. 1490), with a colophon dated 1476, five years after his more famous poem the Compound of Alchymy. Jennifer M. Rampling, in her study published in Ambix (2008), analyzes the Medulla as a key document for understanding Ripley's alchemical sources and methods, particularly his treatment of pseudo-Lullian alchemy. Rampling identifies the text's principal sources as the pseudo-Lullian corpus (above all the Testamentum, the Epistola accurtationis addressed to King Robert of Sicily, and the Quaestiones from the Liber de secretis naturae seu de quinta essentia), and the works of the obscure alchemist Guido de Montanor, alongside references to Morienus, Roger Bacon, Avicenna, and Geber. References to Raymond Lull in the Medulla 'outnumber those to the philosophers Morienus, Guido de Montanor, Roger Bacon, Avicenna and Geber combined.'

The Medulla is distinguished from Ripley's Compound by its more theoretical and Lullian orientation: the Compound's practical 'Twelve Gates' chapter structure, Rampling argues, shows greater influence from Guido de Montanor than from pseudo-Lull, while the Medulla and the Compound's Preface demonstrate the deeper Lullian framework. The contrast between the two texts illustrates Ripley's characteristic practice of drawing simultaneously on multiple alchemical authorities and attempting to reconcile their divergent doctrines. The Medulla was published in William Salmon's English translation in his Medicina Practica (London, 1692) and in Ludwig Combach's edition of Ripley's Opera omnia chemica (Kassel, 1649).

Modern Scholarship. Rampling's 2008 study in Ambix is the first systematic analysis of the Medulla's sources and relationship to Ripley's other authenticated works. For the broader context of Ripley's canonical status and his relationship to the pseudo-Lullian tradition, see also Michela Pereira, The Alchemical Corpus Attributed to Raymond Lull (Warburg Institute, 1989), and Stanton J. Linden, ed., George Ripley's Compound of Alchemy (1591) (Ashgate, 2001).
