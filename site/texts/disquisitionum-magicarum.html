Disquisitionum Magicarum Libri Sex (Del Rio)

Type: TREATISE | Language: None | Composed: 1599 CE

The Disquisitionum Magicarum Libri Sex (Six Books of Investigations into Magic) by the Belgian Jesuit Martin Del Rio (1551–1608), first published at Louvain in 1599, was the most comprehensive and widely read demonological compendium of the Counter-Reformation. The work went through numerous editions throughout the seventeenth century. Martha Baldwin, in Ambix (1993), identifies Del Rio's treatment of alchemy in Book I, chapter 5 as significantly more permissive than his general reputation as a scourge of natural magic would suggest. Del Rio carefully distinguished between legitimate alchemy — which he described as 'the most outstanding child of physiology' in its medicinal and spagyric applications — and transmutatory chrysopoeia, which he neither fully endorsed nor condemned. He reviewed historical claims of successful transmutation by Arnald of Villanova, Raymond Lull, and Bernard Trevisano, and concluded that such claims were 'truthful and worthy of belief.' His argument for alchemy's legality drew on the well-established scholastic principle that 'art imitates nature' — that is, the alchemist's laboratory merely speeds up natural processes of metallic ripening that would otherwise occur over geological time. Del Rio's conclusion was provocative: while the state had the right to prohibit most citizens from undertaking alchemical experiments, '... it is obvious that this art is licit for rich men.' By this reasoning, members of the Society of Jesus — pious, educated, and personally poor through their vow of poverty but institutionally supported — were ideally placed to engage in alchemy legitimately.
