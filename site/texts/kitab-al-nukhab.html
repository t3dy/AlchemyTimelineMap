Kitab al-Nukhab (The Compendium)

Type: TREATISE | Language: None | Composed: late 9th–early 10th century CE

Kitāb al-Nukhab (The Compendium), also widely known as Kitāb al-Baḥth (The Book of the Quest), is a treatise on the science of talismans attributed to Jābir ibn Ḥayyān (fl. ca. 776–815 CE) and belonging to the vast Jabirian Corpus — a large body of Arabic alchemical, magical, and philosophical writing composed over several generations by authors working in the tradition of Jabir's name. Liana Saif, in her study of the Kitab al-Nukhab published in the Journal of Abbasid Studies (2024), introduces this text as a hitherto overlooked work that provides important evidence for the professionalization of talismanry as a science and craft in the Abbasid era. Paul Kraus, the foundational scholar of the Jabirian Corpus, identified cross-references within the Corpus that suggest the Kitab al-Nukhab is among its latest texts, probably composed in the late third/ninth or early fourth/tenth Islamic century.

Content and Theory. The Kitab al-Nukhab contains a long and rich account of the natural and metaphysical foundations of talismanry — the science of making talismans that harness astral and elemental forces for practical effects. It explicitly presents itself as a textbook for teaching talismanry, addresses the proper teacher-student relationship required to transmit this craft, and situates talismanry alongside alchemy and artificial generation as interrelated occult sciences. Saif demonstrates that the text reflects the broader epistemological transformation of the Abbasid period, in which the occult sciences were integrated into the teaching structures and philosophical frameworks of Islamicate learning. The cosmological and metaphysical apparatus invoked — Aristotelian theories of form and matter, Neoplatonic emanationism, astral influence theories derived from Ptolemy and al-Kindi — is shared with the broader Jabirian alchemical project.

Composition and Textual Tradition. The Kitab al-Nukhab is unedited: no critical edition of the Arabic text has been published, and Saif's 2024 article constitutes one of the first sustained scholarly analyses in a Western language. Like much of the Jabirian Corpus, its exact authorship and dating are uncertain; the Corpus is now understood to have been composed by a series of authors working under Jabir's name over several generations, united by a shared program of knowledge production centered on the occult sciences. The Corpus was studied systematically by Paul Kraus in his Jabir ibn Hayyan: Contribution à l'histoire des idées scientifiques dans l'Islam (1942–1943), which remains the standard reference for its scope and contents.

Modern Scholarship. Saif's analysis places the Kitab al-Nukhab within the context of the Abbasid occult sciences as a field that integrated theoretical cosmology, practical craft technique, and pedagogical structure. The text illustrates how alchemy and talismanry were not sharply distinguished disciplines in the Abbasid milieu but rather complementary aspects of a unified science of material and astral transformation. The Jabirian alchemy and magic thus belongs to what Saif and others call the Islamicate occult sciences — a cluster of interrelated practices that shaped the intellectual culture of the ninth and tenth centuries and subsequently influenced both Latin medieval and Byzantine learning through processes of translation and adaptation.
