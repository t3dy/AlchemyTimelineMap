Astronomia Magna (Paracelsus)

Type: PRIMARY_SOURCE | Language: None | Composed: 1537–1538 CE

Astronomia Magna, also known as Philosophia Sagax, is a major philosophical synthesis composed by Paracelsus (Theophrastus Bombastus von Hohenheim, 1493–1541) in 1537–1538, described by Urs Leo Gantenbein, in Ambix (2020), as the work in which Paracelsus 'finally summed up his most important natural-philosophical, theological and magical ideas, leading to a tremendous synthesis that could have served as a scientific model for the sixteenth century.' The work integrates Paracelsus's characteristic four pillars of medicine (philosophia, astronomia, alchimia, virtus) with his distinctive theology of the Radical Reformation, his doctrine of the two lights (the light of nature and the light of the Holy Spirit), and his theories of the macrocosm-microcosm relationship and the tria prima (mercury, sulphur, salt as the three principles of all matter). Gantenbein identifies the Astronomia Magna as incorporating Paracelsus's most explicit engagement with the relationship between his alchemical cosmology and his eschatological theology, particularly his doctrine of the New Man or New Adam who achieves a second birth through the Gospel's grace — a doctrine that parallels the alchemical concept of the ultima materia, the purified end-state of any prima materia. The work was first published in Frankfurt am Main in 1571 by Michael Toxites (Schütz), based on one of Paracelsus's own manuscripts, and was later reprinted in Johannes Huser's collected edition (Basel, 1589–1591).
