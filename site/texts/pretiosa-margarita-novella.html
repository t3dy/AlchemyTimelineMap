Pretiosa Margarita Novella (New Pearl of Great Price)

Type: TREATISE | Language: None | Composed: ca. 1330

Pretiosa Margarita Novella (New Pearl of Great Price) is a Latin alchemical treatise composed by Petrus Bonus of Ferrara, a physician, ca. 1323–1330, and constituting one of the most philosophically sustained defenses of alchemy in the medieval Latin tradition. Written in Pola (modern Pula, Croatia), the treatise takes the form of an extended apologia, engaging systematically with Aristotelian objections to transmutation and arguing that alchemical practice is consistent with legitimate natural philosophy. Its title evokes the parable of the pearl of great price (Matthew 13:45–46), framing alchemy as a divine gift of supreme value concealed from the uninitiated but available to the worthy seeker.

Content and Theory. Petrus Bonus structures his argument around the central question of whether art can produce what nature produces — whether alchemical gold is real gold, or merely a simulacrum. Drawing on Aristotelian categories of form, matter, and natural process, he argues that the alchemist who achieves transmutation does so by reproducing the natural process by which metals are generated within the earth, accelerating and perfecting what nature does slowly and imperfectly. He also invokes the authority of major Arabic alchemical texts (Jabir, Geber, Avicenna) and the Latin tradition (Albertus Magnus, Roger Bacon) to demonstrate that transmutation has been affirmed by the greatest philosophical and scientific authorities. A distinctive feature of the text is Petrus Bonus's treatment of alchemy's relationship to divine revelation: he suggests that the completion of the Great Work may require not only natural knowledge but also a form of illumination granted by God, making alchemy a quasi-religious discipline at its highest level. Lynn Thorndike analyzed this dimension in his History of Magic and Experimental Science (1934), vol. 3, situating it within the broader thirteenth- and fourteenth-century theological anxiety about the limits of natural knowledge.

Composition and Textual Tradition. The Pretiosa Margarita Novella circulated in manuscript through the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries and was printed for the first time in Venice in 1546, in an edition prepared by Janus Lacinius (Giano Lacinio) that included extensive prefatory and commentary material. The 1546 printed edition significantly shaped Renaissance readings of the text and was the primary vehicle for its influence on sixteenth-century alchemical thought. William R. Newman and Lawrence Principe, in Alchemy Tried in the Fire (2002), situate the text within the broader project of scholastic alchemical apologetics that sought to defend transmutation against natural philosophical critics.

Modern Scholarship. The Pretiosa Margarita Novella is recognized as a pivotal text in the intellectual history of medieval alchemy for its systematic philosophical engagement with the discipline's theoretical foundations. It represents a mature phase of Latin alchemical apologetics in which the Aristotelian framework had been thoroughly internalized and turned to the defense of transmutation claims. Newman and Principe use it as evidence for the sophisticated philosophical self-awareness of medieval alchemists, who were not naive practitioners unaware of objections to their art but rather learned authors who engaged with natural philosophy at the highest level available to them.
