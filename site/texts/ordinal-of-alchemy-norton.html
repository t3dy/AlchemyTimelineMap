Ordinal of Alchemy (Thomas Norton)

Type: TREATISE | Language: None | Composed: 1477 CE

The Ordinal of Alchemy (1477) by Thomas Norton of Bristol (d. 1513) is a Middle English poem in seven chapters (treatises) that became one of the two canonical texts of fifteenth-century English alchemy, alongside George Ripley's Compound of Alchemy (1471). Jennifer M. Rampling, in The Experimental Fire: Inventing English Alchemy, 1300–1700 (University of Chicago Press, 2020), describes it as 'the only English work of the fifteenth century to rival Ripley's Compound in posthumous celebrity' and notes that, like Ripley's works, it 'was probably also intended for presentation' to a patron, though 'the vital documentary context is lacking.' The poem's 'Prohemium' opens with Norton's characteristic emphasis on the fragility of alchemical texts and the dangers of copying errors: 'And changing of som oone sillable / May make this boke vnprofitable.' Norton drew on Chaucer's 'Canon's Yeoman's Tale' as an authority on alchemical fraud, citing it to illustrate the use of obscure cover names in alchemical writing — an unusually sophisticated integration of literary and practical knowledge. The Ordinal is notable for its extensive warnings against fraudulent practitioners and recipe collections ('Avoide youre bokis writen of receytis, / For al such receptis be ful of deceytis'), and for its characterization of the author's fictional interlocutor Tonsile as a naive practitioner undone by his reliance on such collections. The text circulated widely in manuscript in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries: manuscripts survive in the British Library and the Bodleian, and at least three manuscript copies were associated with Henry VII. Elias Ashmole (1617–1692) printed the Ordinal in the Theatrum Chemicum Britannicum (London, 1652), which remained the standard text until the critical edition by John Reidy for the Early English Text Society (Oxford, 1975). The Ordinal was frequently cited by Elizabethan and Jacobean English alchemists, who regarded Thomas Norton alongside Ripley as the two pillars of the English alchemical tradition.
