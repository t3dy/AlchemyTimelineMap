Epistle to Edward IV (George Ripley)

Type: PRIMARY_SOURCE | Language: None | Composed: ca. 1471

The Epistle to Edward IV (also known as 'A Compend upon the Philosophers' Stone sent unto King Edward the Fourth') is a fifteenth-century Middle English poem in thirty stanzas, addressed to Edward IV (r. 1461–1483) and attributed to George Ripley, Canon of Bridlington (d. ca. 1490). Jennifer M. Rampling, in the 'Catalogue of the Ripley Corpus' (Ambix 57:2, 2010), notes that although the poem is 'conventionally associated with the Compound of Alchemy (CRC 9) as a dedicatory poem, the two works are not linked in the manuscript record until the second half of the sixteenth century.' The poem employs language similar to that of the 'Wheel' section attached to the Compound, and it refers to the author's studies in Louvain — one of the few autobiographical details in Ripley's works. An early dating is confirmed by the poem's presence in the 'Corthop Group' (MS Sloane 3579 and Ashmole 759, ca. 1475–1500), the earliest surviving manuscripts of the Ripley corpus, although the Corthop copies begin only at stanza 10 ('As the philosopher'), omitting the first nine stanzas addressing the king directly. The full poem opens: 'O Honorable Lord and most victorious Knight ... As the Philosopher in the book of Meteors doth write.'

The Epistle is significant as a record of Ripley's self-presentation to royal patronage: it frames the alchemical work as a gift to the king and rehearses the key doctrines of the Compound in condensed form. The poem was first printed in Ralph Rabbards's edition of the Compound of Alchymy (London, 1591), where it appears as a prefatory piece; it was reprinted by Elias Ashmole in the Theatrum Chemicum Britannicum (London, 1652), and a Latin translation appeared in Combach's Opera Omnia Chemica (Kassel, 1649), with a German version in the Magnalia Medico-chymica continuata (1680). The earliest copies in the Corthop Group are unattributed, a pattern consistent with the manuscript transmission of other Ripleian texts, in which the canon's name was attached to works originally circulated anonymously. The Epistle's reference to 'the book of Meteors' (Aristotle's Meteorologica, the standard Aristotelian foundation for alchemical theory) as authoritative confirms Ripley's grounding in the scholastic natural-philosophical tradition within which English alchemy developed in the fifteenth century.
