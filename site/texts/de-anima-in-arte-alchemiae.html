De Anima in Arte Alchemiae (Pseudo-Avicenna)

Type: COMPILATION | Language: None | Composed: 12th century CE (Latin translation ca. 1226–1235)

De Anima in Arte Alchemiae (On the Soul in the Art of Alchemy) is an Arabic alchemical text composed in twelfth-century Spain, surviving only in a Latin translation made between approximately 1226 and 1235 and falsely attributed to the great physician-philosopher Avicenna (Ibn Sina). Sébastien Moureau, in his study published in Ambix (2009), describes it as 'a significant text in the history of alchemy, as much for the ideas that it introduces into the West as for its influence on medieval thought through authors such as Vincent of Beauvais and Roger Bacon.' The false attribution to Avicenna gave the text enormous prestige in medieval Latin Europe, where Avicenna's medical authority was beyond question; the attribution was contested by Julius Ruska in 1934 and has since been accepted by subsequent scholarship.

Structure and Doctrine. The De Anima is divided into a prologue and ten sections (dictiones), preceded by a 'porta elementorum' (gate of the elements) that serves as the theoretical foundation. The work's alchemical doctrine, according to Moureau, is 'entirely based on operations on the four elements': the alchemist must 'reduce hair, blood or eggs to their elements, and isolate one of their essential properties (warmth, coldness, moisture and dryness), so that he can change the proportion of essential properties of the body he wants to transmute into gold or silver.' This isolated essential property — prepared from hair, blood, or eggs — constitutes the elixir. The theoretical framework draws directly on Jabirian sources for its elixir theory and on al-Razi for its classification of materials, demonstrating the deep roots of the text in the Arabic alchemical tradition even as it was transmitted in a Latin compilation.

Transmission and Influence. The De Anima influenced Vincent of Beauvais, who incorporated it into his encyclopaedic Speculum Naturale, and Roger Bacon, whose alchemical writings engage extensively with its doctrines. The text was first edited from its manuscript tradition by the Sienese humanist Mino Celsi and published at Basel in 1572 by the printer Pietro Perna, in a collection entitled Artis Chemicae Principes, Avicenna atque Geber, alongside the key works of Geber (the Summa Perfectionis, De Inventione Veritatis, and other texts). Two manuscripts give specific dates of translation: one gives 1226, another 1235, both presumably colophon dates from the original translation process. The text's Arabic original is lost; it is known only through the Latin translation.

Modern Scholarship. Julius Ruska established the text's pseudo-Avicennan character in 1934. Subsequent researchers including Georges C. Anawati analyzed its relationship to the Arabic alchemical tradition. Moureau's 2009 article in Ambix provides the most thorough analysis of the text's doctrinal sources, focusing on its debts to both Jabiriyan elixir theory and al-Razi's material classification system.
