Voarchadumia contra Alchimiam

Type: TREATISE | Language: None | Composed: 1530

Voarchadumia contra Alchimiam: Ars distincta ab Archimia et Sophia (Venice, 1530) by the Venetian priest and gold-refiner John Augustine Pantheus (Giovanni Agostino Panteo) is a remarkable document of sixteenth-century efforts to practise and publish alchemy while navigating ecclesiastical and civil prohibitions. Lynn Thorndike, in his survey of alchemy in the first half of the sixteenth century in Ambix (1938), identifies the Voarchadumia as Pantheus's response to the authorities' objections to his earlier Ars Transmutationis Metallicae (Venice, 1518): following the public circulation of his 1518 work, attention was drawn to the existence of a papal decretal and a Venetian decree against alchemists — specifically the Extravagans of Pope John XXII opening 'Spondent quas non exhibent divitias pauperes alchimistae,' the constitution of Emperor Constantine against counterfeiters, and the decree of the Council of Ten against alchemists. Pantheus's solution was to publish a new work in 1530 that claimed to deal not with alchemy at all, but with 'Voarchadumia' — an art he represented as the true wisdom hidden behind alchemy's impostures, 'a kind of cabala of metals' handed down from Tubal Cain through the Chaldeans and Indians. The Voarchadumia was said to be 'an art distinct from alchemy,' its title ostentatiously proclaiming itself to be contra alchimiam — against alchemy.

Thorndike notes the striking paradox: despite this aggressive disclaimer, the Voarchadumia repeats most of the material of Pantheus's 1518 work, now reframed in a Kabbalistic vocabulary that allowed the same alchemical procedures to be presented as belonging to a different and supposedly legitimate art. The volume includes woodcuts of alchemical furnaces and apparatus and a bird's-eye view of Venice and its surroundings; Pantheus again obtained permission to print from the Apostolic Legate and the Council of Ten. Both the Voarchadumia and the 1518 work were reprinted together at Paris in 1550 and again in the second volume of Zetzner's Theatrum Chemicum as published in 1613 and 1659. The Voarchadumia thus represents a documented strategy of terminological and classificatory camouflage that allowed the continued practice and publication of alchemical knowledge within a regulatory environment hostile to the art under its traditional name.
