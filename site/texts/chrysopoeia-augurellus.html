Chrysopoeia (Augurellus)

Type: PRIMARY_SOURCE | Language: None | Composed: 1515 CE

Chrysopoeia (Gold-Making) is a three-book Latin alchemical poem by Johannes Aurelius Augurellus (ca. 1441-1524), published at Venice in 1515 and dedicated to Pope Leo X. It is one of the major humanist alchemical texts of the early sixteenth century, representing the absorption of alchemical themes into Renaissance Latin literary culture. Lynn Thorndike, in Ambix (1938), notes that Augurellus appears to have begun composing the poem around 1500; internal references to the siege of Padua (1509) provide a terminus post quem for that portion of the text. G. Pavanello gave the fullest analysis of the poem in Un maestro del quattrocento (Venice, 1905). A second edition appeared at Basel in 1518, published by Froeben. The dedication to Pope Leo X -- who was known for his patronage of Renaissance arts and letters, including an interest in occult arts -- placed the poem within the courtly alchemical culture of early sixteenth-century Italy. The poem's appearance in 1515 marks one of the earliest major alchemical texts in the vernacular-humanist literary mode, distinct from the manuscript tradition of practical laboratory alchemy. The Chrysopoeia was written in an age when alchemy had not yet been widely printed: Thorndike notes that virtually no alchemical treatises appeared during the incunabula period, and their appearance in the first half of the sixteenth century was gradual.
