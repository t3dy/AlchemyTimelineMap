Mirror of Lights (Speculum Luminum)

Type: TREATISE | Language: None | Composed: ca. 14th–15th century CE

The Mirror of Lights (Speculum luminum) is a late medieval English alchemical treatise produced by an anonymous redactor who took the pseudo-Albertan Semita recta as his primary source and radically reorganized it to make a more systematic, scholastically rigorous argument for alchemy's legitimacy. Peter Grund, in Ambix (2009), establishes that the redactor — most probably working in England in the late fourteenth or early fifteenth century — transformed the Semita recta's chapter-based structure into a strictly hierarchical three-part design: Part 1 (the truth of alchemy, defended through thirteen conclusions, eight objections, and eight solutions); Part 2 (formal description of substances, processes, and precepts); and Part 3 (practical recipes). This organization adopted the same scholastic textual techniques of ordinatio, divisio textus, and compilatio found in university theological and philosophical treatises, particularly John Capreolus's Thomistic Defensiones theologiae. The deployment of these techniques constituted, Grund argues, a strategy to elevate alchemy to the status of a scientia vera et ars firma (a true science and well-established art) by using the very presentational forms associated with the highest academic discourse. The Mirror of Lights survives in some eleven manuscripts, mostly in Middle English of the fifteenth century, with only three copies partially or wholly in Latin; it was never printed. The complete text exists in only one manuscript: Trinity College, Cambridge, MS O. 2. 33. The text is notable for the complexity of its manuscript tradition and for the fact that it circulated almost exclusively in an incomplete form (Parts 2 and 3 only), a pattern Grund interprets as suggesting it was used primarily as a practical alchemical handbook rather than as a theoretical treatise.
