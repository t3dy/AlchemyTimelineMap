Directorium Inquisitorum (Eymerich)

Type: TREATISE | Language: None | Composed: ca. 1376 CE

The Directorium Inquisitorum (Handbook for Inquisitors), composed ca. 1376 by the Aragonese Dominican Nicholas Eymerich (ca. 1316–1399) during his first exile in Avignon, is the most comprehensive medieval manual of inquisitorial procedure and the foundational document in the Catholic Church's legal framework for responding to charges of heresy, sorcery, and diabolical magic. Although Eymerich did not condemn alchemy outright, the Directorium is identified by Neil Tarrant, in research synthesized by Andrew Campbell, Lorenza Gianfrancesco, and Neil Tarrant in Ambix (2018), as the pivotal document in a conceptual shift within the Church: prior to the Directorium, few Christian authorities had associated alchemical practice with sorcery or heresy; after Eymerich's framing, the association between alchemy, demonic invocation, and occult practice began to take institutional form. Eymerich distinguished between diabolical and natural magic, and introduced a framework in which practices previously treated as mere fraud could be prosecuted as diabolical if they involved implicitly or explicitly invoking demonic aid. The Directorium was not printed until 1503 (Barcelona), but it circulated in manuscript; its decisive influence on the Church's stance toward alchemy came when the Spanish canonist Francisco Peña (ca. 1540–1612) published a heavily annotated edition at Rome in 1578, with a commentary that explicitly extended Eymerich's sorcery framework to alchemical practice and enormously expanded the work's circulation and authority among Counter-Reformation inquisitors.
