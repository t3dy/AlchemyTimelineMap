c. 1524 CE Early Modern (15th–18th c.)

Paris

France

c. 1524, Paris: The revolutionary medical and alchemical theories of Paracelsus (Theophrastus Bombastus von Hohenheim) begin to circulate in partial and copied form through French networks, challenging prevailing Galenic medicine and opening new conceptual space for chemical-practice in therapeutic contexts. Paracelsus's assertion that Transmutation and chemical transformation underlie all natural processes resonates with Parisian physicians and natural philosophers seeking alternatives to scholastic Aristotelianism. His influence, though mediated by manuscript copies and secondhand reports, stimulates debate about the relationship between alchemy, medicine, and natural philosophy. The circulation of Paracelsian ideas in Paris marks the beginning of a fundamental reorientation toward chemistry as both theoretical and practical discipline. Paracelsus's impact on Parisian thought demonstrates how radical alchemical innovation in Central Europe transformed European medical and chemical philosophy.
