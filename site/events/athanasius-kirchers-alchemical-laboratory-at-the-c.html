Scholarly Context

Martha Baldwin analyzed Kircher's alchemical activities and Jesuit attitudes to alchemy in 'Alchemy and the Society of Jesus in the Seventeenth Century: Strange Bedfellows?' in Ambix vol. 40 no. 2 (1993) pp.41-64.

Athanasius Kircher SJ (1602–1680), the German Jesuit polymath at the Collegio Romano in Rome, directed or participated in an active group of learned men pursuing alchemical projects in the laboratory of the Collegio Romano in the second half of the seventeenth century. Martha Baldwin, in Ambix (1993), reconstructs this activity primarily from Kircher's published work and from the writings of his collaborator Gaspar Schott SJ (1608–1666). Schott, who worked with Kircher before his reassignment to Germany, 'made frequent references to alchemical projects, both his own and those of his correspondents' in his numerous encyclopedic publications, serving as a major transmitter of Kircherian alchemical findings to a European readership. Kircher published a formal 'diatribe on alchemy' in his two-volume Mundus Subterraneus (Amsterdam, 1665), alongside discussions of vulcanology, ocean currents, hydraulics, geology, metallurgy, and toxicology. Baldwin identifies this embedding of alchemical content within broader encyclopedic works as a characteristic Jesuit strategy, reflecting the institutional ambivalence toward alchemy within the Society: 'Hoping to present their alchemical studies as part of much broader works on natural philosophy suggests that these Jesuits seemed acutely uncomfortable with being known as alchemists.' Francesco Lana Terzi, another Jesuit, similarly embedded recipes for the philosopher's stone and a universal medicament within books that primarily described his inventions for airships, thermometers, and binoculars. The Kircherian laboratory at the Collegio Romano represents the most institutionally significant centre of Jesuit alchemical activity of the seventeenth century, connecting through correspondence networks to alchemical experimenters across Catholic Europe.
