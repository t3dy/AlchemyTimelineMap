Scholarly Context

Georgiana D. Hedesan analyzed the Eisagoge as Van Helmont's first Paracelsian work and identified Petrus Severinus's mediation as its source, in 'The Influence of Louvain Teaching on Jan Baptist Van Helmont's Adoption of Paracelsianism and Alchemy' in Ambix vol. 68 nos. 2-3 (2021) pp.231-246.

In 1607, Jan Baptist van Helmont (1579–1644) composed his Eisagoge in artem medicam à Paracelso restitutam (Introduction to the Medical Art as Restored by Paracelsus), a treatise strongly influenced by the Dane Petrus Severinus (1540–1602), whose Idea medicinae philosophicae (1571) had integrated Paracelsus into a Neoplatonic humanist framework as a restorer of Hippocratic medicine. Georgiana D. Hedesan, in Ambix (2021), identifies the Eisagoge as Van Helmont's earliest explicitly Paracelsian text: it is here that he gives his retrospective account of his conversion vision of 24 September 1599 on the banks of the Schelde, in which the ghost of Paracelsus revealed alchemical secrets to him. The work was never published during Van Helmont's lifetime; it was first printed by Corneille Broeckx in 1853–1854. The Eisagoge marks the beginning of Van Helmont's mature career as a Paracelsian physician and iatrochemist, in which he would attempt to reconcile Paracelsian three-principle chemistry (sulphur, mercury, salt) with Neoplatonic and Augustinian theology. He went on to write three unpublished commentaries on Hippocratic texts (Regimen I, Nutriment, and Breaths) between 1608 and 1634. The trajectory from the Eisagoge to the posthumous Ortus medicinae (1648) represents one of the major intellectual developments of early modern iatromechanical chemistry.
