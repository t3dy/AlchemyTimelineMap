Scholarly Context

Mohamed Yahia Haschmi surveyed this question and summarized the scholarly debate about its authenticity in 'The Beginning of Arab Alchemy' in Ambix vol. 9 no. 3 (1961) pp.155-161, citing al-Nadim, Ruska (Arabische Alchemisten I, 1924), and Holmyard (Endeavour 1955).

Khalid ibn Yazid (d. 704), an Umayyad prince and son of Caliph Yazid I, is described by the tenth-century Arabic bibliographer Ibn al-Nadim in the Fihrist as 'the first Muslim to interest himself in alchemy,' who commissioned from the Egyptian monk Morienus (Marianus) and others the translation of Greek and Coptic alchemical texts into Arabic. Mohamed Yahia Haschmi, surveying the beginnings of Arab alchemy in Ambix (1961), summarizes the tradition: Khalid is said to have had translated a Greek book known as 'Kitab Qaratisul-Hikma' ('The Wisdom of Krates'), and some Arabic alchemical works still circulate under his name. Haschmi notes, however, that Julius Ruska disputed whether any such translation was made in Khalid's lifetime, arguing that these attributions were made at a later date. E. J. Holmyard similarly stated that while Khalid ordered translations and texts attributed to him exist, 'none have been closely studied.' The convergence of later attribution, disputed dating, and the lack of critical manuscript studies makes the historical picture uncertain. What is not disputed is that the tradition of locating the first Arabic engagement with alchemy in the late Umayyad court is very old and persistent: if it reflects a genuine historical event, it would place the beginning of Arabic alchemy significantly earlier than the Jabiriyan corpus, which Kraus dated to the ninth century rather than the eighth.
