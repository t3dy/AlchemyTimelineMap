Scholarly Context

Sébastien Moureau analyzed the De Anima in Arte Alchemiae's doctrinal sources and significance in 'Some Considerations Concerning the Alchemy of the De anima in arte alchemiae of Pseudo-Avicenna' in Ambix vol. 56 no. 1 (2009) pp.49-56.

An anonymous Latin translator rendered the Arabic De Anima in Arte Alchemiae — an alchemical text composed in twelfth-century Spain and falsely attributed to Avicenna — into Latin at some point between 1226 and 1235, the two dates given by colophons in surviving manuscripts. Sébastien Moureau, in Ambix (2009), describes the De Anima as 'a significant text in the history of alchemy, as much for the ideas that it introduces into the West as for its influence on medieval thought through authors such as Vincent of Beauvais and Roger Bacon.' The text's alchemical doctrine draws on two distinct Arabic traditions: its elixir theory is 'directly inspired by Jabirian texts' (the vast corpus attributed to Jabir ibn Hayyan), while its classification of alchemical materials is 'influenced by al-Razi.' The central alchemical operation described involves reducing hair, blood, or eggs to their elemental constituents, isolating an essential property (warmth, coldness, moisture, or dryness), and using this isolated property — the elixir — to alter the proportion of essential properties in a metal being transmuted. The false attribution to Avicenna, one of the most authoritative physicians and philosophers in the medieval curriculum, gave the text enormous prestige in medieval Latin Europe; it circulated widely and influenced the major thirteenth-century encyclopedists and natural philosophers, including Roger Bacon, whose alchemical writings engage directly with its doctrines. The Arabic original is lost; the text is known entirely from the Latin translation and its manuscript tradition.
