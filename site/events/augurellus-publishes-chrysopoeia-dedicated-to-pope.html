Scholarly Context

Lynn Thorndike described the publication of Augurellus's Chrysopoeia and its dedication to Pope Leo X, citing Pavanello's analysis, in Alchemy During the First Half of the Sixteenth Century in Ambix vol. 2 no. 1 (1938) pp.26-38.

In 1515 the Italian humanist Johannes Aurelius Augurellus (ca. 1441-1524) published his three-book Latin alchemical poem Chrysopoeia (Gold-Making) at Venice, dedicating it to Pope Leo X. Lynn Thorndike, in Ambix (1938), identifies the publication as a major early sixteenth-century alchemical text in the humanist literary tradition. Augurellus had apparently begun composing the poem around 1500, and internal references to the siege of Padua (1509) date portions of it. The Chrysopoeia represents the assimilation of alchemical themes into the Renaissance Latin literary culture of northern Italy: it treats the making of gold through the literary conventions of classical epic and didactic poetry, framing the alchemical search as a heroic intellectual quest in the manner of Virgil's Georgics. The dedication to Leo X -- whose Medicean patronage extended to poetry, occult arts, and natural philosophy -- placed the poem at the center of Renaissance court culture. A second edition appeared at Basel in 1518, published by Froeben. The publication of the Chrysopoeia marked one of the few alchemical texts published in the first decade of the sixteenth century, prior to the Paracelsan acceleration of alchemical publication in the second half of the century. Pope Leo X's patronage of both poets and occult arts made him the natural dedicatee for a humanist alchemical poem.
