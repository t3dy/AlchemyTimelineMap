Scholarly Context

Jennifer M. Rampling documents Thomas Charnock's petition to Elizabeth I to practice alchemy in the Tower of London (winter 1565/6), his Booke Dedicated unto the Queenes Maiestie (British Library, MS Lansdowne 703), and the context of Elizabethan alchemical patronage in The Experimental Fire: Inventing English Alchemy, 1300–1700 (Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 2020), pp.230–252. See also Alan Pritchard, 'Thomas Charnock's Book Dedicated to Queen Elizabeth,' Ambix 26:1 (1979) pp.56–73.

In the winter of 1565/6, Thomas Charnock (1524/6–1581) composed his Booke Dedicated unto the Queenes Maiestie (British Library, MS Lansdowne 703), a lengthy petition to Queen Elizabeth I in which he requested royal permission to set up his alchemical practice within the Tower of London — a proposal that would have allowed him to work under royal supervision while offering himself as surety by his physical confinement. As he explained in the petition, it would allow 'the Queenes maiestie and her honourable counsell showlde have [th]e more assuerance for the accomplyshinge off my promes.' Jennifer M. Rampling, in The Experimental Fire: Inventing English Alchemy, 1300–1700 (University of Chicago Press, 2020), notes that the petition was 'probably never made its way to Elizabeth, but was instead filed among Cecil's papers' and that Charnock 'later recorded his dissatisfaction at learning that his proposal had been set aside while Cecil investigated de Lannoy's claims' — a reference to the competing alchemical project of the Flemish alchemist Cornelius de Lannoy, who was simultaneously petitioning Elizabeth's court. The Booke is an important document in the history of Elizabethan alchemical patronage: it shows how English practitioners positioned their alchemical promises within the framework of royal service, offering to manufacture the Philosophers' Stone in exchange for royal protection and support. Alan Pritchard, in his article in Ambix (1979), documented the discovery and contents of the Booke for the first time. The Booke contains an extensive autobiographical account of Charnock's alchemical education and practice, including his youthful dabbling in 'vayne sciencis' such as geomancy and aeromancy before turning to alchemy, his reading of the English alchemical tradition (Norton, Ripley, pseudo-Lullian texts), and his practical laboratory experiments in Somerset.
