Scholarly Context

Betty Jo Dobbs documented Digby's Winchester House imprisonment and glass bottle work in 'Studies in the Natural Philosophy of Sir Kenelm Digby Part II: Digby and Alchemy' in Ambix vol. 20 no. 3 (1973) pp.143-163, citing Longueville and patent records.

In 1642 Parliament imprisoned Sir Kenelm Digby — a Catholic Royalist — at Winchester House, the former episcopal palace of Lancelot Andrewes. Betty Jo Dobbs, in Ambix (1973), reconstructs his activities there from Longueville's Life (1896) and other sources: Digby 'established a laboratory for himself and hired a worker from a nearby glass factory, John Colnett, to be his operator.' The fruit of this collaboration was an improved method for manufacturing glass bottles, stronger than the commercially available glassware of the period, whose defects 'negated many experimental results, even when the glasses did not break at a crucial point.' Digby's invention came to light only in 1662 when Colnett applied for a patent on the process and was opposed by workers who knew the process to have been Digby's; the petitioners against Colnett's patent told the Attorney-General that 'Sir Kenlem first invented glass bottles nearly thirty years since' — placing the activity ca. 1633–5, possibly at Gresham College rather than Winchester House. After being released following petitions by influential friends, Digby went into exile in France (1644), where he lived principally until the Restoration in 1660. The Winchester House period represents the second of Digby's three major phases of chemical and alchemical activity, and the glassware invention demonstrates his empirical orientation toward improving the material conditions of experimental research.
