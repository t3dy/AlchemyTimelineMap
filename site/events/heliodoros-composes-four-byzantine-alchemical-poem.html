Scholarly Context

C. A. Browne identifies four Byzantine alchemical poems (attributed to Heliodoros, Theophrastos, Hierotheos, and Archelaos) as a single composition dating to ca. 715-720 CE based on their dedication to Theodosios III (r. 715-717), their close relationship to Stephanos of Alexandria's nine lectures on the Sacred Art, and the unity of authorship demonstrated by Goldschmidt, in 'Rhetorical and Religious Aspects of Greek Alchemy,' Ambix 2:3-4 (1946) pp.129-137. See also E. Goldschmidt, Heliodori Carmina Quattuor (Religionsgeschichtliche Versuche und Vorarbeiten XIX, 2, Giessen, 1923). F. Sherwood Taylor, 'The Origins of Greek Alchemy,' Ambix 1:1 (1937) pp.30-47, identifies Stephanos and Heliodoros as defining the late Greek alchemical tradition.

Around 715–720 CE, a Byzantine author — conventionally identified as Heliodoros by virtue of the four poems' close relationship to his style — composed four alchemical poems attributed to the personae of Heliodoros, Theophrastos, Hierotheos, and Archelaos. C. A. Browne, in Ambix (1946), demonstrates from close analysis of metre, language, and style that all four poems were composed by a single author, as first argued by Reinesius (1634) and later confirmed by Goldschmidt in his critical edition (Heliodori Carmina Quattuor, Giessen, 1923). The poem attributed to Heliodoros contains a dedicatory title to 'Theodosios the Great King,' which Browne identifies with Theodosios III (r. 715–717), allowing the poems to be dated to approximately that period. The four poems are essentially metrical paraphrases of portions of Stephanos of Alexandria's nine lectures on the Great and Sacred Art (ca. 620–640 CE), and Browne identifies them as the medium through which the rhetorical and religious conventions of Stephanos's alchemical prose — the prayers, invocations, moralizations, and allegorical comparisons — were crystallized into a form that had lasting influence on alchemical writing in Latin, English, French, German, Italian, and other European languages throughout the medieval and early modern periods. The poem of Archelaos, translated by Browne in his article, provides a systematic exposition of the philosophical basis of Greek alchemy as understood in Byzantium ca. 715: the alchemical process is explained in terms of the soul-body-spirit triad, the four elements and their qualities, and the death and resurrection of matter, all framed within an explicitly Christian ascetic and prayerful ethic, ending with a doxology to the Trinity. The manuscript tradition of these poems accompanies those of Stephanos in European library collections; the alchemical poems are also edited in Fabricius, Bibliotheca Graeca VIII (1802) and in Goldschmidt's critical edition.
