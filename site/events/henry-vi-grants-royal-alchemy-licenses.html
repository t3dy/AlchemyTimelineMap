Scholarly Context

D. Geoghegan analyzed this document (MS. Museum 84, Museum of the History of Science, Oxford) in 'A Licence of Henry VI to Practise Alchemy' in Ambix vol. 6 (1957) pp.10-17.

Despite the 1404 statute making alchemical multiplication a felony without royal license, Henry VI granted Letters Patent to John Fauceby, John Kyrkeby, and John Rayny, permitting them to practice alchemy with a non obstante (non-obstruction clause) exempting them from the 1404 prohibition. D. Geoghegan, in Ambix (1957), analyzed the original document — a vellum manuscript comprising the original petition, a draft of the Letters Patent, and a memorandum recording that the king gave the petition to the Lord Chancellor at Shene (Richmond) to be executed — as evidence of both the persistent demand for alchemical practice in fifteenth-century England and the crown's willingness to profit from granting controlled exemptions. Twelve petitioners are named in the document, but only the three named grantees received the license. Geoghegan notes that such licenses were granted on several occasions up to the early sixteenth century, demonstrating that royal patronage of alchemy persisted throughout the Lancastrian and early Tudor periods despite legislative prohibition.
