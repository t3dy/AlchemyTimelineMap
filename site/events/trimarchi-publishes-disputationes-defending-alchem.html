Scholarly Context

Craig Martin analyzed Trimarchi's 1637 Disputationes as the most substantive defense of alchemy within the Renaissance Meteorologica commentary tradition, in 'Alchemy and the Renaissance Commentary Tradition on Meteorologica IV' in Ambix vol. 51 no. 3 (2004) pp.245-262.

In 1637, the Franciscan Girolamo Trimarchi published his Disputationes in libros Aristotelis Meteororum at Genoa (Calenzanus & Farronus), devoting the final section primarily to a defence of alchemy — or what he called ars chemica or archimia — within an Aristotelian natural-philosophical framework. Craig Martin, in Ambix (2004), identifies Trimarchi's work as following the organizational model of the University of Coimbra's Jesuit commentary on Aristotle, but supplementing it with a genuinely substantive discussion of the status of alchemy in scholarly writings. Starting from the Aristotelian axiom that 'art imitates nature,' Trimarchi affirms the possibility of the artificial creation and transmutation of metals; he goes further than most commentators in claiming that the powers of craft are not limited to producing gold, but extend to generating animate beings such as bees, crabs, and scorpions. Trimarchi's 1637 work represents a striking defense of alchemy within the Jesuit-influenced Aristotelian tradition at a moment when many Paduan Aristotelians — including Cesare Cremonini (1550–1631), who had denied that art could reproduce what nature alone accomplishes — had rejected transmutation. Martin argues that Trimarchi's work, along with Niccolo Cabeo's somewhat earlier commentary using alchemical experiments to guide his Aristotle interpretation, demonstrates that by the beginning of the seventeenth century the tradition of commentary on Aristotle's Meteorologica had become deeply intertwined with alchemy and with the emerging experimental natural philosophy.
