Scholarly Context

F. Sherwood Taylor analyzed the Leyden and Stockholm papyri as the earliest practically-oriented alchemical documents, distinct from the philosophical alchemical tradition, in The Origins of Greek Alchemy in Ambix vol. 1 no. 1 (1937) pp.30-47.

The Leyden Papyrus X (Papyrus Graecus Holmiensis) and the Stockholm Papyrus, both written towards the end of the third century CE in Egypt, are the earliest surviving documentary evidence of the systematic attempt to make gold-like and silver-like substances in the ancient world. F. Sherwood Taylor, in Ambix (1937), describes the papyri as almost wholly practical and devoid of mystical content: together they contain several hundred recipes for the preparation (and in some cases the outright falsification) of gold, silver, asemos (a white silver-like metal), precious stones, and dyestuffs. The recipes range from simple dilution of genuine gold with copper, to surface treatments that produce a thin layer of pure gold on a base-metal object (a predecessor of modern gold plating), to amalgam gilding with mercury. One recipe instructs the practitioner to mix copper with iron sulphate, alum, and salt: heating this combination evolves sulphuric and hydrochloric acids which remove base metals from the surface of the gold, leaving a convincing layer for polishing. The papyri's calligraphy and format suggest they were compiled from older sources. Crucially, while one papyrus makes an incidental mention of the alchemist Demokritos -- establishing a connection to the philosophical alchemical tradition -- the papyri themselves contain no mystical content, no concealed symbolism, and no claim to genuine transmutation. Taylor concludes that they cannot be regarded as the source from which true alchemy developed; they are nowhere cited by alchemical authors. Rather, they represent the practical craft tradition of Egyptian goldsmiths and metalworkers alongside which -- but distinct from which -- the philosophical tradition of Greek alchemy developed. The Leyden papyrus uses the symbols of the Sun and Moon for gold and silver, demonstrating that planet-metal symbolism was already practical in Egypt by ca. 300 CE.
