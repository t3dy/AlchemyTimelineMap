Scholarly Context

Edmund Brehm analyzed Roger Bacon's intellectual development and identified the Secretum Secretorum discovery of ca. 1247 as the turning point in his relation to alchemy in 'Roger Bacon's Place in the History of Alchemy' in Ambix vol. 23 no. 1 (1976) pp.53-58.

Around 1247 the Franciscan philosopher Roger Bacon (ca. 1214/1220–ca. 1292), then lecturing at the University of Paris on the traditional topics of scholastic learning, discovered the pseudo-Aristotelian Secretum Secretorum (Secrets of Secrets), and Edmund Brehm, in Ambix (1976), identifies this discovery as the intellectual turning point that 'inspired Bacon's study of medicine, astrology, alchemy, and it was the seed for his vision of a universal science.' Before 1247, Bacon's interests had been firmly scholastic; in his lectures on the pseudo-Aristotelian De Plantis he even maintained 'on philosophical grounds that transmutation of metals per speciem is impossible.' After 1247, his entire intellectual orientation changed: all his later works aimed at integrating all knowledge into a scientia integralis, a universal science, whose roots lay in the Secretum Secretorum's promise of hidden practical wisdom about medicine, astrology, and the governance of nature. The Secretum Secretorum, purporting to be Aristotle's secret instructions to Alexander the Great on governing people, health, and natural knowledge, had been translated from Arabic into Latin by Philip of Tripoli ca. 1220-1243; it became one of the most widely copied texts in medieval Europe. Brehm emphasizes, however, that despite Bacon's turn to alchemical study, his chemical writings are largely 'derivative and superficial' — 'redolent of the lamp rather than of the laboratory' in the words of John Read — and his significance for the history of alchemy lies more in his theoretical framing and his advocacy of experimental method than in any practical chemical contribution.
