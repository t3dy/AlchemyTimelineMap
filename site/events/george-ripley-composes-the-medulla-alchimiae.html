Scholarly Context

Jennifer M. Rampling analyzed the Medulla Alchimiae's sources and relationship to the Compound of Alchymy in 'Establishing the Canon: George Ripley and his Alchemical Sources' in Ambix vol. 55 no. 3 (2008) pp.189-208.

George Ripley composed the Medulla Alchimiae (Marrow of Alchemy) in 1476 — the date given by its colophon — five years after his major poem the Compound of Alchymy. Jennifer M. Rampling, in Ambix (2008), identifies the Medulla as the more theoretically oriented of the two texts and as the document that most clearly displays Ripley's engagement with pseudo-Lullian alchemy: its references to Ramon Lull (as 'Raymond') outnumber combined references to Morienus, Guido de Montanor, Roger Bacon, Avicenna, and Geber. The key Lullian texts that Ripley draws on in the Medulla include the Libro Mercurio and Alphabeto practico (subsections of the Testamentum), the Quaestiones (from the Liber de secretis naturae seu de quinta essentia), the Libro transmutatoriae animae, and the Epistola accurtationis addressed to King Robert of Sicily. Guido de Montanor also appears in the Medulla, though less prominently than in the Compound. The Medulla was published in Latin in Ludwig Combach's edition of Ripley's Opera omnia chemica (Kassel, 1649) and in William Salmon's English translation in his Medicina Practica (London, 1692). Together with the Compound, the Medulla forms the core of the authenticated Ripley canon from which later practitioners — including George Starkey, writing as Eirenaeus Philalethes — constructed new alchemical treatises in the seventeenth century.
