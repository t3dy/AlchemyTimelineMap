Scholarly Context

Urs Leo Gantenbein identified Johannes Huser's 1589-1591 Basel edition as the foundational text event in Paracelsus reception and analyzed its selective exclusion of the theological writings, in 'Cross and Crucible: Alchemy in the Theology of Paracelsus' in Ambix vol. 67 no. 1 (2020) pp.1-12.

Between 1589 and 1591, Johannes Huser (ca. 1545–1600/1601), a physician and editor, published the Bücher und Schrifften des Paracelsus in ten volumes at Basel by the printer Conrad Waldkirch — the first comprehensive collected edition of Paracelsus's medical and natural-philosophical writings and the edition that set the textual canon for three centuries of Paracelsus scholarship. Urs Leo Gantenbein, in Ambix (2020), identifies the Huser edition as the foundational text event for all subsequent Paracelsus reception: before it, Paracelsus's works had circulated in scattered, frequently corrupted printings; after it, scholars had access to a systematically organized corpus. The surgical writings were separately edited by Huser and published in Strasbourg by Lazarus Zetzner (1605). Crucially, Huser deliberately excluded Paracelsus's extensive theological writings from the edition, because 'in the age of hardening religious positions they contained explosive heretical doctrines that would have led to serious problems for any denomination.' This editorial decision ensured that for more than two centuries, Paracelsus was known primarily as a medical and natural-philosophical reformer, with his radical theological dimension — his Eucharistic doctrine, his engagement with the Radical Reformation, and his eschatological framework — remaining largely invisible to mainstream readers. The first selection of Paracelsian theological works only appeared in print with Johannes Staricius's 1618 edition of the Philosophia De Limbo Aeterno. The Huser edition was the text known to alchemists, physicians, and natural philosophers from Francis Bacon to Robert Boyle, and was cited throughout the early modern period as the authoritative Paracelsus.
