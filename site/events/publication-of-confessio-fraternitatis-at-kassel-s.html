Scholarly Context

Carlos Gilly documents the publication of the Confessio Fraternitatis (Kassel, 1615) and its manuscript tradition, in Cimelia Rhodostaurotica: Die Rosenkreuzer im Spiegel der zwischen 1610 und 1660 entstandenen Handschriften und Drucke (Amsterdam: In de Pelikaan, 1995). See also Frances Yates, The Rosicrucian Enlightenment (London: Routledge & Kegan Paul, 1972), pp.42-58, and Lawrence Principe, The Secrets of Alchemy (Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 2013), pp.147-151.

In 1615, the Confessio Fraternitatis R.C., ad Eruditos Europae (Confession of the Fraternity of the Rosy Cross, to the Learned of Europe) was published at Kassel as the companion manifesto to the Fama Fraternitatis (1614). Unlike the German Fama, the Confessio was written in Latin and addressed explicitly to the international learned community of Europe. It elaborated the theological and philosophical framework of the Rosicrucian Brotherhood with greater doctrinal precision: it attacked Aristotelian natural philosophy and promised a new universal science; framed the Brotherhood's mission within a Lutheran Protestant apocalyptic eschatology that denounced the Pope as Antichrist; and positioned the Brotherhood's knowledge of metallic transmutation as real but minor relative to its higher spiritual and theological goals. Carlos Gilly, in Cimelia Rhodostaurotica (Amsterdam, 1995), documents the Confessio's place within the manuscript tradition and its relationship to the Tübingen reformist network. The Confessio is notable for its explicit acknowledgment that the Brethren possess alchemical knowledge — including knowledge of transmutation — while insisting that this is not the Brotherhood's primary purpose. Lawrence Principe, in The Secrets of Alchemy (2013), notes this careful positioning: the Confessio subordinates chemical practice to spiritual goals in a manner characteristic of the Paracelsian tradition it draws upon. The Confessio's attack on Aristotle and its promise of a reformed natural philosophy made it significant for the subsequent debate about natural philosophy in the early seventeenth century; Yates traced its influence on later figures including Francis Bacon, whose New Atlantis (1627) bears structural resemblances to the Rosicrucian utopian vision. The authorship is uncertain; Gilly and others attribute it to the Tübingen circle including Tobias Hess, Christoph Besold, and Johann Valentin Andreae.
