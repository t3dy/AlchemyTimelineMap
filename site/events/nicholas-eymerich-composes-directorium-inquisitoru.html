Scholarly Context

Andrew Campbell, Lorenza Gianfrancesco, and Neil Tarrant synthesized Tarrant's research showing that the Directorium Inquisitorum was the pivotal document in the Church's conceptual shift toward associating alchemy with sorcery, in 'Alchemy and the Mendicant Orders of Late Medieval and Early Modern Europe' in Ambix vol. 65 no. 3 (2018) pp.201-209. See also Zachary A. Matus, Franciscans and the Elixir of Life: Religion and Science in the Later Middle Ages (Philadelphia: University of Pennsylvania Press, 2017), on Franciscan statutes against alchemy.

Around 1376, during his first exile from Aragon in Avignon, the Dominican Inquisitor General Nicholas Eymerich (ca. 1316–1399) completed the Directorium Inquisitorum (Handbook for Inquisitors), the most comprehensive medieval manual of inquisitorial procedure and the foundation of the Catholic Church's legal response to heresy, sorcery, and diabolical magic. Andrew Campbell, Lorenza Gianfrancesco, and Neil Tarrant, in Ambix (2018), synthesizing the research of Tarrant, identify the Directorium as the document that introduced a conceptual shift in the Church's position on alchemy: prior to Eymerich's framing, few Christian authorities had treated alchemical practice as superstitious or heretical. Eymerich himself did not condemn alchemy outright, but in developing his taxonomy of diabolical and natural magic — and in targeting the practices of those who invoked demonic assistance to achieve otherwise unachievable results — he created a framework under which alchemy's more extravagant claims (especially the transmutation of base metals into gold) could be associated with diabolical invocation. Zachary Matus's Franciscans and the Elixir of Life (2017), cited in the same Campbell et al. introduction, notes that the Franciscan Order had already prohibited alchemy in a series of statutes between 1260 and 1337, with the definitions issued between 1295 and 1318 explicitly linking alchemy to occult and sorcerous practices — indicating that concerns about alchemy's relationship to diabolical magic were developing within the mendicant orders in the same period. The Directorium Inquisitorum circulated widely in manuscript but was not printed until 1503 (Barcelona). Its decisive impact on the Church's formal stance toward alchemy came in 1578, when the Spanish canonist Francisco Peña published a heavily annotated new edition at Rome. Eymerich himself was a fierce opponent of the followers of Ramon Lull — a coincidence of some significance, since the pseudo-Lullian alchemical corpus was among the most widely read alchemical texts in late medieval and early modern Europe.
