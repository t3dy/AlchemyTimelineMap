Scholarly Context

Lynn Thorndike analyzed the Ars Transmutationis Metallicae and its context in 'Alchemy During the First Half of the Sixteenth Century' in Ambix vol. 2 no. 1 (1938) pp.26-38.

John Augustine Pantheus (Giovanni Agostino Panteo), a Venetian priest and licensed gold-refiner, published his Ars Transmutationis Metallicae at Venice in December 1518, with the authorization of Pope Leo X and the permission of the Council of Ten. Lynn Thorndike, in Ambix (1938), records the title page as bearing 'Ars transmutationis metallicae cum Leonis X ponti. max. et conci. capi. decemvirorum Venetorum edicto,' and notes that the work culminated in a secondary 'Commentary of the Theory of the Metallic Art of Transmutation' addressed to a noble Pole named William Hyerosky. Pantheus cites a diverse range of alchemical authorities including the Turba Philosophorum, Geber, Alphidius, Avicenna, Rhodianus, and others, some available to him only in manuscript. Thorndike identifies the work as an unusually ambitious attempt to publish alchemical theory in print under explicit papal and civic patronage, obtained at a moment when Pope Leo X's broad Medici patronage of arts and learning extended to alchemy. Pantheus describes himself as 'a priest but a refiner of gold of Venice,' combining ecclesiastical and artisanal identities. Within about a decade, however, objections were raised at the papal or Venetian court about the existence of anti-alchemical prohibitions, leading Pantheus in 1530 to publish a rebranded version of much the same material under the title Voarchadumia, which he claimed was an art entirely distinct from alchemy.
