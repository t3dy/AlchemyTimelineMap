Scholarly Context

Martha Baldwin analyzed Martin Del Rio's treatment of alchemy in Disquisitionum Magicarum Libri Sex, finding that Del Rio carefully distinguished between licit and diabolical alchemy and defended chrysopoeic transmutation as licit in principle, in 'Alchemy and the Society of Jesus in the Seventeenth Century: Strange Bedfellows?' in Ambix vol. 40 no. 2 (1993) pp.41-64.

In 1599, the Belgian Jesuit Martin Del Rio (1551–1608) published his Disquisitionum Magicarum Libri Sex (Six Books of Investigations into Magic) at Louvain, dedicating the work to a broad Counter-Reformation audience of confessors and inquisitors seeking guidance on the demarcation between legitimate and diabolical practice. The work went through numerous editions throughout the seventeenth century and was widely read across Catholic Europe. Martha Baldwin, in Ambix (1993), identifies Del Rio's treatment of alchemy in Book I, chapter 5 as representing the institutional Counter-Reformation position on alchemy in its most carefully reasoned form. Del Rio distinguished sharply between spagyric and medicinal alchemy — which he endorsed as 'the most outstanding child of physiology' — and chrysopoeic or transmutatory alchemy, about which he was more cautious but by no means dismissive. He reviewed historical claims of successful gold-making attributed to Arnald of Villanova, Raymond Lull, and Bernard Trevisano, and — in marked contrast to the spirit of Francisco Peña's 1578 expansion of Eymerich's inquisitorial framework — concluded that such claims were 'truthful and worthy of belief.' Del Rio's central philosophical argument was that alchemical transmutation did not require demonic assistance: a human practitioner could arrive at knowledge of the transmutation process 'either with the devil as teacher or through human industry and zeal,' and only the first route was illicit. He further argued that the fear of demonic involvement was not sufficient reason to prohibit chrysopoeic alchemy outright: 'it is obvious that this art is licit for rich men.' Del Rio was aware that his colleagues in the Society of Jesus — including François Aguilon at Antwerp (1596) and later Athanasius Kircher at the Collegio Romano — were themselves engaged in alchemical experiments, and his defense of alchemy as a legitimate and potentially successful practice within carefully circumscribed conditions provided the theological justification for those activities.
