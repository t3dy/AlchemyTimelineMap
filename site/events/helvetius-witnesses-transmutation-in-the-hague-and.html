Scholarly Context

Mike A. Zuber established the Vitulus Aureus as a major touchstone of late seventeenth-century transmutation debates, identified the retroactive Rosicrucian identification of Helvetius's adept as originating with Vierordt (dismissed by Leibniz), and noted Spinoza's investigation of the event in Epistola XL (March 1667) and Newton's reading of the account, in 'The Duke, the Soldier of Fortune, and a Rosicrucian Legacy: Exploring the Roles of Manuscripts in Early-Modern Alchemy' in Ambix vol. 65 no. 2 (2018) pp.1-21.

Around the turn of 1667, the Dutch physician Johann Friedrich Helvetius (1630–1709), physician-in-ordinary to the Prince of Orange and a self-described former sceptic about alchemical chrysopoeia, published Vitulus Aureus, Quem Mundus Adorat et Orat (The Golden Calf; Amsterdam, 1667) describing his claimed experience of witnessing a metallic transmutation at The Hague. An anonymous adept had visited Helvetius twice at his home; on the second visit, the adept allowed him to take a tiny quantity of a yellow substance resembling sulphur from a locket and use it to project upon molten lead in a crucible. The result, according to Helvetius, was approximately two drams of gold, certified by a silversmith, an assayer, and a goldsmith as genuine. Mike A. Zuber, in Ambix (2018), identifies the publication and the event as having immediately attracted scholarly attention across the Dutch Republic: Baruch Spinoza (1632–1677) investigated the transmutation shortly afterward, reporting to Jarig Jelles in his Epistola XL (March 1667) that he had spoken with a goldsmith who had assayed the gold and found it genuine, but remained agnostic about the cause. Leibniz later received secondhand accounts. Isaac Newton read the Vitulus Aureus and excerpted Helvetius's account in his alchemical notebooks. The episode became a touchstone in late seventeenth-century arguments for and against the reality of transmutation: those who accepted the Vitulus Aureus as genuine evidence were confronted with the testimony of a named, respectable physician and former sceptic; those who rejected it could point to the absence of a named adept, the impossibility of verification, and the suspect motives of the itinerant alchemists who later claimed to identify the anonymous adept as a Rosicrucian named Gottmann. The anonymous adept identified in the Vitulus Aureus was retroactively associated with the Rosicrucian movement from 1701 onward in printed sources — an identification that Zuber traces to the oral circulation by Johann Heinrich Vierordt beginning ca. 1681, dismissed by Leibniz as 'nonsense.'
