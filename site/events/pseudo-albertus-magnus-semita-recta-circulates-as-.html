Scholarly Context

Peter Grund analyzed the Semita recta's pseudepigraphic attribution, manuscript tradition, and scholastic significance, drawing on Pearl Kibre's research establishing the text's non-Albertan authorship, in 'Textual Alchemy: The Transformation of Pseudo-Albertus Magnus's Semita Recta into the Mirror of Lights' in Ambix vol. 56 no. 3 (2009) pp.202-225.

By the last decades of the thirteenth century, the Semita recta (The Straight Path), an alchemical treatise falsely attributed to the Dominican natural philosopher Albertus Magnus (ca. 1200–1280), had entered wide circulation as a foundational medieval alchemical text. Peter Grund, in Ambix (2009), identifies the Semita recta as a 'bestseller' in medieval alchemical literature: its focus on practice over theory, its logical structure, and its almost complete avoidance of metaphorical or obscure language made it uniquely accessible in comparison to most other alchemical treatises. The false attribution to Albertus Magnus — the most celebrated natural scientist of the medieval period, whose authentic works on minerals and chemistry (especially his De mineralibus et rebus metallicis) showed genuine empirical interest in alchemy — was a deliberate strategy for claiming authority: since alchemy was forbidden by secular and ecclesiastical law in many medieval contexts, hiding one's authorship behind a famous cleric also reduced the danger of imprisonment or excommunication. Pearl Kibre has definitively demonstrated that Albertus did not write the Semita recta or the other alchemical texts attributed to him. Nonetheless the text survived in more than one hundred manuscripts from the thirteenth through the seventeenth centuries — a corpus that Kibre began cataloguing in 1942 and that has never been fully mapped. The Semita recta's broad manuscript diffusion, its translation into vernacular languages from the fifteenth century, and its eventual inclusion in the Borgnet critical edition of Albertus's works (Paris, 1898) under 'Opera dubia et spuria' confirm its exceptional importance in the medieval and early modern alchemical canon.
