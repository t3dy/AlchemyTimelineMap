ca. 1583–1597 Early Modern (15th–18th c.)

Prague

Bohemia

ca. 1583–1597, Třeboň and Prague: Edward Kelley, settled first in Třeboň and later at the court of Emperor Rudolf II in Prague, establishes himself as a leading alchemical practitioner in the Holy Roman Empire, performing transmutations — some in the Emperor's presence — and building a network of alchemical clients and patrons in which the works of George Ripley (d. ca. 1490) play a central role. Jennifer M. Rampling, in 'John Dee and the Alchemists' (Studies in History and Philosophy of Science 43:3, 2012), documents Kelley's systematic promotion of Ripleian authority as social and intellectual capital: he compiled manuscript anthologies of Ripleian works as gifts for powerful patrons, including a collection of seven Ripleian texts (Ksiaznica Cieszynska SZ DD.vii.33) inscribed with a dedication to the Silesian councillor Karl von Biberstein (1528–1593) on 2 August 1589; he encouraged the German alchemist Nicolaus Mai to produce a verse translation of Ripley's Compound of Alchymy for presentation to the Emperor; and he served as the conduit through which the Bosome Book and the Viaticum (practical extracts from the Bosome Book) entered Bohemian alchemical circulation. The consequence of Kelley's activities was a significant Bohemian reception of English alchemical texts: nine Ripleian works were transcribed in Bohemia in 1605–06 by the alchemist Simon Thadeas Budek, and the manuscript tradition Kelley had fostered provided primary exemplars for Ludwig Combach's printed edition of Ripley's Opera Omnia Chemica (Kassel, 1649).
