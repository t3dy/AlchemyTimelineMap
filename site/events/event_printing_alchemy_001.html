Scholarly Context

Paula Findlen demonstrated that Venetian merchants and collectors were crucial patrons of alchemical knowledge and natural philosophy in *Possessing Nature* (1994) ch.9.

c. 1470, Venice: Venetian printers and booksellers began turning manuscript alchemy into a more widely available printed market, including works later associated with the pseudo-Geber tradition such as the Summa Perfectionis (Sum of Perfection). William Newman has shown that the Latin Geber corpus mattered because it offered a systematic theory of metallic composition, while Lawrence Principe emphasizes that print changed the scale and audience of chymical literature. Claims about Transmutation circulated through this market as historical arguments made by authors and readers, not as validated transformations. Its historiographical significance is that Venice marks the shift from local manuscript circulation to a durable printed infrastructure for alchemical debate.
