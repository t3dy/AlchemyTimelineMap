Scholarly Context

William Newman documented the Introitus's publication history, reception, and influence on Newton in 'Prophecy and Alchemy: The Origin of Eirenaeus Philalethes' in Ambix vol. 37 no. 3 (1990) pp.97-115.

The Introitus Apertus ad Occlusum Regis Palatium, attributed to 'Eirenaeus Philalethes,' was published in Amsterdam in 1667, twelve years after its composition and two years after its actual author George Starkey died of plague in London during the epidemic of 1665. William Newman, in Ambix (1990), documents the work's extraordinary reception: it went through at least eight Latin editions between 1667 and 1749; William Cooper's English translation, Secrets Reveal'd: or, An Open Entrance to the Shut-Palace of the King (London, 1669), introduced the name 'Eirenaeus Philaletha Cosmopolita' into the title, connecting Philalethes to the Alexander Seton legend; and the Danish scholar Olaus Borrichius reported in 1697 that Philalethes' Introitus was considered 'by the whole family of chemists' to belong among 'their classics.' Newman identifies the Introitus's influence on Isaac Newton as particularly significant: Newton transcribed and used as the basis of his own laboratory practice the 'Key' or 'Clavis' that first appears in Starkey's English letter to Boyle of spring 1651 and forms the structural core of the Introitus's procedures. Newton's alchemical notebooks show intensive engagement with Philalethan processes, making Starkey — writing as Philalethes — one of the most important single influences on Newton's alchemical thought. William Newman and Lawrence Principe, in Alchemy Tried in the Fire (2002), situate the Introitus within the broader transformation of seventeenth-century 'chymistry' and its relationship to the natural philosophy of the Royal Society.
