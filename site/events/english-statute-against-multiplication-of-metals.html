Scholarly Context

D. Geoghegan documented the statute and its consequences in 'A Licence of Henry VI to Practise Alchemy' in Ambix vol. 6 (1957) pp.10-17.

The English Parliament under Henry IV enacted the Statute of 1403–4 (5 Hen. 4), which declared that 'none from henceforth shall use to multiply Gold or Silver, nor use the Craft of Multiplication: And if any the same do, and be thereof attaint, that he incur the Pain of Felony in this Case.' D. Geoghegan, analyzing a contemporary legal document in Ambix (1957), identifies this statute as the defining legal framework for English alchemy for nearly three centuries: it did not abolish alchemical practice but instead made it criminal unless specifically licensed by royal Letters Patent, transforming alchemy into a regulated activity controlled by the crown. Geoghegan notes the statute was not formally repealed until 1689. The legislation reflects a late-medieval anxiety about currency manipulation and monetary fraud, and established the legal basis for subsequent royal alchemy licenses granted under Henry V, Henry VI, and their successors, turning alchemical practice into a matter of royal prerogative in England.
