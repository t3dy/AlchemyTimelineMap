Scholarly Context

William Newman reconstructed Starkey's participation in the Hartlib Circle from Hartlib's Ephemerides and Starkey's letters to Boyle in 'Prophecy and Alchemy: The Origin of Eirenaeus Philalethes' in Ambix vol. 37 no. 3 (1990) pp.97-115.

George Starkey (1628–1665), a New England-born alchemist who had taken bachelor's and master's degrees at Harvard College (B.A. 1646, M.A. ca. 1650), migrated to London in 1650 and was quickly integrated into the scientific and utopian circle surrounding the German expatriate Samuel Hartlib. William Newman, in his study of the origin of Eirenaeus Philalethes in Ambix (1990), reconstructs from Hartlib's Ephemerides and Starkey's letters to Robert Boyle (Royal Society Library) how Starkey impressed Hartlib, Boyle, Benjamin Worsley, John Dury, and other Hartlibians with his knowledge of alchemy and iatrochemistry. In 1651 Starkey was engaged in experimentation with Boyle to produce a 'pauper's medicine,' the ens veneris; Boyle's own 'Memorials Philosophicall' attests to the impression Starkey's practical chemical knowledge made on him. Starkey also introduced his London associates to a mysterious 'adept' from New England — a fictional or real figure about whom he told Boyle in the spring of 1651 — and to a series of procedures involving antimony and mercury that Newman traces, through Starkey's Latin letters to Boyle, to the same processes that would later constitute the core of the Introitus Apertus attributed to Eirenaeus Philalethes. Hartlib's Ephemerides of early 1650/1 record that Starkey 'had told Boyle about a filius Hermetis in N(ew) E(ngland) who had the elixir.' The Hartlib circle's alchemical network thus functioned as the incubator within which Starkey developed both the laboratory practice and the pseudonymous literary persona that would make him the most influential English alchemist of the mid-seventeenth century.
