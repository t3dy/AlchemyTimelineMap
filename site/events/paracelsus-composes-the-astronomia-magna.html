Scholarly Context

Urs Leo Gantenbein analyzed the Astronomia Magna as Paracelsus's synthetic summation of natural-philosophical, theological, and magical ideas, and its use of alchemical imagery in his theology, in 'Cross and Crucible: Alchemy in the Theology of Paracelsus' in Ambix vol. 67 no. 1 (2020) pp.1-12.

Between 1537 and 1538, Paracelsus (Theophrastus Bombastus von Hohenheim, 1493–1541) composed his Astronomia Magna (also known as the Philosophia Sagax), described by Urs Leo Gantenbein in Ambix (2020) as the work in which Paracelsus 'finally summed up his most important natural-philosophical, theological and magical ideas, leading to a tremendous synthesis.' The Astronomia Magna represents the culmination of Paracelsus's theoretical work: it integrates the four pillars of medicine (philosophia, astronomia, alchimia, virtus) with his two-lights theology (the light of nature and the light of the Holy Spirit), his distinctive doctrine of the Radical Reformation, and his cosmology of the tria prima (mercury, sulphur, salt) as the three principles of all things. In the work, Paracelsus synthesizes his theory that 'all things are procured as prima materia, whereupon Vulcanus [i.e. fire] will proceed, making it into ultima materia through the art of alchemy,' with an eschatological theology in which the alchemical purification process serves as an analogy (not a literal model) for the spiritual renovation of humans through the Gospel. Gantenbein notes that Paracelsus draws a sharp line between metallic alchemy (chrysopoeia) and spiritual transformation: only Christ's grace can produce the New Man or New Adam who achieves immortal life. The Astronomia Magna was first published in Frankfurt in 1571 by Michael Toxites and later included in Johannes Huser's Basel collected edition (1589–1591). Dane Thor Daniel's 2003 doctoral dissertation demonstrated the work's importance for understanding Paracelsus's synthetic vision of Bible-based science and the religious roots of the Scientific Revolution.
