Scholarly Context

Erwin F. Lange analyzed the Pirotechnia in 'Alchemy and the Sixteenth Century Metallurgists' in Ambix vol. 13 no. 2 (1966) pp.92-95.

Vannoccio Biringuccio's De la Pirotechnia (Venice, 1540), published posthumously by Curtio Navo under auspices of the Pope, the Holy Roman Emperor, and the Venetian Senate, appeared as the first systematic printed treatise on metallurgy and technical chemistry. Erwin F. Lange, in Ambix (1966), identifies the Pirotechnia as the opening text of a distinctly sixteenth-century tradition of practical, observational metallurgical writing that stood in explicit contrast to the allegorical obscurity of the alchemical literature. Biringuccio described the assaying, smelting, and alloying of gold, silver, copper, lead, tin, iron, mercury, and antimony; the preparation of mineral acids, saltpetre, and gunpowder; and technological processes including distillation, bell-casting, and wire-drawing. The treatment is notably free from mystical or symbolic vocabulary. Lange situates the Pirotechnia alongside subsequent works by Georg Agricola (De Re Metallica, 1556) and Lazarus Ercker (Beschreibung aller fürnemisten mineralischen Ertzt, 1574) as constituting a tradition of sixteenth-century metallurgists who 'denounced the practices and frauds of the alchemists' while developing a substantial body of practical chemical knowledge about the metals. The Pirotechnia appeared in the same year as Vesalius's De Humani Corporis Fabrica, situating it within the broader sixteenth-century empiricization of natural knowledge.
