Scholarly Context

Betty Jo Dobbs documented Digby's backing of the Hartlib universal laboratory scheme from Hartlib's letters to Boyle in 'Studies in the Natural Philosophy of Sir Kenelm Digby Part II: Digby and Alchemy' in Ambix vol. 20 no. 3 (1973) pp.143-163.

During a temporary return to England in 1654, Sir Kenelm Digby met and immediately became an enthusiastic backer of Frederick Clodius, the son-in-law of Samuel Hartlib, who had recently established a laboratory in Hartlib's back-kitchen in London. Betty Jo Dobbs, in Ambix (1973), reconstructs the episode from Hartlib's letters to Robert Boyle: Hartlib reported that Digby had 'protested seriously... that in all his travels and converses with the choicest wits, both in Italy and France, he hath not met so much of theoretical solidity and practical dexterity both together, as he finds in my son [Clodius].' Digby's enthusiastic endorsement reinvigorated Hartlib's circle's pansophic project for a 'general chemical council' and a 'universal laboratory,' which was one of Hartlib's many schemes for state-supported institutional science. The timing was significant: Hartlib had by late 1653 given up hope in George Starkey, writing to Boyle that Starkey was 'altogether degenerated and hath, in a manner, undone himself and his family,' while Clodius was conducting experiments 'days and nights with no small success.' Digby's appearance in this context demonstrates both the network character of mid-century English chymical culture — in which Digby, Boyle, Starkey, Clodius, and Hartlib were all connected through the same correspondence circles — and the persistent aspiration for institutional organization of alchemical-chemical research that would eventually be realized through the Royal Society.
