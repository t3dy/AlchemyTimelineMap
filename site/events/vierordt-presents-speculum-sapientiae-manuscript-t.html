Scholarly Context

Mike A. Zuber reconstructed Vierordt's presentation of the Speculum Sapientiae to Duke Friedrich I from Friedrich's diary and the Gotha State Archive correspondence (GA, EXI), in 'The Duke, the Soldier of Fortune, and a Rosicrucian Legacy: Exploring the Roles of Manuscripts in Early-Modern Alchemy' in Ambix vol. 65 no. 2 (2018) pp.1-21.

On June 19/29 (Old Style/New Style), 1684, Duke Friedrich I of Saxe-Gotha (1646–1691), an accomplished alchemist, spent considerable time at his residential Friedenstein castle in Gotha dealing with an itinerant cavalry captain named Johann Heinrich Vierordt (ca. 1649–1685). Mike A. Zuber, in Ambix (2018), reconstructs this episode from Friedrich's diary (Tagebücher, published 1998–2003) and correspondence preserved in the Gotha State Archive. Vierordt had come to seek support for a military expedition against the Ottomans following the Battle of Vienna (1683), and leveraged his alchemical claims to gain the duke's trust. Two days after arriving, he presented Friedrich with a Neuprozess vor Sonne von Gottmann (new process for the sun [gold] from Gottmann) — a Dutch alchemical treatise attributed to a figure named Johann Gottmann, who identified himself in the text as a 'Brother of the Rosicrucian Order' dying on his deathbed in Leiden in 1672. The text concluded with the Rosicrucian formula: Ex Deo nascimur, in Jesu morimur, per Spiritum Sanctum reviviscimus (Out of God we are born, in Jesus we die, through the Holy Spirit we are reborn). The same text, with the title Speculum Sapientiae (Mirror of Wisdom), later appeared in print in the Quadratum Alchymisticum (Hamburg, 1705). Subsequently, reports of multiple conflicting manuscript copies of the Speculum surfacing in Amsterdam undermined Vierordt's claim to exclusive possession of the text and sealed his fall from the duke's favor. Zuber uses this episode to illuminate three functions of manuscripts in early modern alchemy — provenance, materiality, and exclusivity — demonstrating that manuscript transmission provided an aura of authenticity and rarity that print could not replicate.
