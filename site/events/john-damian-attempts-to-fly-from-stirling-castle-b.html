Scholarly Context

John Read documented John Damian's attempted flight from Stirling Castle battlements and the subsequent account by Bishop Lesley and satirical poem by William Dunbar, in 'Alchemy Under James IV of Scotland,' Ambix 2:2 (1938) pp.60-67, drawing on the Accounts of the Lord High Treasurer of Scotland (vols. 1-9, Edinburgh, 1877-1916), Bishop John Lesley, History of Scotland from 1436 to 1561 (Edinburgh, 1830), and the Poems of William Dunbar (Scottish Text Society, 1893).

Around 1507, John Damian — court alchemist and physician to King James IV of Scotland (r. 1488–1513) and Abbot of Tungland since 1503-4 — equipped himself with a pair of wings fashioned from feathers and attempted to fly from the battlements of Stirling Castle to Paris. The attempt ended immediately: he fell into the dung heap ('midden') at the foot of the castle wall and broke his thigh bone. John Read, in Ambix (1938), records that Damian undauntedly attributed his failure not to the impossibility of human flight but to a material defect: the wings had contained hen feathers, which, partaking of the earthly nature of hens, 'coveted the midden and not the skies.' Had the wings been made exclusively of eagle feathers, he argued, they would have possessed a natural tendency to soar upward toward the element of fire. The episode was celebrated in one of the most famous satirical poems of early modern Scotland: William Dunbar's The Fenyeit Freir of Tungland ('The Feigned Friar of Tungland, how he fell in the mire flying to Turkey-land'), which mocks the entire sequence — Damian's failed alchemy, his improbable appointment as abbot, and his catastrophic flight. Bishop John Lesley, in his History of Scotland from 1436 to 1561 (1570; published Edinburgh, 1830), records the incident straightforwardly: 'This Abbott tuik in hand to fliew ith wingis; and to that effect he caust mak ane pair of wingis of fedderis … bot shortlie he fell to the ground and brak his thee bane.' The Accounts of the Lord High Treasurer of Scotland document that Damian's work in the Stirling laboratory continued through 1508 despite the fracture, with James IV reportedly playing cards and dice with his recovering alchemist at unusually high stakes. Read notes that, unlike the many court alchemists who received execution or imprisonment for failure, Damian was treated throughout with royal generosity, suggesting that James IV regarded him as a sincere experimenter rather than a charlatan.
