Scholarly Context

H. J. Sheppard identified Bolos of Mendes and the Baphika as likely foundational to Greek alchemy, drawing on Wellmann and Festugiere, in Gnosticism and Alchemy in Ambix vol. 6 no. 2 (1957) pp.86-101.

Bolos of Mendes (fl. ca. 200 BCE), an Egyptian natural philosopher and craftsman from the Nile Delta, composed or was associated with a treatise on the tinting of metals entitled Baphika (On Dyeing) and a collection of natural and mystical writings later attributed pseudonymously to Democritus, known as the Physika kai Mystika (Natural and Mystical Matters). H. J. Sheppard, drawing on Max Wellmann and A. J. Festugiere in Ambix (1957), identifies Bolos as probably the first in the field in the development of a theorized practice of transmutation. Wellmann argued that the Baphika may have served as the source of both the technical contents of the papyri and the theory and practice of Pseudo-Demokritos. The Leiden and Stockholm papyri -- compiled ca. 250-350 CE from much earlier records -- are believed to draw from this tradition. From Festugiere's analysis of the Demokritean fragments, Sheppard reconstructs the early alchemical theoretical framework: the Aristotelian doctrine of qualitative change, the Stoic concept of pneuma (spirit) as the animating force of natural processes, and the Stoic spermatikos logos (seminal formula). Together these concepts envision the base metal as one killed (its qualities removed by heat), leaving a dormant seed which, when treated appropriately with warmth and moisture, could regenerate into gold -- the perfection of the mineral world. This theoretical framework, articulated ca. 200 BCE, underlies all subsequent Western alchemy.
