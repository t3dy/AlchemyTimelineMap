Scholarly Context

Raphael Patai analyzed Maria the Jewess as the founding mother of western alchemy in 'Maria the Jewess–Founding Mother of Alchemy' in Ambix vol. 29 no. 3 (1982) pp.177-197, drawing on the writings of Zosimus of Panopolis and the secondary scholarship of Berthelot, Lippmann, and C.G. Jung.

Maria the Jewess (Maria Hebraea, fl. ca. 200 CE) was active as an alchemist in or near Alexandria in the late second or early third century CE, making her the earliest non-mythical practitioner of alchemy attested in the historical record. Raphael Patai, in Ambix (1982), argues for this dating on the basis of Zosimus of Panopolis's treatment of Maria as 'the first' and as one of 'the ancients': since Zosimus himself wrote in the late third and early fourth centuries CE, and treats Maria with 'respect bordering on awe' as a figure of a considerably earlier generation, Patai assigns her floruit 'at least two generations before Zosimus,' that is, no later than the early third century CE. Maria made fundamental contributions to alchemical apparatus: the balneum Mariae (water bath, still known as bain Marie in French); the tribikos (a copper three-pronged still with glass collection flasks); and the kerotakis (a metal plate apparatus for treating metals with alchemical vapors), all of which are described in her treatise Kaminographia (On Apparatus and Furnaces). She described glass vessels as valuable because 'they see without touching' and permit safe manipulation of mercury, which she called 'the deadly poison.' Her most celebrated doctrinal contribution is the axiom that 'one becomes two, two becomes three, and by means of the third the fourth achieves unity,' which C.G. Jung identified as 'one of the central axioms of alchemy' persisting for over seventeen centuries. Patai reconstructs her doctrines from Zosimus's writings, the seventh-century author Christianos (who quotes her as the 'Hebrew prophetess'), and later references in the Turba Philosophorum (ca. 12th century).
