Scholarly Context

Lynn Thorndike analyzed the Voarchadumia and its context in 'Alchemy During the First Half of the Sixteenth Century' in Ambix vol. 2 no. 1 (1938) pp.26-38.

In 1530 John Augustine Pantheus published Voarchadumia contra Alchimiam: Ars distincta ab Archimia et Sophia at Venice — a rebranded version of his 1518 alchemical work that claimed to present not alchemy but 'Voarchadumia,' a distinct art he defined as a 'cabala of metals' handed down from Tubal Cain through the Chaldeans. Lynn Thorndike, in Ambix (1938), identifies this as a direct response to challenges raised at the papal or Venetian court about his 1518 publication in light of existing prohibitions: specifically, the Extravagans of Pope John XXII opening 'Spondent quas non exhibent divitias pauperes alchimistae,' the Emperor Constantine's constitution against counterfeiters, and the Venetian Council of Ten's decree against alchemists. Despite the Voarchadumia's title explicitly announcing itself as against alchemy (contra alchimiam), Thorndike notes that Pantheus 'repeats most of his work of 1518 in the course of the Voarchadumia,' with the same procedures now reframed in Kabbalistic vocabulary. The work includes woodcuts of alchemical furnaces and apparatus, a bird's-eye view of Venice, and again obtained permission to print from the Apostolic Legate and the Council of Ten. Both works were reprinted together at Paris in 1550 and in Zetzner's Theatrum Chemicum (1613, 1659), attesting to their lasting authority in the printed alchemical canon. The episode is a documented case of the regulatory pressures that shaped alchemical publication in the early sixteenth century and the strategies practitioners devised to navigate them.
