Scholarly Context

Tara Nummedal analyzed Anna Zieglerin's alchemical career and the lion's blood recipe in Anna Zieglerin and the Lion's Blood: Alchemy and End Times in Reformation Germany (Philadelphia: University of Pennsylvania Press, 2019), reviewed and summarized by Bruce Janacek in 'The Historic Role of Alchemy in the Holy Roman Empire' in Ambix vol. 67 no. 4 (2020) pp.1-8.

On 1 April 1573, Anna Maria Zieglerin (d. 1575), an alchemical practitioner at the court of Duke Julius of Braunschweig-Wolfenbüttel, submitted to the Duke a twenty-page manuscript of alchemical recipes centered on the production of a substance she called 'the lion's blood' (Löwenblut). Tara Nummedal, in her monograph Anna Zieglerin and the Lion's Blood: Alchemy and End Times in Reformation Germany (University of Pennsylvania Press, 2019), reviewed and analyzed by Bruce Janacek in Ambix (2020), identifies this submission as a pivotal act by which Anna transformed herself from an assistant to an independent alchemical authority in the Duke's eyes: the recipes were 'detailed, concrete, specific in materials, techniques and outcomes,' and earned Duke Julius's confidence in a way that protected her — at least temporarily — from the accusations of sorcery and adultery circulating against her. The lion's blood was conceived within a distinctly Paracelsian framework: it claimed medical powers (curing leprosy, promoting fertility), agricultural powers (causing fruit trees to produce fruit in winter), and alchemical powers (producing precious gemstones and promoting metallic transmutation). Anna also integrated the lion's blood into an eschatological theology rooted in Lutheran apocalypticism and a comparison of her own alchemical purity to the Virgin Mary's spiritual purity: as Christ's blood had redeemed sinners, the lion's blood would 'redeem metals,' and the alchemically purified children that the lion's blood would supposedly produce would usher in the Last Days. Anna's submission of the lion's blood recipe marked the high point of her position at the Wolfenbüttel court; by spring 1574, court politics had turned against her and she was arrested on charges of sorcery, fraud, adultery, and poisoning. She was executed at Wolfenbüttel on 7 February 1575.
