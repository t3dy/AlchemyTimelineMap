Scholarly Context

Mike A. Zuber analyzed the 1705 Hamburg printing of the Speculum Sapientiae in the Quadratum Alchymisticum and its relationship to earlier manuscript versions, in 'The Duke, the Soldier of Fortune, and a Rosicrucian Legacy' in Ambix vol. 65 no. 2 (2018) pp.1-21.

In 1705, the Quadratum Alchymisticum: Das ist: Vier auserlesene rare Tractät vom Stein der Weisen (Alchemical Square: Four Choice, Rare Treatises on the Philosopher's Stone) was published in Hamburg by Christian Liebezeit. Mike A. Zuber, in Ambix (2018), identifies this collection as the first printed appearance of the Speculum Sapientiae (Mirror of Wisdom), attributed to one 'J.G.M.,' identified in other versions of the text as Johann Gottmann, a self-described 'Brother of the Order of the Rosy Cross' who claimed to have written the text while on his deathbed in Leiden on 27 March 1672. The title page featured elaborate claims of rarity and age — one of the advertising strategies deployed by early modern alchemical printers. Two of the four texts in the volume had only recently been put into print; the Speculum Sapientiae itself was accompanied by a 'second part' of uncertain date. The printed edition was anticipated by at least six manuscript copies circulating across European archives (Gotha Forschungsbibliothek, Hannover Leibniz Bibliothek, Hamburg SUB, British Library Sloane MS, and elsewhere), dating from the 1680s onward. Zuber argues that once the text was printed, manuscript copies became worthless to collectors like Benedict Nicolaus Petraeus (fl. 1699–1717) of Hamburg, who noted with exasperation of one copy: 'this has been printed, therefore it counts for nothing.' The episode illustrates a central paradox of early modern alchemy: that adepts' injunctions to secrecy and the alchemical value of exclusive manuscript transmission were in permanent tension with the drive to print and disseminate their knowledge.
