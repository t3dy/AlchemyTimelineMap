Scholarly Context

Betty Jo Dobbs analyzed Evelyn's account of his Paris visit to Digby, drawing on Evelyn's Diary (ed. E. S. de Beer, 1955, III, 48), in 'Studies in the Natural Philosophy of Sir Kenelm Digby Part II: Digby and Alchemy' in Ambix vol. 20 no. 3 (1973) pp.143-163.

In November 1651 the diarist John Evelyn visited Sir Kenelm Digby in Paris and the two men exchanged chemical preparations, as Evelyn recorded in his Diary: 'I visited with Sir Kenholm Digby with whom I had much discourse of chymical matters. I shew'd him a particular way of extracting oyle of [sulphur] & he gave me a certaine powder with which he affirm'd he had fixed [mercury] before the late King, which he advised me to try and digest a litlle better, & gave me a Water, which he said was... raine water of the Autumnal aequinox exceedingly rectified, very volatile, it had a tast of a strong vitriolique, and smelt like aquajortis, he intended it for a disolvant of [gold].' Betty Jo Dobbs, in Ambix (1973), identifies several items of significance in Evelyn's brief account: the 'free exchange of information and chemicals'; the reference to Digby having previously fixed mercury 'before the late King,' Charles I, who had been executed in January 1649; and the transparency of the chemical language, which 'is rational and refers to specific physical substances' rather than allegorical states of soul. Dobbs identifies the powder as possibly the same one described in Digby's posthumous Secrets (1682): 'A Process to fix [mercury of antimony], or the Common [mercury].' The Paris encounter illustrates the character of gentlemen's alchemical culture in the mid-seventeenth century: cooperative, technically specific, and oriented toward laboratory verification.
