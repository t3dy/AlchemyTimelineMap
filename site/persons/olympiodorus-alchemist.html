Olympiodorus the Alchemist

Role: ALCHEMIST | Era: LATE_ANTIQUE

Olympiodorus the Alchemist (fl. ca. 490–520 CE) is one of the later writers in the Graeco-Egyptian alchemical tradition, distinguished from his near-contemporary the Neoplatonist philosopher Olympiodorus of Alexandria with whom he is sometimes confused. The alchemist Olympiodorus composed a substantial commentary on the alchemical writings of Zosimos of Panopolis, and his text is preserved in the major Greek alchemical manuscript collections. In it he addresses questions of theory and practice arising from the Zosimic corpus, while drawing on the contemporary Platonic and Aristotelian philosophical vocabulary of the late antique schools.

F. Sherwood Taylor, in his 1937 survey of the origins of Greek alchemy in Ambix, identifies Olympiodorus as representing the final phase of the Greek alchemical tradition before the Arabic transmission transformed and expanded the discipline. Olympiodorus preserves quotations from older authors that are not found elsewhere, making his commentary an important source for reconstructing the textual history of the Greek alchemical corpus. H. J. Sheppard, in his studies of Gnosticism and alchemy and of the Ouroboros symbol (Ambix, 1957; 1962), observes that authors like Olympiodorus demonstrate the continued interpenetration of alchemical speculation with contemporary Platonic cosmology: the unity of matter, the cyclical transformation of the elements, and the symbolic registers inherited from Zosimos and his predecessors all appear in Olympiodorus's commentary.

The text attributed to Olympiodorus is also notable for its account of practical laboratory operations, including the preparation of the divine water and the tincturing of metals, which suggests that the late antique alchemical tradition retained practical dimensions alongside its increasingly philosophical framing. The Greek manuscripts that preserve Olympiodorus's commentary are among the primary witnesses to the overall Greek alchemical corpus and were central to Berthelot and Ruelle's Collection des anciens alchimistes grecs (1887–1888), the foundational modern edition. Modern scholars continue to use Olympiodorus's text as evidence for the transmission and interpretation of Zosimic alchemy in the centuries immediately before the Arabic translation movement.
