Oswald Croll

Role: PHYSICIAN | Era: EARLY_MODERN

Oswald Croll (ca. 1563–1609) was a German Paracelsian physician and alchemist whose Basilica Chymica (Frankfurt, 1609), published posthumously, became one of the most widely read and influential Paracelsian medical texts of the early seventeenth century. Born in Wetter in Hesse, Croll studied at several German universities before entering the service of Christian I of Anhalt-Bernburg, in whose court he composed the Basilica Chymica during the final years of his life. The work combines practical pharmaceutical chemistry with an extended philosophical preface — the Admonitory Preface — that articulates the theoretical principles of Paracelsian medicine and its relationship to natural philosophy, theology, and the macrocosm-microcosm analogy.

Croll's significance in the history of Paracelsian alchemy rests on the Basilica Chymica's role in systematizing and disseminating Paracelsian medical chemistry to the learned European public. The work appeared in numerous Latin and vernacular editions throughout the seventeenth century and was translated into English in 1657 under the title Basilica Chymica, or Royal and Practical Chymistry. Lawrence Principe, in The Secrets of Alchemy (2013), notes that the Basilica Chymica exemplifies the transformation of alchemy into what contemporaries called chymistry or iatrochemistry: a discipline oriented toward the preparation of medical remedies from mineral and metallic substances using distillation, calcination, and related operations, justified by Paracelsian theories of the Three Principles and the macrocosm-microcosm correspondence. William R. Newman and Lawrence Principe, in Alchemy Tried in the Fire (2002), analyze how Paracelsian texts like Croll's contributed to the redefinition of the alchemical enterprise in the late sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries, preparing the way for the emergence of what would later be called chemistry as a distinct discipline.

Croll's Paracelsian credentials include an explicit defense of Paracelsus as the reformer of medicine and natural philosophy, a theme that runs through the Admonitory Preface and situates the Basilica Chymica within the ongoing late sixteenth-century controversy over Paracelsian versus Galenic medicine. He draws extensively on Paracelsian cosmology, including the doctrine of the Archeus (the vital principle governing physiological and alchemical processes) and the theory of signatures (the doctrine that the external form of a natural substance signals its medicinal virtues). His circle of contacts included Rudolf II's Prague court, where Paracelsian alchemy flourished in the late sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries, as documented by Bruce Moran in The Alchemical World of the German Court (1991).
