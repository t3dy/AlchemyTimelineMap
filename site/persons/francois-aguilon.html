François Aguilon SJ

Role: SCHOLAR | Era: EARLY_MODERN

François Aguilon SJ (1566–1617), also known as Franciscus Aguilonius, was a Belgian Jesuit mathematician and natural philosopher best known for his major optical treatise Opticorum Libri Sex (Antwerp, 1613), which included illustrations by Peter Paul Rubens. Martha Baldwin, in her study of alchemy in the Society of Jesus in Ambix (1993), recovers an episode from Aguilon's career that is absent from his optical reputation: Jan Baptist van Helmont, writing about 1624 in a text defending himself against heresy charges, claimed to have personally witnessed Aguilon conducting alchemy experiments at the Jesuit College in Antwerp in 1596. According to van Helmont's account, Aguilon had enlisted an artisan goldsmith to teach him manual operations required for transmutation, had conducted 'repeated experiments attempting the transmutation of gold from various forms of silver,' and had used 'glass vessels imprinted with the yellow seal of the Society of Jesus' — a detail that led van Helmont to infer that the experiments 'had not happened without the approval of the Jesuit superiors.'

Baldwin notes that van Helmont made these claims in the context of defending himself against Jesuit-backed Inquisition charges (1634) and that his hostility to the Society colored his account. Nevertheless, the specific and detailed nature of his testimony, combined with the fact that 'these charges were never refuted by those ecclesiastics involved in his 1634 trial before the Inquisition,' lends some credibility to the account. According to van Helmont, Aguilon ultimately abandoned alchemy — 'dispirited and embarrassed by his lack of success and by the expense of the experiments' — and turned to optics, in which he achieved permanent distinction. The artisan goldsmith Aguilon had employed was dismissed and, according to van Helmont, 'lost his workshop, was forced to earn his livelihood as a soldier and to abandon his wife and children.' The episode illustrates both the existence of alchemical activity within Jesuit institutional contexts and the ambiguous attitudes toward such activity within the Society.
