Muhammad Ibn Umail

Role: ALCHEMIST | Era: MEDIEVAL

Muhammad ibn Umail al-Tamimi (fl. c. 900–960 CE), also known as Senior Zadith filius Hamuelis in Latin translation, was an Arabic alchemist active probably in Egypt whose works represent a significant strand of Islamic alchemy oriented toward spiritual and visionary dimensions alongside practical chemical knowledge. His major text, the Kitāb al-Māʾ al-Waraqī wa-al-Arḍ al-Najmiyya (Book of the Silvery Water and the Starry Earth), also known as the Mā al-Waraqī, combines allegorical poetry with prose commentary interpreting alchemical symbolism in terms of philosophical and spiritual transformation. The text circulated widely in Latin translation under the title Tabula Chemica (Chemical Table) and became one of the more influential Arabic alchemical texts in medieval Latin Europe, particularly for its imagery and allegorical approach. Ibn Umail's work was later subjected to elaborate commentary by Arabic alchemical scholars and was used by Carl Jung as evidence for the psychological interpretation of alchemical symbolism, though historians have largely rejected the Jungian framework in favor of contextual historical analysis.

Works and Significance

The Mā al-Waraqī describes the author's visionary encounter with a temple or hall in Egypt, where he interprets hieroglyphic images as encoding alchemical secrets. This imagery of Egyptian priestly wisdom encoded in visual symbols — and the narrative of the initiated practitioner who can read what the uninitiated cannot see — connects Ibn Umail's work to the broader Hermetic tradition that attributed the origins of alchemical knowledge to ancient Egypt. His allegorical and visionary approach contrasts with the more systematic, operationally focused alchemy of Jabir ibn Hayyan and Al-Razi (Rhazes), and represents a different strand of Islamic alchemical literature in which spiritual transformation and philosophical enlightenment are as central as practical chemistry. M. Mertens and H.E. Stapleton have provided scholarly analyses of Ibn Umail's work and its place in the history of Arabic alchemy.
