Stephan Michelspacher

Role: PHYSICIAN | Era: EARLY_MODERN

Stephan Michelspacher (fl. 1613–1619) was a Paracelsian physician and publisher active in the Tyrol and later Augsburg, best known as the author and publisher of Cabala: Spiegel der Kunst und Natur, in Alchymia (1615), one of the most visually striking alchemical emblem books of the early seventeenth century. The essay on Michelspacher in Divine Wisdom, Divine Nature: The Message of the Rosicrucian Manifestoes in the Visual Language of the Seventeenth Century (Amsterdam, 2014), edited by José Bouman and Cis van Heertum, provides the fullest modern account of his life and works, establishing the biographical outline and analyzing the visual and alchemical content of the Cabala in detail.

Little is known about Michelspacher's origins beyond that he practiced medicine as a Paracelsian physician in Tyrol in the early seventeenth century. As a Lutheran living in re-Catholicized Tyrol, he relocated to Augsburg around 1613 — possibly to escape the religious pressure that had led to the persecution of his associate Adam Halsmayr, who was sent to the galleys by the Jesuits. Augsburg, as a bi-confessional city under the terms of the Peace of Augsburg, offered the religious coexistence unavailable in Tyrol. There Michelspacher established a publishing house that operated until at least 1619 and produced works ranging across alchemy, anatomy, geometry, and music, reflecting the broad intellectual curiosity of his circle. Among his collaborators were Johann Remmelin (1583–1632), with whom he co-published the anatomical work Catoptron Microcosmicum (1613), and the Rosicrucian-affiliated mathematician Daniel Mögling, whose Speculum Sophicum Rhodostauroticum appeared from Michelspacher's press in 1618. The art collector Philip Hainhofer (1568–1647) was another important figure connected to his publishing activities.

The Cabala (1615 German, 1616 Latin) is Michelspacher's major contribution to alchemical literature. Its four elaborate engravings — designed by Michelspacher and executed by Raphael Custos or Custodis (1590/91–1664) — present the stages of the alchemical Great Work in a Paracelsian framework that integrates the three principles (Salt, Mercury, Sulphur or Tria Prima), Kabbalistic symbolism, and astrological correspondences. The Latin edition was dedicated to the Brotherhood of the Rosy Cross, making the Cabala one of the earliest works to formally align Paracelsian alchemy with the Rosicrucian movement whose manifestoes had appeared just two years earlier. The essay in Divine Wisdom, Divine Nature demonstrates how Michelspacher's visual program synthesizes Khunrath's Amphitheatrum tradition, Paracelsian medical alchemy, and the new Rosicrucian symbolic vocabulary into a coherent alchemical teaching.
