Hildegard of Bingen

Role: SCHOLAR | Era: MEDIEVAL

Hildegard of Bingen (1098–1179) was a German Benedictine abbess, visionary, composer, naturalist, and medical writer whose encyclopedic engagement with the natural world produced two major Latin texts on natural philosophy and medicine — Physica and Causae et Curae — that represent the most comprehensive female-authored natural-philosophical writing of the medieval period and an important node in the transmission of practical medical and mineralogical knowledge in twelfth-century Latin Europe. Born into a noble Rhineland family, she was offered as a tithe to the Church at the age of eight, educated under the anchoress Jutta of Sponheim at the Disibodenberg monastery, and eventually became abbess at Disibodenberg before founding her own monastery at Rupertsberg near Bingen around 1150. Her prophetic-visionary texts — the Scivias, the Liber Vitae Meritorum, and the Liber Divinorum Operum — were authorized by Pope Eugenius III in 1147–1148 following investigation by Bernard of Clairvaux, giving her the rare status of an officially approved female prophet in the medieval Catholic Church.

Natural Philosophy and Medicine

The Physica (also known as Liber Subtilitatum Diversarum Naturarum Creaturarum) is an encyclopedic treatment of the natural properties of plants, elements, trees, stones, fish, birds, animals, reptiles, and metals, organized by category and presenting the curative and harmful properties of each substance in a format that combines Galenic humoral theory with local practical knowledge. The text draws on the earlier tradition of herbals and lapidaries, including Isidore of Seville's Etymologiae and Dioscorides's De Materia Medica (accessible to Hildegard primarily in Latin adaptations), but it also reflects independent observation and a strong sense of the aesthetic and moral character of natural substances. Her descriptions of plants regularly note their taste, smell, and color as indicators of their medicinal properties, and her approach to mineralogy — including discussions of gold, silver, gems, and salts — is grounded in an understanding of their formative powers as expressions of divine creative order.

The Causae et Curae presents a more systematic medical theory, integrating Galenic humoral physiology with Hildegard's own cosmological framework. Her central concept of viriditas — greenness, vitality, the generative power flowing from God through creation — operates as an organizing principle for understanding health, disease, and the healing properties of natural substances. This concept, while grounded in Christian theological frameworks, has structural similarities to the alchemical notion of a universal animating principle underlying material change, and later writers have noted the resonance between Hildegardian viriditas and alchemical ideas of vital force in matter.

Relationship to Alchemical Tradition

Hildegard was not an alchemical practitioner, and her writings do not engage with transmutational alchemy as such. However, her encyclopedic treatment of the natural properties of minerals and metals, her interest in the formative powers of specific substances, and her articulation of a vitalistic philosophy of nature place her within the broader medieval intellectual context from which alchemical thought drew. Her descriptions of the properties of metals and stones — their hardness, their color, their celestial associations, their healing properties when properly prepared — overlap with the lapidary and mineralogical tradition that informed alchemical theory about metallic generation. Barbara Newman's extensive scholarship on Hildegard has situated her within medieval theories of nature and gender, and has identified her natural philosophy as a form of "natural theology" that integrated empirical observation with cosmological vision.

Scholarly Debates

The scholarly assessment of Hildegard's natural philosophical texts has shifted substantially over recent decades. Earlier dismissals of her medicine as merely folkloric or theologically conditioned have given way to more nuanced assessments that acknowledge both her dependence on the learned tradition and her independent observations. Flanagan's biography provides a comprehensive account of her life and institutional context. Victoria Sweet's medical-historical analysis of viriditas has argued for its coherence as a physiological concept. The question of Hildegard's literacy and access to sources — what texts she actually read, how thoroughly she knew Latin medical literature — remains contested. Her music has received substantial scholarly attention independent of her natural philosophy, and the relationship between her musical theory (including her use of the monochord) and her natural-philosophical thinking is an area of ongoing exploration.
