Hildegard of Bingen

Role: SCHOLAR | Era: MEDIEVAL

Hildegard of Bingen (1098-1179) was a German Benedictine abbess, visionary, composer, and natural philosopher whose encyclopedic writings on medicine, cosmology, and natural history made her one of the most intellectually productive figures of the twelfth century. Born in Bermersheim vor der Hohe in the Rhineland to a noble family, she was offered to the Church as a child oblate and educated at the Benedictine monastery of Disibodenberg under the anchoress Jutta von Sponheim. After Jutta's death in 1136 she became head of the female community and in 1150 led her community to a new foundation at Rupertsberg near Bingen. Her major works — Scivias (Know the Ways, 1141-1151), Liber Vitae Meritorum (Book of Life's Merits, 1158-1163), Liber Divinorum Operum (Book of Divine Works, 1163-1173), and the natural history encyclopedias Physica and Causae et Curae — combine prophetic vision, theological speculation, and practical knowledge of herbs, minerals, and medicines in ways that reflect the intellectual culture of twelfth-century monastic learning.

Natural Philosophy and Medicine

Hildegard's Physica (also known as Liber Simplicis Medicinae) is a nine-book encyclopedic treatment of natural substances: plants, elements, trees, stones, fish, birds, animals, reptiles, and metals, each entry describing the substance's properties, its relationship to human health, and its medical uses. Her Causae et Curae provides a systematic account of human physiology and pathology based on a humoral framework adapted from Galenic medicine and modified by her own theological and cosmological principles. The theory of viriditas (greening power or vitality) — Hildegard's concept of the living force animating plants, animals, and healthy human bodies — represents a distinctively Hildegardian modification of the standard Galenic framework, integrating theological ideas about divine creative power with practical medical knowledge.

Her treatment of minerals and metals in the Physica is relevant to the history of alchemy, though Hildegard was not an alchemist in any technical sense. She describes the properties of gold, silver, copper, iron, lead, tin, and mercury — and their medical uses — in terms that reflect the overlap between mineralogy, medicine, and the nascent Latin alchemical tradition of her period. The twelfth century was precisely the era in which the first Arabic alchemical texts were being translated into Latin by Gerard of Cremona and others; Hildegard's natural philosophy represents the pre-translation Latin tradition — grounded in Isidore of Seville, Bede, and Pliny — against which the new Arabic learning would be assimilated. Pamela Smith's framework for artisanal epistemology and embodied knowledge is applicable to Hildegard: her practical knowledge of plants, minerals, and medicines reflects direct engagement with material substances as well as textual learning.

Scholarly Debates

Scholars have debated the authenticity of works attributed to Hildegard and the relationship between her prophetic writings and her natural-philosophical encyclopedias. The Physica and Causae et Curae were long treated as authentically Hildegardian but more recent scholarship has raised questions about their precise relationship to her acknowledged prophetic works. Barbara Newman's Sister of Wisdom: Saint Hildegard's Theology of the Feminine (1987) provides the most comprehensive theological analysis; Priscilla Throop's translations of the Physica and Causae et Curae have made them accessible to non-specialists. Hildegard's significance for the history of alchemy and chemistry is as a representative of pre-scholastic Latin natural philosophy — the tradition that the Arabic translations would transform in the later twelfth and thirteenth centuries into the systematic natural philosophy within which Latin alchemy developed.

Natural Philosophy and the Hildegardian Tradition

Hildegard's medical and natural philosophical writings stand apart from the mainstream of twelfth-century Latin learning in their explicit grounding in visionary experience rather than scholastic commentary on authoritative texts. Where Albertus Magnus and his contemporaries worked within Aristotelian frameworks imported through Arabic and earlier Latin translation, Hildegard developed her understanding of the natural world through a different authority: direct divine illumination, which she described as the "living light" revealing the hidden correspondences of creation. This alternative epistemological claim gave her work both a distinctive character and a particular kind of authority: not the authority of Aristotle or Avicenna, but the authority of prophetic vision grounded in the institutional context of the Benedictine order and confirmed by Bernard of Clairvaux and, eventually, Pope Eugenius III.

The Physica — also known as the Liber Subtilitatum (Book of Subtleties) — covers minerals, plants, animals, and the elements. Its sections on metals and stones describe their curative properties, their spiritual correspondences, and their generation within the earth — a topic that connects to alchemical matter theory in later medieval Latin writers even if Hildegard herself does not engage with alchemical texts or procedures. Her four-element cosmology, her concern with the balance of qualities (hot, cold, wet, dry) as the foundation of health, and her interest in the hidden powers of created things (viriditas, the greening power of living nature) overlap with alchemical natural philosophy without being derived from it.

The Causae et Curae addresses the physiology and pathology of the human body within a framework that combines Galenic humoral medicine with Hildegard's distinctive cosmological vision. Her account of sexuality, conception, and the formation of the fetus draws on humoral physiology but also on a vision of how the elements and the stars contribute to human temperament. The gender categories in her physiology — her account of different humoral constitutions in men and women — have attracted attention from historians of medicine and gender interested in medieval understandings of bodily difference.

Within the historiography of alchemy and learned magic, Hildegard matters primarily as evidence for the breadth of natural philosophical inquiry in the twelfth century before the full impact of Aristotelian natural philosophy arrived through translation. She represents a different current of medieval learning — one that was not displaced but rather supplemented by the scholastic tradition, and that retained influence in practical contexts (herbalism, medicine, music, theology) that scholastic philosophy addressed less directly. Barbara Newman's scholarship (no relation to William Newman) on Hildegard's visionary theology and Sabina Flanagan's biography provide the scholarly foundations for situating her within the history of medieval learning. Her Physica and Causae et Curae have been edited critically and translated, making her natural philosophical work accessible for comparative study with contemporaneous alchemical and medical traditions.

The modern revival of interest in Hildegard — through recordings of her music, through the "alternative medicine" appropriation of her herbalism and dietary recommendations, and through her beatification and canonization by Pope Benedict XVI in 2012 — has sometimes distorted the historical picture by projecting contemporary concerns onto a twelfth-century abbess. The scholarly Hildegard, whose visionary claims were grounded in the specific institutional context of Benedictine religious life and the theological controversies of her era, is a more complex and more interesting figure than the "medieval health guru" of popular appropriation. Her Physica reflects genuine observation of the natural world, but within a framework of symbolic correspondences and theological meaning that is inseparable from her medical recommendations and cannot be extracted from them without misrepresenting what she was doing.
