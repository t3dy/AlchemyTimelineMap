Francis Bacon

Role: PHILOSOPHER | Era: MEDIEVAL

Francis Bacon (1561–1626) was an English philosopher, statesman, and essayist whose works on the reform of natural philosophy — most notably the Novum Organum (1620) and the New Atlantis (published posthumously, 1627) — articulated an influential program for a new, empirically grounded science based on inductive method, systematic experimentation, and collaborative investigation rather than the received authority of Aristotle and the scholastic tradition. Born in London and educated at Trinity College, Cambridge, and Gray's Inn, Bacon pursued a legal and political career that culminated in his appointment as Lord Chancellor in 1618 before his fall from favor and conviction for accepting bribes in 1621. His relationship to alchemy is ambivalent: he criticized the obscurantism and fraud associated with alchemical practitioners, while simultaneously acknowledging the practical knowledge embedded in alchemical operations and drawing on alchemical concepts — particularly the notion of separating and recombining material principles — in his own natural philosophical framework.

Works and the Reform of Natural Philosophy

Bacon's intellectual program, outlined in the unfinished Instauratio Magna (Great Instauration), called for a wholesale reform of the sciences based on proper inductive method: moving from careful, systematic observation of natural phenomena to general principles, rather than deducing consequences from pre-established theoretical frameworks. The Novum Organum presented his theory of the "Idols" — systematic distortions of perception and reasoning that prevent accurate natural knowledge — and proposed a refined method of eliminative induction. The New Atlantis depicted a utopian research institution, Salomon's House, staffed by specialists in every form of natural investigation, where knowledge was systematically gathered and applied to human benefit. This vision of collaborative, institutionalized natural inquiry was influential on the founders of the Royal Society.

Bacon's attitude toward chemistry and alchemy was nuanced. He criticized alchemists for their "enigmatical writing" and their tendency to fraud, but he also acknowledged that alchemists had produced useful practical results — mineral acids, distilled essences, new preparations of metals — that natural philosophers should not ignore. His own natural philosophy drew on the concept of "latent schematism" — the hidden configuration of particles underlying the observable properties of matter — that has structural similarities to corpuscular accounts used by alchemical theorists. Pamela Smith has noted that Bacon's program for a reformed natural science, while explicitly opposed to scholastic speculation, was less far from the practical, workshop-based knowledge of artisans and alchemists than his polemical self-presentation suggests.

Relationship to Alchemical Tradition

The Sylva Sylvarum (1627), Bacon's wide-ranging natural history, contains numerous reports of chemical experiments and natural observations that overlap significantly with alchemical practice: the behavior of metals in fire, the production of colored flames, the dissolving of metals in acids, the behavior of mercury. His theoretical framework of form — understanding the "form" of a property like heat or gold as the specific particle configuration that produces it — resonates with scholastic accounts of substantial form used in alchemical theory, even though Bacon explicitly positioned himself against scholastic natural philosophy. William Newman has noted that Bacon's criticisms of alchemy were often aimed at particular practices and practitioners, not at the underlying goal of understanding and manipulating material processes, which he shared.

Scholarly Debates

The question of Bacon's influence on the Scientific Revolution has been extensively debated. Earlier historiography (from the seventeenth century through much of the twentieth) credited Bacon as the prophet of empirical science whose inductive method inspired Newton and the Royal Society. More recent scholarship has complicated this picture: Lisa Jardine and Alan Stewart's biography examines Bacon's intellectual career in its political context; Pamela Smith's work on artisanal knowledge has situated Bacon's reform program within a broader culture of practical knowledge; and historians of logic and method have questioned how novel and how influential Bacon's inductive theory actually was. His relationship to alchemy specifically has been examined by Newman and Principe as part of their broader reassessment of the relationship between alchemy and early modern chemistry.

Travels and Connections

Francis Bacon (1561-1626) was born at York House in london and moved through the institutions of the Elizabethan and Jacobean establishment: Trinity College, cambridge (1573-1575), Gray's Inn, and a formative diplomatic posting in paris and across France under the ambassador Sir Amias Paulet (1576-1579). His father's sudden death in 1579 recalled him to England, where he built a long legal and political career culminating as Lord Chancellor (1618), Baron Verulam, and Viscount St Alban, before a 1621 conviction for accepting gifts from litigants ended his public life.

In retirement at the family seat of Gorhambury near St Albans he completed much of his philosophical work, assisted by his amanuensis Thomas Hobbes. Bacon was a methodologist and critic of alchemy, not a practitioner: he derided the alchemists and Paracelsian chemists as undisciplined 'empirics' who hoard particulars like ants, even as his own theory of tangible and pneumatic ('spirit') matter drew on the chemical tradition he sought to reform. His program of inductive natural history and the imagined research college of Salomon's House in New Atlantis shaped the experimental ambitions of Robert Boyle and the early Royal Society.

He died at Highgate in April 1626; tradition (reported by John Aubrey) attributes his fatal chill to an experiment stuffing a hen with snow to test refrigeration, a story modern scholars treat as semi-legendary.

Itinerary

  1. London (York House, Strand) 1561–1573 attested
    Born 22 January 1561 at York House near the Strand, son of Sir Nicholas Bacon (Lord Keeper of the Great Seal) and Anne Cooke Bacon. Educated largely at home in early years owing to poor health; some childhood spent at the family seat at Gorhambury.
  2. Cambridge (Trinity College) 1573–1575 attested
    Entered Trinity College, Cambridge, on 5 April 1573 at age 12 with his elder brother Anthony, under the tutelage of John Whitgift (future Archbishop of Canterbury). Completed his course of study c. December 1575. There he reportedly grew dissatisfied with Aristotelian scholasticism, a discontent that later informed his methodological reform.
  3. London (Gray's Inn) 1576 attested
    Admitted to Gray's Inn on 27 June 1576 with his brother Anthony to begin legal study, before departing almost immediately for France. Gray's Inn would be his principal London base for the rest of his career (readmitted 1579; outer barrister 1582; bencher 1586; Reader 1587).
  4. Paris and France (embassy of Sir Amias Paulet) 1576–1579 attested
    Travelled to France in September 1576 in the train of Sir Amias Paulet, Elizabeth I's resident ambassador to the French court. Remained roughly two and a half years, based at Paris but also visiting Blois, Poitiers, and Tours, studying civil law, languages, and statecraft and undertaking routine diplomatic errands (on at least one occasion carrying letters to England for Walsingham, Burghley, Leicester, and the Queen). Returned to England following his father's sudden death in February 1579. (Note: some sources mention onward travel to Italy and Spain; this is less securely documented.)
  5. London (Gray's Inn and Westminster / political career) 1579–1621 attested
    Returned 1579 and pursued law and a parliamentary/political career from his Gray's Inn base. MP from 1581 (Bossiney, then Melcombe, Taunton, Liverpool, Middlesex, etc.); knighted 1603 at the accession of James I; Solicitor General 1607; Attorney General 1613; Lord Keeper 1617; Lord Chancellor 1618; created Baron Verulam 12 July 1618 and Viscount St Alban 27 January 1621. In May 1621 he was convicted of accepting bribes (gifts from litigants), fined, briefly imprisoned in the Tower, and barred from office, ending his political life.
  6. Gorhambury and Verulam House, near St Albans (Hertfordshire) 1601–1626 attested
    Family country seat at Old Gorhambury House, built 1563-68 by his father Sir Nicholas Bacon; Francis inherited and extended it, adding a water garden and Roman-style banqueting house, and built nearby Verulam House (completed c. 1617). After his 1621 fall this was the retreat where he completed much of his philosophical work, assisted by his amanuensis Thomas Hobbes. His viscountcy ('St Alban') and barony ('Verulam', the Roman name for St Albans) take their names from this locality.
  7. Highgate, London (Arundel House) 1626 attested
    Died 9 April 1626 of pneumonia/respiratory illness at the Earl of Arundel's house in Highgate. Tradition (reported by John Aubrey) holds that he caught a fatal chill while stuffing a hen with snow near Highgate to test refrigeration as a means of preserving flesh; modern scholarship treats the frozen-chicken story as semi-legendary, well attested in flavour but thin in documentary support. Buried at St Michael's Church, St Albans.
