Francis Bacon

Role: PHILOSOPHER | Era: MEDIEVAL

Francis Bacon (1561–1626) was an English philosopher, statesman, and essayist whose program for the reform of natural philosophy — set out above all in the Novum Organum (1620) and the posthumous New Atlantis (1627) — became one of the most influential blueprints for an empirically grounded, collaborative, and institutionally organised science in early modern Europe. Working at the intersection of law, court politics, and natural inquiry, Bacon argued that received Aristotelian and scholastic learning had become sterile, and that genuine knowledge of nature required a disciplined method of observation, experiment, and eliminative induction. His attitude toward the alchemical tradition was deliberately double-edged: he ridiculed the secrecy, fraud, and credulity he associated with practitioners of Chrysopoeia (Gold-Making), yet he repeatedly mined alchemical and artisanal practice for the very kind of operational knowledge of matter that his new philosophy was meant to systematise.

Life and Historical Context

Bacon was born in London on 22 January 1561, the youngest son of Sir Nicholas Bacon, Lord Keeper of the Great Seal under Elizabeth I, and Anne Cooke, a formidably learned woman connected through her sister to William Cecil, Lord Burghley. He entered Trinity College, Cambridge, at the age of twelve, where he later claimed to have conceived an early distaste for the philosophy of Aristotle — not, he insisted, for the man himself but for the unfruitful disputatiousness into which his followers had fallen. After legal training at Gray's Inn and a formative period in France, Bacon embarked on a long parliamentary and legal career, serving as a member of the Commons, as Solicitor General, Attorney General, and finally, in 1618, as Lord Chancellor, the highest legal office in the realm. He was created Baron Verulam and, in 1621, Viscount St Alban.

That same year his public career collapsed. Accused of accepting gifts from litigants, Bacon confessed to corruption, was stripped of office, fined, and briefly imprisoned in the Tower. He spent his final five years in enforced retirement, an enforced leisure that proved extraordinarily productive: much of his most ambitious natural-philosophical and historical writing dates from after his fall. He died in April 1626, reportedly after catching a chill while stuffing a fowl with snow to test the preservative effect of cold — an anecdote, transmitted by John Aubrey, that has been cherished precisely because it casts the philosopher of experiment as dying in the act of one. Modern biographers such as Lisa Jardine and Alan Stewart caution against reading Bacon's life through the later myth of the disinterested scientist, stressing instead a career-long entanglement of natural philosophy with ambition, patronage, and the machinery of the early Stuart state.

The wider context of Bacon's work was a Europe in which the boundaries between natural philosophy, alchemy, medicine, and craft were porous and contested. The recovery of ancient texts during the Renaissance had brought renewed authority to Aristotle but also to rival traditions — Hermetic, Neoplatonic, and Paracelsian — that promised power over nature through occult correspondences and chemical operation. Universities still taught a syllabus organised around the disputation of received authorities, while a parallel world of physicians, assayers, distillers, and metallurgists accumulated practical knowledge that rarely found its way into print. Bacon positioned himself self-consciously against the first of these worlds and ambivalently toward the second, arguing that learning had stagnated because it prized eloquence and logical subtlety over fruitful works. His insistence that the proper end of knowledge was use — the relief of the human condition through command over nature — reflected both a Protestant providential confidence and the practical mentality of a lawyer accustomed to weighing evidence and arguing from particulars.

Works and the Great Instauration

Bacon conceived his mature writings as parts of a single vast, ultimately unfinished project, the Instauratio Magna or Great Instauration, planned in six divisions running from a survey of the existing sciences to a fully reconstructed natural philosophy. Only fragments were completed. The first part is represented by The Advancement of Learning (1605) and its expanded Latin version De Augmentis Scientiarum (1623), a taxonomy of human knowledge that mapped what was known and, more pointedly, what remained to be discovered. The second and most famous part is the Novum Organum (1620), the "new instrument" intended to replace the Aristotelian Organon of logic.

The Novum Organum opens with the doctrine of the Idols — the Idols of the Tribe rooted in human nature, of the Cave in individual temperament, of the Marketplace in the distortions of language, and of the Theatre in inherited dogmas and philosophical systems. These were Bacon's diagnosis of the systematic errors that corrupt perception and reasoning before any inquiry begins. Against them he set a method of eliminative induction, organised through tables of presence, absence, and degrees, by which the investigator would progressively exclude false explanations and converge on the "form" — the underlying law or configuration — of a phenomenon such as heat. The third part of the Instauration was to be a vast natural and experimental history, of which the Sylva Sylvarum (1627), a sprawling collection of a thousand experiments and observations, is the chief surviving instalment. The New Atlantis, appended to the Sylva Sylvarum, sketched the imagined research college of Salomon's House, where teams of investigators gathered, tested, and applied knowledge for "the relief of man's estate." Antonio Pérez-Ramos has emphasised that this productive, "maker's knowledge" ideal — knowing a thing through being able to make it — runs as a unifying thread through the entire corpus.

Bacon's method was meant as a corrective to two failed extremes. The "Empirics," he wrote, resembled ants, heaping up undigested observations; the "Rationalists" resembled spiders, spinning systems out of their own substance with no relation to fact. The true philosopher should imitate the bee, gathering material from the natural world and transforming it by the power of the mind. Induction, on this account, was not the mere enumeration of instances — which Bacon dismissed as childish — but a rigorous, graduated procedure of exclusion that ascended cautiously from particulars through intermediate axioms to the most general laws, always returning to experiment to check its results. He devoted attention to "prerogative instances," especially privileged or revealing cases that could shortcut the labour of induction, and to the deliberate contrivance of "experiments of light" designed to yield understanding rather than immediate profit. Graham Rees, the modern editor of Bacon's natural-philosophical writings, has shown how much technical detail underlies this apparently programmatic scheme, including a speculative cosmology of active "spirits" and pneumatic matter that Bacon developed in works such as the De Principiis atque Originibus and that connects his method to a substantive, if idiosyncratic, theory of matter.

Operational Knowledge and the Matter of the Laboratory

For all his reputation as a methodologist, Bacon insisted that knowledge be tested against the resistant particularity of material things, and the Sylva Sylvarum is dense with reports of operations that overlap directly with the chemical and metallurgical workshop. It records the behaviour of metals heated in fire, the production of coloured flames, the dissolving of metals in mineral acids, the volatility and recombination of mercury, the souring and fermenting of liquids, and the slow alterations wrought by burial, putrefaction, and time. Bacon was attentive to Distillation, to Calcination, and to Putrefaction as means of separating and recombining the constituents of bodies, and he treated the furnace and the still as instruments of natural philosophy rather than as the property of secretive adepts.

Underlying these reports was Bacon's conviction that the observable qualities of matter arise from a hidden inner structure he called the "latent schematism" and "latent process" of bodies — the concealed arrangement and motion of their minute parts. To grasp the "form" of gold or of heat was, for Bacon, to grasp this latent configuration, so that in principle a body could be reconstituted by reproducing it. Here his thought ran close to the corpuscular and matter-theoretical ideas that also animated alchemical writers, even as he repudiated their language. William Newman has stressed that the long alchemical tradition of analysing and reconstituting substances through fire supplied exactly the model of controlled material transformation that Bacon's program required, and that his distinction between attacking bad practitioners and embracing the underlying project of manipulating matter was deliberate. Bacon credited the practitioners of Operational Chemistry with real discoveries — mineral acids, distilled essences, refined preparations of metals — that abstract natural philosophy had failed to produce, and he insisted these be gathered into his experimental history. At the same time he was emphatic that he did not endorse the alchemists' grandest claim: he treated the alleged Transmutation of base metals into gold as an open and largely dubious question, to be settled by experiment rather than asserted on the authority of secret texts, and he mocked those who staked fortunes and reputations on the Philosopher's Stone.

Critique and Appropriation of Alchemy and Natural Magic

Bacon's published verdict on alchemy was famously ambivalent, and is best read as a critique of a discipline he simultaneously plundered. He compared the alchemists to the sons in Aesop's fable who, told that treasure lay buried in their vineyard, dug it over and found no gold but, by their digging, secured an abundant harvest: their pursuit of an illusory goal had, almost in spite of itself, advanced the practical arts. He condemned the "enigmatical" and allegorical mode of alchemical writing — the deliberate obscurity that later scholars associate with Emblematic Alchemy — as an obstacle to the open, cumulative communication that science demanded, and he scorned the fraud and self-deception he saw among practitioners. He was similarly wary of Magia Naturalis (Natural Magic), the tradition of natural magic that promised command over occult virtues; he wanted its operative ambition, the desire to do things with nature, while stripping away its credulity and its appeals to hidden sympathies that could not be tested.

Yet the appropriation was substantial. The Baconian ideal of wringing nature's secrets from her by "vexing" her under the constraint of art — putting nature to the question, as one would a witness or a prisoner — drew on the long magical and alchemical conviction that art could accelerate and redirect natural processes. Pamela Smith has argued that Bacon's reform, for all its anti-scholastic polemic, stood far closer to the practical, embodied knowledge of artisans and alchemists than his rhetorical self-presentation allowed, and she situates him within the wider culture of Artisanal Epistemology in which knowing was inseparable from doing and making. Where Bacon broke decisively from contemporaries such as Robert Fludd — whose Hermetic and Paracelsian cosmology he implicitly rejected — was in refusing to treat the universe as a book of correspondences to be read; nature was instead to be interrogated, taken apart, and rebuilt. The chemical reformers in the line of Paracelsus (Theophrastus Bombastus von Hohenheim) and Jan van Helmont shared Bacon's hostility to bookish Aristotelianism, but Bacon distrusted their visionary and theological framing as much as he valued their laboratory results. He took particular care to separate the operative claims of these traditions, which could in principle be tested, from their metaphysical scaffolding, which he regarded as unverifiable. The doctrine of Sulfur-Mercury Theory, for instance, or the broader conviction that all bodies could be reduced to a small set of active principles, interested him chiefly as a hypothesis about the inner constitution of matter that experiment might confirm or refute, not as a revealed truth handed down through a chain of ancient sages. In this respect Bacon's posture toward Chrysopoeia (Gold-Making) mirrored his posture toward natural magic: he wished to convert their secret, individual, and quasi-religious pursuit of power over nature into a public, collective, and methodical enterprise, retaining the ambition while discarding the mystery.

Relationships, Patrons, and Contemporaries

Bacon's natural philosophy was embedded in a dense web of family, court, and intellectual relationships rather than pursued in scholarly isolation. His maternal connection to the Cecils placed him at the centre of Elizabethan and Jacobean political life, even as his rivalry with Sir Edward Coke over law and office shaped much of his career. He dedicated major works to James I, courting royal patronage for an Instauration he hoped the crown might fund as a national project — a hope embodied in the state-sponsored research college of Salomon's House. He employed assistants and amanuenses, among them for a time the young Thomas Hobbes, who served as a secretary and translator and whose later mechanical philosophy bears the imprint of Baconian naturalism even as Thomas Hobbes diverged sharply on method and metaphysics.

Bacon read and reacted to the leading natural philosophers of his age. He was sceptical of the new astronomy, never fully accepting the Copernican system, and he engaged critically with the Hermetic and magical currents represented by Robert Fludd and, at a remove, by the tradition descending from Henry Cornelius Agrippa and Giordano Bruno. He stood at a generational distance from the English magus John Dee, whose blend of mathematics, alchemy, and angelic conversation epitomised the older synthesis Bacon sought to discipline and replace. Within the alchemical lineage, his English near-contemporaries included figures such as Samuel Norton, heir to a tradition of native chrysopoeia that Bacon regarded with characteristic wariness. The relationship that mattered most for Bacon's posterity, however, was not with any living contemporary but with the generation that would read him after his death, and who would convert his programmatic writings into an institutional reality he had only imagined.

Transmission and Reception in the Royal Society

Bacon's influence ran through the mid-seventeenth-century reform movements associated with Samuel Hartlib's circle and, decisively, into the founding ideology of the Royal Society of London, chartered in 1660–62. The Society's apologists explicitly cast Bacon as their prophet: Thomas Sprat's History of the Royal Society (1667) presented the institution as the fulfilment of Salomon's House, and Joseph Glanvill hailed Bacon as the herald of a new experimental age. The frontispiece to Sprat's History placed a bust of Bacon beside the figures of the Society's patron and president, fixing the iconography of Bacon as founder of modern science for generations.

The fellows who carried this program forward were themselves deeply engaged with the chemical tradition Bacon had both criticised and exploited. Robert Boyle, the Society's most prominent natural philosopher, pursued a corpuscular Chymistry that retained transmutational ambitions while insisting on the rigorous, witnessed experiment that Baconian method prescribed; Boyle worked closely with the chymist George Starkey, whose furnace practice supplied much of his laboratory expertise. Isaac Newton, heir to this milieu, devoted decades to alchemical reading and experiment alongside his mathematical and optical work, a pursuit that twentieth-century scholarship long suppressed and recent historiography has restored to view. Bacon's vision of cumulative, collaborative, state-supported inquiry — knowledge gathered by many hands and applied to human benefit — proved more durable than the specific details of his inductive logic, and it shaped the rhetoric and organisation of European science well into the eighteenth century. Even the later chemical revolution traced by figures such as Antoine Lavoisier unfolded within an experimental and institutional culture whose self-understanding owed much to the Baconian template.

Scholarly Debates and Modern Historiography

The interpretation of Bacon has swung sharply over four centuries. From the seventeenth century through much of the twentieth, he was canonised as the prophet of empirical science whose inductive method anticipated and inspired the achievements of the Royal Society and of Newton. Twentieth-century philosophers of science, by contrast, frequently dismissed Baconian induction as naïve — a mechanical fact-gathering that no working scientist actually practised — and questioned whether his method had ever genuinely guided discovery. More recent scholarship has moved past both the hagiography and the dismissal. Antonio Pérez-Ramos reinterpreted Bacon through the "maker's knowledge" tradition, arguing that his epistemology was operative and constructive rather than passively observational, and far subtler than the caricature of crude empiricism allowed.

The reassessment of Bacon's relation to alchemy belongs to the broader revisionist project led by William R. Newman and Lawrence M. Principe, who have dismantled the older sharp opposition between "irrational" alchemy and "rational" chemistry, coining the historians' term chymistry to capture their continuity, and who read Bacon's critique of practitioners as compatible with his appropriation of their operative goals. Pamela H. Smith has placed Bacon within a history of artisanal and embodied knowledge, narrowing the gap between his learned program and the workshop. Wouter J. Hanegraaff and Brian P. Copenhaver, working on the Hermetic and magical traditions, have clarified what Bacon was reacting against, situating his rejection of correspondence-thinking within the long retreat of Renaissance occult philosophy. Against the once-dominant reading associated with Frances Yates — the Yates Paradigm that made Hermeticism the secret engine of the Scientific Revolution — these scholars present a more differentiated account in which Bacon selectively absorbs operative ambitions while repudiating the metaphysics that carried them. Lisa Jardine and Alan Stewart, finally, have re-embedded Bacon in the political and rhetorical world of the Jacobean court, reminding historians that the philosopher of experiment was also a working lawyer, courtier, and disgraced minister, and that his science cannot be cleanly separated from his statecraft. The modern consensus is neither that Bacon founded experimental science single-handedly nor that his method was a sterile fiction, but that he furnished an enormously consequential image of what organised, cumulative, useful natural inquiry might be — an image whose power lay as much in its institutional and rhetorical vision as in its philosophical detail.
