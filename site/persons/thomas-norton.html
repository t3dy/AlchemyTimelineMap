Thomas Norton of Bristol

Role: SCHOLAR | Era: MODERN

Thomas Norton of Bristol (d. 1513) was one of the most important English alchemical practitioners of the fifteenth century and the author of the Ordinal of Alchemy (1477), a Middle English poem that became, alongside George Ripley's Compound of Alchemy (1471), one of the two canonical texts of fifteenth-century English alchemy. Jennifer M. Rampling, in The Experimental Fire: Inventing English Alchemy, 1300–1700 (University of Chicago Press, 2020), identifies the Ordinal as 'the only English work of the fifteenth century to rival Ripley's Compound in posthumous celebrity.' Almost nothing is known about Norton's life outside of what can be inferred from the poem itself. He describes himself as a 'Somerset practitioner' and his great-grandson was Samuel Norton (1548–1604), who served Queen Elizabeth I as an alchemical authority and drew heavily on his great-grandfather's text in his own alchemical Key (1577). In the 'Prohemium' to the Ordinal, Thomas Norton warned against textual corruption: 'And changing of som oone sillable / May make this boke vnprofitable' — a characteristic concern with the accurate transmission of alchemical knowledge. Norton also drew on Chaucer's 'Canon's Yeoman's Tale' as an alchemical authority, indicating his sophisticated reading of English literary tradition. Along with Ripley, Norton was incorporated by Elias Ashmole (1617–1692) into the Theatrum Chemicum Britannicum (London, 1652), which became the preeminent compilation of English alchemical verse and served as the primary vehicle for Norton's posthumous reputation well into the eighteenth century. The modern critical edition of the Ordinal is Thomas Norton's The Ordinal of Alchemy, ed. John Reidy (Oxford: Early English Text Society, 1975).
