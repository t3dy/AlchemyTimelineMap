Maria the Jewess (Maria Hebraea)

Role: ALCHEMIST | Era: LATE_ANTIQUE

Maria the Jewess (Maria Hebraea, fl. ca. 200 CE), also known as Mary the Prophetess, is the earliest non-mythical alchemist known to the historical record. Raphael Patai, in his comprehensive study published in Ambix (1982), identifies her as 'the founding mother of western alchemy,' arguing that the extant documentary evidence places her earlier than all other named practitioners except the entirely mythical figures (Hermes Trismegistus, Isis, and their legendary predecessors). The primary evidence for Maria comes from the writings of Zosimus of Panopolis (fl. late 3rd–early 4th century CE), who quotes her as 'the first' of the ancient authorities and treats her with 'respect bordering on awe,' frequently calling her 'the divine Maria' and citing her as 'the supreme authority in the Great Art.' From the fact that Zosimus treats her as one of 'the ancients,' Patai infers that her floruit should be placed at least two generations before Zosimus, that is, no later than the early third century CE.

Maria made major contributions to alchemical apparatus. The most famous is the balneum Mariae (water bath or double boiler), still known in French as the bain Marie and in German as the Marienbad. She also invented or described the kerotakis (a metal plate apparatus for treating metals with vapors), the tribikos (a copper three-pronged still), and a range of other furnaces and apparatus described in her treatise On Apparatus and Furnaces (Greek: Kaminographia or Peri Kaminon kai Organon). The tribikos is described in detail in Zosimus's own words: copper tubes 'a little thicker than that of a pastry-cook's copper frying pan,' a cubit and a half long, with three openings fitted to a still-head, glass flasks at the ends 'large and strong so that they may not break with the heat coming from the water in the middle.' Maria described glass vessels as especially valuable 'because they see without touching' and permit the safe manipulation of dangerous materials including mercury, which she called 'the deadly poison, since it dissolves gold.'

Maria's most famous doctrinal contribution is the axiom that 'one becomes two, two becomes three, and by means of the third the fourth achieves unity; thus two are but one,' which C.G. Jung called 'one of the central axioms of alchemy' that 'runs like a leitmotiv throughout almost the whole of the lifetime of alchemy, extending over more than seventeen centuries.' Patai interprets the axiom as referring to the successive combination of three or four metallic bodies (the tetrasomia: copper, iron, lead, and zinc) that are distinct at the outset and become unified at the end of the operation. Maria also taught that the Great Work must be performed only in the Egyptian month Pharmuthi (March–April), and emphasized the unification of 'male' and 'female' materials as the fundamental alchemical operation. Her religious identity as Jewish is significant in the context of Zosimus's writings, which treat the Jews as 'the repositories of alchemical wisdom.'
