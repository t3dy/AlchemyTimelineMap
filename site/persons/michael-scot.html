Michael Scot

Role: TRANSLATOR | Era: MEDIEVAL

Michael Scot (ca. 1175 – ca. 1232) was a Scottish scholar, astrologer, translator, and natural philosopher who served at the court of Holy Roman Emperor Frederick II in Sicily and whose name appears in the prologues of the Ars Alchemie, one of the earliest surviving Latin practical alchemy texts. Antony Vinciguerra, in his study of the Ars Alchemie as the first Latin text on practical alchemy (Ambix, 2009), identifies Scot as the compiler or co-compiler of the text: one of the work's prologues opens, 'Ego vero magister Miccael scottus interpretationem aggredior et tibi magno Theophilo gayto saracenorum tecum Tunixe, huius munusculum apporto et secreta nature et verba philosophica que audivi tecum volo alchimiam translatare' — 'I, Master Michael Scot, undertake this translation and bring to you, great Theophilus, master of the Saracens with you at Tunis, this small gift, and wish to translate with you the secret nature and philosophical words that I have heard.' This dedication to a 'Theophilus of Tunis' reflects the Mediterranean cross-cultural milieu of Frederick II's court and the Arabic translation movement in which Scot played a central role.

Scot was among the most productive Arabic-Latin translators of the thirteenth century. At Toledo and later at the Sicilian court, he translated or assisted in translating the astronomical works of al-Bitruji (Alpetragius), the biological writings of Aristotle with Averroes' commentaries, and a range of other Arabic scientific texts that had no Latin equivalent. Dante placed him in Hell's fourth circle of sorcerers in the Commedia, indicating his reputation as an astrologer-magician across subsequent generations. Vinciguerra's analysis establishes that the Ars Alchemie is largely a compilation, adapting the prologue of Hugo of Santalla's Ars Geomantie (12th century) with alchemical substitutions for astrological terms, and drawing on other Arabic-derived texts including the De Aluminibus et Salibus and the Liber Hermetis. The result, Vinciguerra argues, is the earliest surviving example of a Latin alchemical text organized around theory and practice that is not a direct Arabic translation but rather a Latin compilation from multiple Arabic-derived sources — making Scot's compilation a pivotal moment in the establishment of an autonomous Latin alchemical tradition.
