Khalid ibn Yazid

Role: PATRON | Era: MEDIEVAL

Khalid ibn Yazid (d. 704) was an Umayyad prince and member of the ruling house of early Islamic Syria who has been associated in medieval sources with the earliest patronage of Arabic alchemy. Mohamed Yahia Haschmi, in his survey of the beginnings of Arab alchemy (Ambix, 1961), summarizes the evidence: according to the tenth-century bibliographer al-Nadim, Khalid was 'the first Muslim to interest himself in alchemy' and commissioned from the Egyptian monk Morienus (Marianus) and others the translation of Greek alchemical texts into Arabic, including a work known as 'Kitab Qaratisul-Hikma' ('The Wisdom of Krates'). Haschmi notes, however, that Julius Ruska disputed whether this translation was in fact made during Khalid's lifetime, rather than attributed to him at a later date. E. J. Holmyard, summarizing the evidence, concluded that Khalid ibn Yazid ordered the first Arabic translations of Greek alchemy texts and that some Arabic alchemical works attributed to him still exist, though none 'have been closely studied.'

The historical question of Khalid's role in Arabic alchemy is entangled with the broader uncertainty about the origins of the Jabiriyan corpus. Paul Kraus, in his fundamental research, dated the composition of the works attributed to Jabir ibn Hayyan to the second half of the ninth century, which would preclude Jabir from being an eighth-century court alchemist under Harun al-Rashid. If this dating is accepted, Arab alchemy as a literary tradition began later than the traditional accounts suggest, and Khalid's role in commissioning translations becomes even harder to evaluate. Haschmi himself argues against Kraus's late dating, pointing to several pieces of circumstantial evidence — including a poem by al-Akhtal (d. 710) that contains the word 'elixir' — for an early Arabic knowledge of alchemical concepts predating the main Jabiriyan corpus.
