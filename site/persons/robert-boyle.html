Robert Boyle

Role: CHEMIST | Era: EARLY_MODERN

Robert Boyle (1627–1691) was an Anglo-Irish natural philosopher, experimentalist, and lay theologian, one of the most influential figures of the seventeenth-century scientific movement and a founding fellow of the Royal Society of London. Born at Lismore Castle in the Irish province of Munster and active chiefly at Oxford and London during the Restoration era, Boyle is remembered in popular accounts as a father of modern chemistry who freed the discipline from alchemical mysticism. The historical reality, recovered through decades of archival scholarship by Lawrence Principe and others, is more interesting and more unsettling to that tidy narrative: Boyle was a serious and lifelong practitioner of Chymistry, who pursued the transmutation of metals and the preparation of the Philosopher's Stone as legitimate experimental goals even as he reformed the rhetoric, secrecy, and conceptual looseness of the chymical tradition he inherited.

Life and Historical Context

Boyle was born on 25 January 1627, the fourteenth child and seventh son of Richard Boyle, first Earl of Cork, one of the wealthiest and most ruthless of the English settlers who had risen to power in Ireland. This inheritance mattered: throughout his life Boyle was financially independent, never needing a salaried position, a patron's favour, or the sale of medicines to sustain himself. He could afford the best-equipped private laboratory in England and a staff of paid assistants, and he could afford to publish, or withhold, exactly as he chose. Sent to Eton as a boy, he then undertook a continental tour with a tutor, spending formative years in Geneva and travelling in Italy, where he was in Florence at the time of Galileo's death in early 1642. A famous storm during his adolescence triggered a religious crisis that fixed in him a lifelong, scrupulous piety; he came to regard the study of nature as a form of priesthood, the investigator a "priest of nature" reading the divine handiwork in the created world.

Returning to a Britain convulsed by civil war, Boyle eventually settled at his estate of Stalbridge in Dorset, where in the late 1640s he began the chemical experiments that would absorb him for the rest of his life. In these years he also fell under the influence of the reforming circle gathered around the émigré intelligencer Samuel Hartlib, a network committed to the advancement of useful knowledge, agriculture, medicine, and chymistry for the public good; it was through Hartlib's connections that Boyle first encountered the practical alchemy that would shape his laboratory. By the mid-1650s he had moved to Oxford, then home to a circle of experimental philosophers sometimes called the "Oxford experimental club," whose members included John Wilkins, Christopher Wren, and Thomas Willis, and there he assembled the laboratory and the assistants — among them Robert Hooke — that made his most celebrated work possible. In 1660 he was among the founders of the Royal Society, whose motto Nullius in verba and program of public, witnessed experiment he embodied perhaps more fully than any other fellow. In 1668 he moved to London to live with his sister Katherine Jones, Lady Ranelagh, herself an accomplished natural philosopher and chymical practitioner, in whose house on Pall Mall he maintained his laboratory until his death a week after hers, in late December 1691. He declined a knighthood and the presidency of the Royal Society, the latter partly because the oath conflicted with his scruples about swearing. A chronic invalid plagued by weak eyes and recurrent illness, he nonetheless sustained an output that few of his contemporaries could match. Michael Hunter's biography Boyle: Between God and Science remains the authoritative account of this devout, valetudinarian, and intensely productive life.

Works and Texts

Boyle was an enormously prolific author whose published corpus fills many volumes, and whose unpublished manuscripts — work-diaries, recipe collections, theological reflections — fill many more. His most famous single work is The Sceptical Chymist (1661), a dialogue in which the principal speaker, Carneades, dismantles the claim of both Aristotelian and Paracelsian chymists that chemical analysis reveals the true elements or principles of bodies. In pneumatics his New Experiments Physico-Mechanicall, Touching the Spring of the Air, and its Effects (1660) reported the experiments with the air-pump from which the pressure-volume relation now called Boyle's Law was later drawn. His The Origine of Formes and Qualities (1666) gave the fullest statement of his corpuscular matter theory, while Some Considerations Touching the Usefulnesse of Experimental Naturall Philosophy (1663) defended the practical and pious value of experiment. Late in life he gathered his religious convictions about science into The Christian Virtuoso (1690), arguing that the diligent study of nature, far from breeding irreligion, disposed the investigator to devotion. His deliberately plain, circumstantial prose — reporting failures alongside successes and refusing to dress conjecture as certainty — became a model for the experimental report and a conscious contrast to the cryptic, allusive style of the alchemical authors he otherwise read so closely.

Across this output Boyle treated chymistry both as a means of investigating the structure of matter and as a productive art. He wrote on the chemistry of colours, on acids and alkalis and the use of vegetable indicators to distinguish them, on mineral waters, on cold, on the saltiness of the sea, and on human blood. In 1680 he published, anonymously and in Latin, an account of an "icy noctiluca" — cold-glowing phosphorus, newly isolated from urine — one of the more spectacular substances handled in his laboratory. Crucially, alongside these public works ran a parallel and largely unpublished body of distinctly alchemical writing. Boyle composed a "Dialogue on Transmutation," published only one frankly alchemical narrative in his lifetime — an account in the Philosophical Transactions of an "anti-elixir," a powder that degraded gold rather than perfecting it — and lobbied successfully in 1689 for the repeal of an old statute against the "multiplication" of gold and silver, so that adepts might practise transmutation without fear of prosecution. Principe's The Aspiring Adept reconstructs the lost "Dialogue on the Transmutation and Melioration of Metals" from surviving fragments.

Operational and Chemical Contribution

What Boyle actually did at the laboratory bench grounds every interpretation of his thought. His was a working chemical operation: furnaces of several kinds, glass retorts and receivers for Distillation, crucibles for the assaying and melting of metals, and the celebrated air-pump, an expensive and temperamental machine built by Hooke that could partially evacuate a glass receiver so that the behaviour of flames, animals, barometers, and sounding bells could be observed in the rarefied air. With it Boyle showed that a candle was extinguished and a bird suffocated in the exhausted vessel, that sound did not carry through the void, and that the mercury in a Torricellian tube fell as the air was withdrawn — results that he marshalled to argue for the "spring," or elasticity, of the air and against the doctrine that nature abhors a vacuum.

On the chemical side his operations were those of the assayer and the iatrochemist. He worked extensively with mineral acids — what his era called the corrosive spirits of nitre, salt, and vitriol — observing how they dissolved metals, how the resulting solutions changed colour, and how alkalis precipitated them again. He systematised the use of plant-derived colour indicators, noting that syrup of violets turned green with alkalis and red with acids, giving an operational test for distinguishing the two classes of substance. He examined the products of Calcination, the residues of combustion, and the fixed and volatile fractions yielded by destructive Distillation of organic matter, using such results in The Sceptical Chymist to argue that what fire separates out of a body need not have pre-existed in it as an element. The hazards were real and routine: corrosive acids, the toxic fumes of mercury and antimony, the explosive potential of niter mixtures, and the eye-straining labour of attending furnaces at heat for days.

The recovery of what Boyle did at his most secret bench is itself a triumph of method. Principe has shown that several of Boyle's published "experiments" can be reconstructed and repeated, and in collaboration with Newman he undertook the actual laboratory reproduction of historical chymical procedures — heating regulus of antimony with silver and mercury to produce the branching, tree-like "Philosophical Tree" (the arbor Dianae) that adepts read as a sign of the active, growing principle they sought. This experimental historiography demonstrated that the recipes in Starkey's and Boyle's notebooks were not idle fantasy but described real, reproducible material phenomena, however mistaken the chrysopoetic interpretation placed upon them. Boyle's most secret operations were attempts at metallic transmutation of just this kind, including the careful melting of a "philosophical mercury" — a specially prepared, supposedly animated quicksilver — with gold, watched for the gentle heating, the swelling, and the change of colour that adepts read as evidence of an active principle at work. It must be stressed, in keeping with the standards of modern scholarship, that no such operation ever transmuted a base metal into gold; the phenomena Boyle observed have natural explanations in the alloying and amalgamation behaviour of antimony, mercury, and the noble metals, and the interpretive framework that read them as steps toward the Philosopher's Stone was mistaken.

The Sceptical Chymist and the Corpuscular Philosophy

The argument of The Sceptical Chymist is narrower and more careful than its later reputation suggests. Boyle's spokesman Carneades does not deny that chymistry is valuable, nor that bodies are composed of some ultimate constituents; he denies that the standard analytical procedures of the chymists — chiefly the action of fire — can demonstrate what those constituents are. Against the Aristotelians he argues that the four elements of earth, water, air, and fire are not shown to be the true components of mixed bodies. Against the followers of Paracelsus (Theophrastus Bombastus von Hohenheim) he argues, with equal force, that the Tria Prima (Three Principles) of salt, sulphur, and mercury cannot be established as universal principles, since fire often creates apparent "principles" rather than liberating pre-existing ones, and since the number of fractions one obtains depends on the violence and manner of the analysis. The book is thus a critique of careless inference within chymistry, written by a committed chymist, not a repudiation of the art from outside it.

In place of elements and principles Boyle offered a corpuscular matter theory, indebted to the revived Epicurean atomism of Pierre Gassendi and to the mechanical philosophy of Descartes, though Boyle was studiously cautious about the deepest metaphysical questions. On this view there is one universal matter, divided into minute particles, or corpuscles, which combine into stable clusters; the size, shape, motion, and arrangement of these corpuscles determine the sensible qualities and chemical behaviour of bodies. Importantly, this framework was not hostile to Transmutation. If the difference between one metal and another lay only in the texture and arrangement of corpuscles, then a sufficiently powerful agent that could break down and reorganise that texture might in principle turn a base metal into gold. Far from refuting Chrysopoeia (Gold-Making), Boyle's corpuscularianism furnished it with a mechanical rationale, a point that William Newman has pressed in arguing that corpuscular thinking was itself nourished by the alchemical tradition's long experience of bodies dissolved and recomposed.

Relationships and Collaborators

Boyle's most consequential chymical relationship was with the New England-born adept George Starkey (1628–1665), who under the alchemical pseudonym Eirenaeus Philalethes wrote some of the most widely read and technically sophisticated transmutational treatises of the age. Newman's biography of Starkey, Gehennical Fire, and the joint work of Newman and Principe have shown that when Starkey arrived in London around 1650 he became, in effect, Boyle's tutor in serious laboratory alchemy, supplying recipes, theory, and the all-important "philosophical mercury" said to be prepared from antimony. Boyle's early notebooks are saturated with Starkey's instruction. The relationship illustrates how the supposed reformer of chemistry was schooled by an avowed seeker of the Philosopher's Stone.

Among his contemporaries, Boyle drew on the iatrochemistry of the Flemish physician Jan van Helmont, whose emphasis on water as a universal material and on chemically active "ferments" left deep marks on his thought, and whose Helmontian program Starkey transmitted to him in concrete laboratory form. The legacy of Paracelsus (Theophrastus Bombastus von Hohenheim) reached Boyle in this mediated, critical fashion: he absorbed the chymical medicine and the conviction that fire-analysis revealed nature's workings, while rejecting the dogmatic doctrine of the Tria Prima (Three Principles) and the obscurantism of Paracelsian rhetoric. He sparred at a distance with the philosopher Thomas Hobbes, who ridiculed the air-pump experiments and the whole project of basing natural philosophy on artificial, witnessed trials — a dispute made famous by Steven Shapin and Simon Schaffer's Leviathan and the Air-Pump as a clash over what should count as a "matter of fact." He knew and corresponded with Kenelm Digby, a fellow gentleman-virtuoso with chymical and occult interests, and his work was admired by Francis Bacon's heirs in the Royal Society, whose program of cooperative, useful, fact-gathering natural history Boyle did much to realise. At the bench his indispensable collaborator was Robert Hooke, who engineered the air-pump, and in his household his sister Lady Ranelagh was an intellectual partner and herself a practitioner of chymical medicine. The young Isaac Newton inherited and extended Boyle's corpuscular matter theory and shared his absorption in alchemy, the two men exchanging guarded letters on the dangers of disclosing transmutational secrets.

Transmission and Reception

Boyle's published works circulated rapidly across Europe, many translated into Latin for an international readership, and his reputation as the model of the cautious, pious, fact-respecting experimenter became part of the Royal Society's self-image and a template for later natural philosophers. His corpuscular theory of matter and qualities passed to Newton and, through the eighteenth century's textbooks, into the mainstream of chemical thought; his law of gases entered the permanent technical canon. The continental chymist Wilhelm Homberg, working in Paris, engaged closely with Boylean experiment and matter theory, one channel by which Boyle's approach shaped the Académie des Sciences. In his will Boyle endowed the lectures that still bear his name, the Boyle Lectures, intended to defend Christianity against atheism — a measure of how thoroughly he saw experimental philosophy and religion as allies.

The alchemical Boyle, by contrast, was largely invisible to posterity for two and a half centuries, partly by his own design and partly by that of later editors. He shrouded his most sensitive transmutational results in dispersed manuscripts and elaborate ciphers, and the eighteenth- and nineteenth-century image of a sober founder of chemistry had no place for an adept who melted gold with animated mercury in search of the stone. Only with the recovery and cataloguing of the vast Boyle archive at the Royal Society, and its sustained study from the 1980s onward, did this second Boyle return to view, forcing a rewriting of the standard story of how chemistry separated from alchemy.

Scholarly Debates and Modern Historiography

For much of the twentieth century, historians such as Marie Boas Hall treated The Sceptical Chymist as a decisive break, the moment at which a recognisably modern chemistry threw off alchemical superstition; Boyle was cast as the discipline's liberator. The decisive revision came from Lawrence M. Principe, whose The Aspiring Adept (1998) used Boyle's own manuscripts to show that he pursued Chrysopoeia (Gold-Making) in earnest throughout his life, believed on several occasions that he had witnessed or nearly achieved Projection, maintained a network of secretive alchemical informants whom he treated as serious experimental sources, and lobbied to legalise the practice of transmutation. Principe argued that earlier readings had projected a much later disciplinary boundary back onto a man who did not recognise it. With William R. Newman he developed the broader thesis, set out in Alchemy Tried in the Fire and in Newman's Atoms and Alchemy, that "alchemy" and "chemistry" were not two enterprises in Boyle's century but one, best named by the period term Chymistry, and that alchemical laboratory practice actively contributed to the corpuscular matter theory usually credited to the mechanical philosophers. Newman and Principe accordingly urge that the modern analyst's categories — distinguishing a rational "chemistry" from an irrational "alchemy" — must not be read back into the language and self-understanding of seventeenth-century practitioners.

Other scholars have refined rather than overturned this picture. Antonio Clericuzio, in Elements, Principles, and Corpuscles, situates Boyle's matter theory within Continental debates and stresses the role of chemically active principles, qualifying any purely mechanical reading of his corpuscularianism. Michael Hunter has edited the correspondence and work-diaries and provided the documentary foundation on which all interpretation now rests, while also tracing Boyle's piety, casuistry, and ill health. Shapin and Schaffer, approaching from the sociology of knowledge, read the air-pump controversy with Hobbes as the construction of new conventions for producing and certifying experimental facts, locating Boyle's authority in social practices rather than in method alone — an emphasis that Principe and Newman, more concerned with what Boyle actually believed and did at the bench, have treated with some reserve. The consensus that has emerged is that Boyle dissolves the comfortable opposition between alchemy and chemistry: he was at once a reformer of chymical rhetoric and method and a devoted seeker of the philosophers' stone, and the historiographical task is to hold both truths together without anachronism, and without implying that any metal was ever genuinely transmuted.
