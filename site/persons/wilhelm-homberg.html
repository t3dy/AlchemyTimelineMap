Wilhelm Homberg

Role: CHEMIST | Era: MEDIEVAL

Wilhelm Homberg (1652–1715) was a Dutch-German chymist and physician who became the leading experimental chemist of the Académie royale des sciences in Paris during the reign of Louis XIV, a cosmopolitan figure whose laboratory work on metals, light, and the principles of matter made him one of the most influential practitioners of his generation. Born in colonial Batavia and trained across half of Europe, he ended his career as first physician to Philippe II, Duke of Orléans, the future regent of France, in whose lavishly equipped laboratory at the Palais-Royal he pursued a research program that modern scholarship — above all that of Lawrence M. Principe — has shown to be, at its core, a sustained and serious attempt at Chrysopoeia (Gold-Making), the making of gold. Homberg thus stands as a pivotal case for understanding how the boundary between alchemy and chemistry was not a wall but a continuum in the decades around 1700.

Life and historical context

Homberg was born in 1652 in Batavia, the capital of the Dutch East Indies (modern Jakarta), where his father, a soldier and administrator originally from Saxony, served the Dutch East India Company. The family returned to Europe during Homberg's childhood, settling at Amsterdam, and the young Homberg was sent to study law at the Lutheran academies, taking a degree in jurisprudence and briefly practicing as an advocate at Magdeburg. Law did not hold him. Like many curious men of the later seventeenth century, he was drawn into the new experimental natural philosophy, and he abandoned his legal career to undertake one of the most extensive educational itineraries of any chymist of the age.

Over roughly a decade Homberg moved from city to city, attaching himself to the leading experimentalists of Europe. At Magdeburg he studied with Otto von Guericke, the inventor of the air pump and famous for the Magdeburg hemispheres; he took medical instruction in Italy, England, and the Low Countries; he learned mineralogy and assaying in the mines of Hungary and Saxony, where the practical metallurgy of the smelters gave him a grounding in the behavior of metals that few academic chemists possessed. He took a medical doctorate, reportedly at Wittenberg, and absorbed the chymical traditions of central Europe at first hand. This restless apprenticeship gave Homberg a rare double competence: he was both a learned physician versed in the textual tradition and a skilled operator who had worked at the furnace and the assay balance.

In the 1680s Homberg arrived in Paris, where his evident skill won him the attention of the chemist and academician who recommended him to the Académie royale des sciences. He was admitted to the Académie in 1691, converting to Catholicism to do so, and from that point Paris became his home. In 1704 he entered the service of the Duke of Orléans, who built him a magnificent private laboratory and acquired for it a celebrated large burning lens. Homberg married late and died in Paris in 1715, the same year as Louis XIV, having spent his final decade as the most richly supported chemist in France.

Works and texts written

Homberg's published output appeared almost entirely in the annual Mémoires and Histoire of the Académie royale des sciences, the principal venue for French savants of his day. Across some three decades he contributed dozens of papers reporting individual investigations: on the production of what he called sal sedativum (now recognized as boric acid), on the analysis of plants and salts, on the behavior of acids and alkalis, on phosphorus, and on the action of the burning lens upon metals and minerals. These memoirs, taken together, constitute his real corpus, and they were read across Europe through the official Academy volumes and their reprints.

Most importantly, Homberg attempted to gather his chemical doctrine into a connected system in a sequence of memoirs he titled the Essais de chimie ("Essays of Chemistry"), published in the Academy's Mémoires in the first decade of the eighteenth century. The Essais were programmatic. They set out Homberg's account of the chemical principles — the small number of basic substances into which all bodies could be resolved and from which they were composed — and they organized his experimental findings around that theory. He treated salt, sulphur, and mercury in the manner of the chymical tradition, but reworked them in light of his own laboratory results and of the corpuscular natural philosophy he had absorbed from Robert Boyle and others. The Essais de chimie are the closest Homberg came to a textbook, and they are the document in which his theory of the sulphurous principle and of light as a material agent is most fully laid out.

The architecture of the Essais repays close attention, because it shows how Homberg fused a traditional doctrine of principles with the new corpuscularism. The opening essay proposed a roster of basic principles — salt, sulphur, mercury, together with water and earth as passive matrices — but Homberg insisted that these were not the abstract qualities of the scholastic chymists but real, isolable, ponderable substances with characteristic corpuscular shapes that governed how they combined. He thus stood at a deliberate distance from the Tria Prima (Three Principles) of the Paracelsian Alchemy tradition even as he retained its vocabulary, the kind of fine terminological negotiation the project's actor/analyst distinction is meant to track: Homberg used the inherited words salt, sulphur, and mercury while quietly reconceiving what they named. Successive essays then took each principle in turn, the sulphur essay being the pivot on which his theory of light and his hopes for the recomposition of metals both turned. Read as a unit, the Essais are less a finished system than a research program in print, repeatedly revised across editions of the Mémoires as Homberg's lens experiments forced him to amend his account of what sulphur was and where it came from.

Beyond the printed memoirs, Homberg left a body of laboratory records and correspondence. Crucially, much of his most ambitious work — the explicitly gold-making research he pursued in the Orléans laboratory — was never published in the form he gave it in manuscript. The full extent of his chrysopoetic program survives in archival notebooks and in the unpublished or heavily edited drafts that Principe has reconstructed, and it differs significantly in emphasis from the more cautious public face Homberg presented in the Academy's official record. Principe has shown that Homberg even prepared a late, expanded version of the Essais in which the chrysopoetic implications were far more openly drawn than in anything that reached print, so that the published corpus and the manuscript corpus together form two registers of a single project — one calibrated for the Academy's public, the other for the prince's private laboratory and for Homberg himself.

Operational and chemical contribution

Homberg's distinction lay in his hands as much as in his theories. His central instrument in his later years was the great burning lens, a convex glass roughly a meter across that focused sunlight to a point capable of melting and volatilizing substances that no ordinary furnace could touch. The instrument that came to the Orléans laboratory was the celebrated Tschirnhaus lens, ground by the Saxon savant Ehrenfried Walther von Tschirnhaus and reckoned among the largest and finest in Europe; mounted on an adjustable frame and trained on the sun, it concentrated a beam intense enough to fuse refractory earths and to ignite or vaporize materials in seconds. With it Homberg subjected gold, silver, antimony, tin, copper, lead, and a range of ores and minerals to the most intense heat then available, observing methodically how each fumed, calcined, vitrified, melted, or changed weight, and recording the residues and sublimates the beam threw off. The lens mattered because it removed a standing objection: ordinary furnace fire was contaminated by the fuel and its smoke, whereas focused sunlight acted, Homberg argued, as a pure agent, so that any change it wrought in a metal could be attributed to the light itself rather than to soot or to the air of the hearth.

The metallic experiments were the heart of the program. Homberg reported that gold, supposedly the most fixed and incorruptible of metals, could under prolonged focus be driven into a faint vapor and made to lose a little substance, and that other metals could be calcined into earthy chaux that, weighed carefully, sometimes proved heavier than the metal from which they came. He took the volatilization of gold as evidence that even gold was a compound body, decomposable into more fundamental principles rather than an unalterable element, and the weight gains of the calces as evidence that something material had entered the metal during the operation. Both observations pointed the same way: if gold could be taken apart and if a material principle could be added to a metal, then in principle gold could be built up by recomposition. This was the operational basis for his theory of matter and for his hope, pursued in earnest at the Palais-Royal, of recomposing gold from its parts.

The crucial interpretive move concerned light. Homberg argued that the focused sunlight of the burning lens was not merely heat but a material substance that combined chemically with the bodies it acted upon. He identified this luminous matter with the sulphurous principle — the principle of combustibility, color, and oily inflammability long recognized in the Sulfur-Mercury Theory of the chymical tradition. In his mature view, the sulphur principle of the chymists simply was the matter of light, a real, weighable substance disseminated through the sun's rays, and the burning lens was the instrument that captured it and forced it into the bodies it irradiated. The weight gains he observed when metals were calcined under the focus confirmed for him that corporeal light-sulphur was being fixed into them, fattening the metal with the very principle that gave gold its color, lustre, and incorruptibility. On this reasoning, gold differed from the baser metals chiefly in carrying a greater and more perfectly fixed charge of this luminous sulphur; to ennoble a base metal was, in effect, to drive enough sun-sulphur into it under the lens. This identification of light with the active sulphurous principle of metals is the most original and most discussed element of his natural philosophy, and it is the conceptual hinge that joined his respectable optics and his outright Chrysopoeia (Gold-Making).

The theory was, by any modern measure, mistaken — there is no luminous sulphur, and the weight gains Homberg recorded are now understood as the uptake of oxygen during combustion — but it was a disciplined, testable physical hypothesis rather than a mystical gesture, and that is precisely what makes it historically significant. Homberg reasoned from measured weights and reproducible operations to a material cause, exactly as a corpuscularian was supposed to do. The same focused beam that grounded his metallic chrysopoeia also produced one of the durable curiosities of his bench: the intense heat fused alumina-rich clays into a hard, needle-bearing glassy mass, a vitrification phenomenon connected to the mineral now called Mullite, illustrating how Homberg's lens generated reproducible material novelties alongside his contested theory.

Homberg's bench work ranged far beyond the lens. His preparation of sal sedativum (boric acid) from the mineral later understood to contain boron was a genuine and reproducible discovery in salt chemistry. He worked extensively with phosphorus, with the analysis of acids and alkalis by their mutual neutralization, with the distillation of plant matter, and with the assay of metallic ores using the dry methods he had learned in the mines. The hazards were real: the burning lens could blind an inattentive operator and ignited combustibles instantly; phosphorus burned the skin; the volatilized fumes of antimony and arsenic compounds were poisonous. Homberg's program drew on Distillation, Calcination, and the manipulation of the Sulfur-Mercury Theory of metallic composition, deploying the full Operational Chemistry repertoire of the chymical laboratory in the service of a systematic chemistry.

Relationships and collaborators

Homberg's career was made by patronage and by institutional membership. His decisive patron was Philippe II, Duke of Orléans, nephew of Louis XIV and after 1715 regent of France during the minority of Louis XV. Orléans was himself an enthusiastic amateur chemist who worked alongside Homberg in the Palais-Royal laboratory, and his protection gave Homberg both the resources — the great burning lens, an extensive apparatus, ample materials — and the social cover to pursue research that might otherwise have drawn suspicion. The relationship was unusually close: the prince was as much collaborator as sponsor.

Within the Académie royale des sciences, Homberg worked among the leading French savants of the age, including the secretary Bernard le Bovier de Fontenelle, whose éloges and editorial framing shaped how Homberg's work reached the public. The astronomer and physicist Christiaan Huygens, the most eminent natural philosopher associated with the early Academy, belonged to the same Parisian scientific milieu, and Homberg's corpuscularism drew on the same mechanical philosophy that animated Huygens's circle. Homberg's intellectual debts ran above all to Boyle, whose corpuscular chymistry and insistence on careful experiment Homberg had encountered during his travels in England and whose program of resolving the chymical principles into corpuscular structures Homberg extended.

Homberg trained and influenced the next generation of French chemists. Among those who passed through or were shaped by his orbit were the younger academicians who would carry French chemistry forward into the era of Étienne-François Geoffroy, whose famous table of chemical affinities owed much to the experimental culture Homberg helped establish. His doctrine of principles and his experimental style remained reference points in the Iatrochemistry and operational chemistry of early eighteenth-century France, and his memoirs were studied by the systematizers who followed him.

Transmission and reception

Because Homberg published through the Académie royale des sciences, his work circulated with the authority and reach of the most prestigious scientific institution in Europe. The Academy's Mémoires were reprinted, translated in part, and read from London to Saint Petersburg, so that Homberg's findings on salts, on phosphorus, and on the burning lens entered the common stock of European chemical knowledge. His sal sedativum became a standard preparation, and his lens experiments were cited in the long eighteenth-century debate over whether metals gained or lost a material principle upon calcination — a debate that fed directly into the phlogiston theory developed by Georg Ernst Stahl and, ultimately, into the oxygen chemistry of Antoine Lavoisier that overturned it.

Yet the reception of Homberg was sharply selective. His careful, quantitative bench chemistry was embraced and built upon, while the larger speculative and chrysopoetic framework within which he had conceived that chemistry was quietly set aside by his successors and editors. Fontenelle's official éloge and the Academy's editing presented Homberg as a sober experimentalist, downplaying the gold-making ambitions that had in fact organized his most intense efforts. By the later eighteenth century, as the word "alchemy" hardened into a term of contempt, Homberg was remembered as a chemist who happened to hold some outdated theories, and his serious engagement with transmutation was either forgotten or treated as an embarrassment to be explained away. This filtering shaped the historiographical record for nearly three centuries and made the recovery of the real Homberg a genuine scholarly achievement.

It must be stated plainly, in keeping with the historical record, that Homberg did not succeed in making gold; no Transmutation of base metal into gold has ever occurred, and the Philosopher's Stone was never real. What the sources document is the seriousness and sophistication of his belief that such recomposition was possible, and the experimental program he built upon that belief.

Scholarly debates and modern historiography

The modern understanding of Homberg is overwhelmingly the work of Lawrence M. Principe, whose archival research transformed a minor figure in the prehistory of the Chemical Revolution into a central case study in the continuity between alchemy and chemistry. Working through Homberg's unpublished notebooks and the suppressed portions of his Essais de chimie, Principe demonstrated that Homberg's burning-lens experiments and his theory of the light-sulphur principle were not incidental curiosities but the scaffolding of a deliberate program to decompose and recompose metals — in short, a program of chrysopoeia conducted at the heart of the Académie royale des sciences and under the patronage of a prince of France. Principe's reconstruction shows that what later editors presented as cautious chemistry was, in Homberg's own conception, the rational pursuit of the goal that the alchemical tradition called the making of gold.

Principe's case rests on close textual and material work rather than on speculation. By collating the printed Mémoires against Homberg's surviving drafts and correspondence, he traced where the Academy's editorial apparatus — chiefly under Fontenelle — softened or excised the transmutational language, and he showed that the so-called sulphur principle of the published essays functions, in the manuscripts, explicitly as the agent of gold-making. He has also tied Homberg's program to its patron, arguing that the Orléans laboratory was conceived in significant part as a chrysopoetic workshop, and that the prince's personal participation gave the project both its means and its discretion. Importantly, Principe is careful to keep the actor's framework and the analyst's verdict apart: he reconstructs what Homberg believed and attempted without ever suggesting that the attempt could have worked. It must be stated plainly that no Transmutation occurred, that the Philosopher's Stone was never real, and that the weight gains Homberg read as captured light-sulphur have a mundane chemical explanation; the historical achievement lies in recovering the seriousness of the program, not in vindicating its goal.

This finding is methodologically important. Principe and his frequent collaborator William R. Newman have argued, against an older historiography that drew a bright line between "superstitious" alchemy and "scientific" chemistry, that the two were a single enterprise — for which they revive the period term Chymistry — and that figures such as George Starkey, Robert Boyle, Isaac Newton, and Homberg all moved fluidly between transmutational and analytical aims. The Homberg case is among the strongest pieces of evidence for this revisionist thesis, because it locates a fully serious chrysopoetic program not in a marginal adept but in the official scientific establishment of the most powerful state in Europe, at the very threshold of the eighteenth century. Newman's broader work on corpuscular matter theory, including his study Atoms and Alchemy: Chymistry and the Transformation of Matter in the Early Modern Period, situates Homberg's account of decomposable gold within the long tradition of alchemical atomism that fed the mechanical philosophy, and helps explain why a corpuscularian's commitment to taking bodies apart and reassembling them made the recomposition of gold seem not an occult fantasy but a reasonable extrapolation from accepted matter theory.

The historiographical reorientation that makes Homberg legible belongs to a wider movement. Scholars including Wouter J. Hanegraaff have traced how the very category of "alchemy" was constructed and stigmatized by Enlightenment polemicists, distorting the record so that practitioners like Homberg were retrospectively cleansed of their transmutational concerns — a stigmatization that, on Hanegraaff's account, is itself a historical artifact to be analyzed rather than a neutral description of the past. Historians of practice such as Pamela H. Smith, in her work on Artisanal Epistemology and The Body of the Artisan: Art and Experience in the Scientific Revolution, have emphasized the hands-on, material character of early modern science that Homberg's lens work exemplifies, an emphasis congenial to the Material Culture Approach now central to the history of chymistry. Brian P. Copenhaver and others have illuminated the philosophical traditions of principles and occult qualities on which his theory of light-sulphur drew, while Michela Pereira and a generation of historians of medieval and early modern alchemy have shown how deep the intellectual lineage of such matter theory ran. The current scholarly consensus, following Principe, is that Homberg cannot be understood as a chemist who incidentally dabbled in alchemy; he was a chymist for whom the analysis of matter and the project of gold-making were one and the same investigation, and his case has become a touchstone for how historians now narrate the emergence of modern chemistry from its chymical matrix.
