Al-Ghazali

Role: PHILOSOPHER | Era: MEDIEVAL

Al-Ghazali (1058–1111), known to medieval Latin readers as "Algazel," was a Persian jurist, Ash'arite theologian, and self-described seeker who became the most influential intellectual figure of the classical Sunni world. Born at Tus in Khurasan and active in Nishapur, Baghdad, Jerusalem, Damascus, and again Tus, he rose to occupy the most prestigious teaching chair of the Seljuk empire before abandoning it for a decade of ascetic withdrawal, then returned to teaching at the end of his life. He did not practise alchemy and wrote no metallurgical treatise, yet his work matters acutely to the history of the discipline for two reasons. First, his great spiritual handbook bears the title Kimiya-yi Saʿadat, "The Alchemy of Happiness," and so stands as the most famous medieval instance of alchemy used as a religious metaphor rather than a description of the workshop. Second, his philosophical theology reshaped how educated Muslims could understand natural causation, divine omnipotence, and the legitimacy of investigating the order God placed in matter — the very questions practising alchemists had to answer. The actor's vocabulary of "alchemy" in his title and the analyst's category of laboratory chrysopoeia must therefore be kept rigorously distinct, a distinction this essay treats as its central caution.

Life and historical context

Abu Hamid Muhammad al-Ghazali was born in 1058 at Tus, near modern Mashhad in north-eastern Iran, into a family of modest means. Orphaned young, he was educated in the Shafiʿi school of law and trained at the Nizamiyya college of Nishapur under al-Juwayni, the leading Ash'arite theologian of the age. The Nizamiyya colleges were the great state-funded institutions of the Seljuk sultanate, founded by the vizier Nizam al-Mulk to consolidate Sunni orthodoxy, and Al-Ghazali's career was bound to this apparatus of patronage. After al-Juwayni's death he entered Nizam al-Mulk's circle and in 1091, at the age of thirty-three, was appointed to the Nizamiyya of Baghdad, the most coveted teaching post in the Islamic east. There he lectured to hundreds of students and produced his polemical and theological masterworks.

In 1095 Al-Ghazali underwent a personal crisis that he describes in his autobiography, the Munqidh min al-Dalal ("Deliverer from Error"). Afflicted by doubt about the foundations of knowledge and by anxiety that his fame and salary had corrupted his motives, he suffered a breakdown that he reports as a physical inability to speak or lecture. The Munqidh recounts how, before this collapse, he had subjected the claims of the senses and even of reason to a searching scepticism, doubting whether any knowledge could be held with absolute certainty, until — by his own account — God restored his confidence not through fresh argument but through a "light" cast into his breast, an experiential certitude he identifies with the path of the Sufis. He left Baghdad on the pretext of pilgrimage, distributed his wealth, and spent roughly a decade in withdrawal — in Damascus, where he is said to have retired to the minaret of the Umayyad mosque, in Jerusalem at the Dome of the Rock, on pilgrimage to Mecca and Medina, and eventually back at Tus — devoting himself to ascetic and contemplative discipline. Frank Griffel has shown that this period was less a total retreat from scholarship than a reorientation toward the inner, experiential dimension of religion that Al-Ghazali had come to regard as the highest form of certainty; it was during and after these years that he composed the Ihyaʾ and its Persian abridgement. Around 1106, under pressure from a Seljuk vizier who appealed to the duty of "reviving religion" at the turn of the Islamic century, he was persuaded to return to teaching at the Nizamiyya of Nishapur, and he died at Tus in 1111. His life thus spans the high Seljuk period, an era of intense competition among the rational theology of the Ash'arites, the Neoplatonized Aristotelianism of the philosophers (falasifa), the esotericism of the Ismaʿili Shiʿa, and the experiential piety of the Sufis — four currents that his writings successively engage and adjudicate.

Works and texts written

Al-Ghazali was extraordinarily prolific, and his works fall into several registers. As a logician and reporter of philosophy he wrote the Maqasid al-Falasifa ("Intentions of the Philosophers"), a neutral exposition of the logic, physics, and metaphysics of the falasifa, principally as systematized by Avicenna. He intended it as a preliminary to refutation, but when it circulated in Latin translation as part of the corpus attributed to "Algazel," European readers frequently mistook its careful summary of Avicennan doctrine for Al-Ghazali's own teaching — an irony that shaped his Western reputation, as Frank Griffel and others have noted.

His polemical masterpiece is the Tahafut al-Falasifa ("The Incoherence of the Philosophers"), completed in 1095, which attacks twenty positions held by the philosophers and declares three of them tantamount to unbelief. Against it Averroes (Ibn Rushd) would later write his Tahafut al-Tahafut ("The Incoherence of the Incoherence"). Al-Ghazali's theological and juristic authority rests above all on the Ihyaʾ ʿUlum al-Din ("The Revival of the Religious Sciences"), an immense forty-book synthesis of law, devotion, ethics, and Sufi spirituality that became one of the most widely read works in the Islamic world. His spiritual autobiography, the Munqidh min al-Dalal, narrates his intellectual itinerary through the four schools.

For the present subject the crucial text is the Kimiya-yi Saʿadat ("The Alchemy of Happiness"), a Persian work he composed after his return from withdrawal as a condensed, accessible vernacular reworking of the themes of the Ihyaʾ for a lay audience. The book is organized around four "elements" of knowledge that mirror the four-part structure of the Ihyaʾ: knowledge of self, knowledge of God, knowledge of this world, and knowledge of the next world. Its title is a deliberate metaphor: just as the alchemist's kimiya — the elixir — was believed to transmute base metal into gold, so these disciplines together constitute a spiritual "elixir" that transmutes the base human soul into something precious. In the introduction Al-Ghazali himself unfolds the conceit, comparing the alchemist who seeks the elixir in the bowels of the earth with the seeker who must look instead within his own heart, and contrasting the worldly alchemy that enriches the body with the higher alchemy that perfects the soul for eternity. None of these are alchemical treatises in the technical sense; none of them appears in the present database as a linkable text, and the operational substances they invoke are figures of speech, not reagents. The book's enormous popularity — it remained a staple of Persian-language piety for centuries — is itself evidence of how naturally a medieval audience accepted alchemical imagery as a vehicle for religious instruction.

Operational and chemical contribution: a metaphor, not a metallurgy

It is essential to state plainly that Al-Ghazali made no operational or chemical contribution whatsoever. He did not distil, calcine, sublime, or assay; he ran no furnace and described no apparatus, no glassware, no acids or mineral spirits. The temptation to enrol him among the alchemists arises entirely from the title of the Kimiya-yi Saʿadat, and resisting that temptation is the single most important interpretive task this essay performs. In the actor's own vocabulary, Al-Ghazali borrows the word kimiya — the same Arabic-Persian term that named the practical art of Transmutation — precisely because his readers already understood what the alchemist claimed to do. The elixir (al-iksir) was thought to convert lead or copper into gold by imparting the perfect proportion and "tincture" of the noble metal; Al-Ghazali transfers that image wholesale to the moral life. The "base metal" is the unreformed soul, mired in appetite; the "gold" is the soul polished by knowledge and devotion until it reflects the divine; the "elixir" is the fourfold knowledge his book sets out to teach. The transmutation he promises is ethical and eschatological, never metallurgical.

The analyst's category of laboratory Chrysopoeia (Gold-Making) — the actual attempt to make gold, with its sal ammoniac, its mercury and sulfur, its aludels and pelicans — belongs to a different world, that of Jabir ibn Hayyan and Al-Razi (Rhazes), whose Corpus Jabirianum and practical recipe-books described genuine operations on genuine substances. Al-Ghazali stands outside that workshop entirely. To read the Kimiya-yi Saʿadat as a chemical text is precisely the kind of category error the actor/analyst distinction is designed to prevent: it conflates a religious writer's metaphor with the technical claims of practitioners he never joined. What Al-Ghazali's usage does demonstrate, valuably, is how thoroughly the imagery of Transmutation had saturated the medieval Islamic imagination — alchemy was so familiar a frame that a theologian could expect a lay readership to grasp salvation through its analogy. The hazards in his book are spiritual, not toxicological; the only "fire" is the purifying discipline of the self.

The critique of the philosophers

Al-Ghazali's most consequential intellectual act was his critique of the falasifa in the Tahafut al-Falasifa. His principal targets were Avicenna (Ibn Sina) and al-Farabi, whose synthesis of Aristotle with Neoplatonism had produced a self-confident metaphysics that Al-Ghazali judged both internally incoherent and, at three points, irreconcilable with revelation. He singled out for the gravest condemnation their claims that the world is eternal and uncreated, that God knows particulars only in a universal way, and that the resurrection of the body is impossible — these three he held to be unbelief. The other seventeen disputed positions he regarded as erroneous but not heretical.

The argument with the deepest bearing on natural philosophy is his analysis of causation, set out in the seventeenth discussion. Against the philosophers' insistence that natural causes act by their own necessary natures — that fire necessarily burns cotton — Al-Ghazali advanced an occasionalist position descended from Ash'arite theology: the only true agent is God, and what we call natural causes are merely the customary occasions upon which God directly produces effects. The conjunction of fire and burning is habitual, not necessary; God could withhold the burning, as the Qur'an reports He did for Abraham in the flames. Michael Marmura's analysis stresses that Al-Ghazali does not simply abolish secondary causation but suspends judgement on its necessity, insisting that the regularity we observe is a divine custom (the Qur'anic sunnat Allah) rather than an autonomous law of nature.

This argument cuts in two directions for the history of alchemy, and scholars disagree about which prevails. On one reading, occasionalism corrodes the very idea of fixed natural kinds and stable causal powers, threatening the alchemist's confidence that a determinate operation yields a determinate result. On another, more widely defended reading, Al-Ghazali's doctrine of divine custom actually licenses empirical inquiry: because God maintains regular patterns in creation, the investigator who observes and repeats can learn those patterns, and because no created nature is metaphysically necessary, even the transformation of one metallic species into another is not ruled out a priori. The point bears directly on a debate that would later preoccupy the alchemical tradition. Avicenna had argued in a famous passage of his Kitab al-Shifaʾ that the alchemists could at best counterfeit the accidents of gold — its colour and weight — but could never alter the specific form that made a metal what it essentially was, so that true Transmutation was impossible to art; this verdict, translated into Latin as the Sciant artifices, became the standard philosophical objection to chrysopoeia in the Latin schools. Against a metaphysics of necessary forms of that kind, Al-Ghazali's insistence that no created nature holds its properties by necessity, only by divine custom, removes the in-principle barrier: if God could withhold the burning of fire, He could equally remit whatever fixes lead as lead. Al-Ghazali drew no such conclusion himself — he had no interest in defending the goldmakers — but his theology, by loosening the necessity of natures, left more conceptual room for the alchemist's hope than the Avicennan metaphysics he attacked. It is one of the quiet ironies of the field that the theologian who wrote no chemistry supplied premises more hospitable to transmutation than the philosopher who is sometimes counted among its theorists.

Relationships and collaborators

Al-Ghazali's intellectual relationships were defined by his teachers, his patrons, and his adversaries on the page rather than by any laboratory circle. His formative teacher was al-Juwayni, the "Imam of the Two Sanctuaries," under whom he absorbed the Ash'arite kalam that structured all his later thought. His decisive patron was the Seljuk vizier Nizam al-Mulk, whose network of Nizamiyya colleges gave Al-Ghazali both his platform and, by his own account, the worldly temptation he later fled; Nizam al-Mulk's assassination in 1092 and the political turmoil that followed form part of the backdrop to Al-Ghazali's crisis.

His most important relationships, however, were polemical and posthumous. Avicenna was his chief philosophical antagonist: Al-Ghazali read him closely enough to expound his system faithfully in the Maqasid before dismantling it in the Tahafut, and Griffel has argued that Al-Ghazali absorbed far more Avicennan natural philosophy and cosmology than his polemic admits, smuggling it into his own theology even while condemning its excesses. Against Al-Ghazali stood the great Andalusian Aristotelian Averroes, who replied a generation later in defence of the philosophers. With Al-Razi (Rhazes) — the physician and practising alchemist Abu Bakr al-Razi (Rhazes), not the later theologian Fakhr al-Din — Al-Ghazali shared no contact, but the contrast is instructive: al-Razi defended both alchemy and a robust naturalism, exactly the confidence in autonomous natural causes that Al-Ghazali's occasionalism was designed to chasten. He likewise targeted the Ismaʿili "Taʿlimites," the esoteric Shiʿa who claimed authoritative instruction from a hidden Imam, in a separate refutation. Toward the Sufis, by contrast, Al-Ghazali moved from critique to embrace, taking the experiential knowledge of figures in the tradition of al-Junayd and al-Muhasibi as the surest path to certainty — the conviction that animates the Ihyaʾ and the Kimiya-yi Saʿadat.

Transmission and reception

Al-Ghazali's afterlife divides sharply between the Islamic and the Latin worlds. In the Islamic east the Ihyaʾ ʿUlum al-Din became a devotional and juristic classic of unrivalled authority, and the Kimiya-yi Saʿadat circulated widely in Persian and in translation as a manual of practical piety. His vindication of Sufism within the framework of Sunni orthodoxy helped legitimate the mystical tradition for centuries, while his critique of the philosophers contributed to the relative decline of pure falsafa in the central Islamic lands — though scholars now reject the older claim that he single-handedly "killed" philosophy, noting that Avicennan thought persisted and even flourished in later kalam, partly through Al-Ghazali's own transmission of it.

In the Latin West, Al-Ghazali entered through Toledo. The Maqasid al-Falasifa was translated in the twelfth century — associated with the circle of Gerard of Cremona and Dominicus Gundissalinus — and circulated as the Logica et philosophia Algazelis. Because Latin readers usually possessed the Maqasid without the Tahafut that it was meant to introduce, "Algazel" was read as a straightforward exponent of Avicennan philosophy rather than its critic, and his name was cited alongside Avicenna and al-Farabi as an authority on the soul, the intellect, and cosmology. Albertus Magnus and Thomas Aquinas both engaged "Algazel" in this guise. The occasionalist analysis of causation that defined the genuine Al-Ghazali, by contrast, reached Latin scholastics chiefly at second hand and was generally resisted: Aquinas defended the reality and dignity of secondary causes precisely against the kind of position Al-Ghazali had advanced. His indirect bearing on Latin alchemy is therefore real but diffuse — part of the broad Arabic philosophical inheritance within which European writers debated whether the species of metals were fixed by necessary forms or open to art, a debate that runs through the alchemical tradition down to the early modern period.

Scholarly debates and modern historiography

Modern scholarship on Al-Ghazali is vast, but the questions most relevant here concern his metaphysics of causation and the proper handling of his alchemical metaphor. The central modern interpretation is Frank Griffel's Al-Ghazali's Philosophical Theology (2009), which overturned the older image of Al-Ghazali as an enemy of reason who destroyed Arabic philosophy. Griffel demonstrates how deeply Avicennan cosmology and natural philosophy are woven into Al-Ghazali's own positions, and reads the occasionalism of the Tahafut not as a blanket denial of secondary causation but as a carefully bounded argument leaving several causal models open. Michael Marmura, whose 1997 translation of the Tahafut al-Falasifa remains standard, likewise emphasized the philosophical seriousness and precision of the seventeenth discussion, arguing that Al-Ghazali leaves room for a "secondary causation" compatible with divine omnipotence rather than abolishing causal connection outright. Richard Frank's studies pressed the opposite case, finding Al-Ghazali far more committed to Avicennan necessitarianism and less to pure Ash'arism than the tradition allowed — a reading Griffel disputes. The debate between these positions directly conditions what one can say about Al-Ghazali and the possibility of Transmutation: a more occasionalist Al-Ghazali makes natural kinds contingent and transmutation conceivable, while a more necessitarian one tightens the boundaries between species.

On the alchemical question proper, historians of chemistry are cautious precisely because Al-Ghazali wrote no chemistry. William R. Newman, whose Atoms and Alchemy (2006) reconstructs the matter-theory debates that underlay claims about Transmutation, situates the Islamic background in the corpuscular and mercury-sulfur theories of practitioners like Jabir ibn Hayyan, not in Al-Ghazali, and treats theological frameworks as resources that alchemists deployed strategically rather than as systematic foundations. Lawrence M. Principe, in his broad histories of the discipline, similarly stresses that the operational and the metaphorical uses of alchemical language must be kept apart — a methodological caution that applies with full force to the Kimiya-yi Saʿadat, whose kimiya is spiritual through and through. Scholars of Western esotericism such as Wouter J. Hanegraaff have warned more generally against retrojecting a unified "spiritual alchemy" onto premodern sources, and Al-Ghazali offers a clean test case: here the spiritual meaning is explicit, authorial, and theological, owing nothing to the laboratory and making no claim about metals. The responsible conclusion is that Al-Ghazali belongs to the history of alchemy not as a practitioner or theorist of the art but as the author of its most celebrated devotional metaphor and as a theologian whose account of causation framed the conditions under which later thinkers could argue about whether the art was possible at all. To claim more — to enrol him among the makers of gold — would be to mistake an actor's borrowed word for an analyst's technical category, the very error his case so usefully exposes.
