Johann Friedrich Helvetius

Role: SCHOLAR | Era: MODERN

Johann Friedrich Helvetius (1630–1709) was a Dutch physician, physician-in-ordinary to the Prince of Orange at The Hague, and medical reformer whose brief encounter with an unknown adept around the turn of 1667 became one of the most celebrated and debated transmutation accounts of the seventeenth century. A confessed sceptic about alchemical chrysopoeia before the event, Helvetius described in his Vitulus Aureus, Quem Mundus Adorat et Orat (Golden Calf, Amsterdam, 1667) how a stranger who identified himself as an adept visited him twice at his home in The Hague; on the second visit, he allowed Helvetius to take a tiny piece of a substance from his locket (described as resembling yellow sulphur) and use it to project upon lead heated in a crucible. The result, according to Helvetius, was about two drams of gold. Helvetius submitted the gold to a silversmith, an assayer, and a goldsmith, all of whom certified it as pure gold of the highest quality. He was then converted from sceptic to convinced believer. The Vitulus Aureus went through numerous editions and translations; the Dutch edition of 1667 was followed by Latin and German versions, and the account was widely read in natural-philosophical circles in England, France, and Germany. Baruch Spinoza (1632–1677) investigated the event shortly after it occurred, writing to his friend Jarig Jelles (Epistola XL, March 1667) that he had spoken to a goldsmith who had assayed Helvetius's gold and had found it genuine; Spinoza himself remained agnostic about the explanation, neither endorsing transmutation nor dismissing it. Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz recorded information about the Helvetius case from secondary sources. Isaac Newton read the Vitulus Aureus and excerpted its account of the projection in his own alchemical notebooks. Mike A. Zuber, in Ambix (2018), uses the Helvetius transmutation as a touchstone event around which several itinerant alchemists of the late seventeenth century — notably Johann Heinrich Vierordt — built their own claims to possess the tincture that the adept had allegedly demonstrated.
