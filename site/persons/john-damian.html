John Damian (Abbot of Tungland)

Role: SCHOLAR | Era: MODERN

John Damian (fl. 1501–1513), known also as 'the French physician' (Franch leich) and later as 'Maister Johne,' was the principal court alchemist and physician to King James IV of Scotland (r. 1488–1513) and one of the most extraordinary figures in the history of early modern alchemy. A foreigner — probably Italian or French in origin — Damian was attracted to the Scottish court where he established an alchemical laboratory in Stirling Castle, sponsored by James IV and documented in remarkable detail in the Accounts of the Lord High Treasurer of Scotland (1501–1513). John Read, in Ambix (1938), draws on these accounts to show that Damian received substantial financial support for his work on the quinta essencia (quintessence), including funds for materials (gold, silver, quicksilver, aqua vitae, alum, cinnabar, antimony), laboratory apparatus (alembics, furnaces, cauldrons), and wages for furnace-keepers and 'ministers.' On 12 March 1503-4, James IV rewarded Damian by appointing him Abbot of Tungland in Galloway, providing him with an ecclesiastical income to sustain his alchemical work — an episode memorably lampooned by the court poet William Dunbar, who had hoped for the appointment himself. In 1507, Damian achieved historical fame of a different kind when, equipped with wings fashioned from feathers, he attempted to fly from the battlements of Stirling Castle to Paris. He fell immediately into a dung heap and broke his thigh bone; he ascribed the failure to the presence of hen feathers in his wings, which, he argued, 'coveted the midden and not the skies' rather than soaring heavenward like eagle feathers. Dunbar celebrated the episode in his sardonic poem The Fenyeit Freir of Tungland. On 8 September 1508, Damian received from James IV 'a licence to pass out of the realm and remain in what place he pleases … during the space of five years,' probably to visit Continental alchemical centres and acquire manuscripts and materials. He returned to Scotland in 1512; the Lord High Treasurer's accounts record continued work on the quintessence at Stirling Castle through 1513. On 9 September 1513, James IV died at the Battle of Flodden, bringing the crown's investment in alchemy at Stirling to an abrupt end.
