George Agricola

Role: SCHOLAR | Era: MEDIEVAL

Georg Bauer, known as Georgius Agricola (1494–1555), was a German physician, humanist, and natural philosopher whose De Re Metallica (1556), published posthumously, provided the most comprehensive and accurate account of sixteenth-century mining, metallurgy, and mineral processing produced up to that date, and whose earlier Bermannus (1530) and De Natura Fossilium (1546) established a systematic Latin vocabulary for minerals and ores. Born in Glauchau (Saxony), he studied medicine at Leipzig, Bologna, and possibly Venice before settling as a physician in Joachimsthal (Jáchymov), the Bohemian mining center, in 1527; he later served as city physician and burgomaster of Chemnitz. Agricola's work is significant to the history of alchemy because it represents the practical, empirical knowledge of miners and metallurgists — the very practitioners whose workshop knowledge contributed to the development of early modern chemistry — rendered in systematic Latin for a learned readership.

De Re Metallica and Practical Chemistry

The De Re Metallica, illustrated with remarkable woodcuts depicting mining operations, furnaces, bellows, and processing equipment, described in detail the practical procedures for mining, smelting, assaying, and refining metals — gold, silver, copper, iron, lead, tin, mercury — and for the chemical processing of ores using fire, water, and reagents including sulfur and mercury. This practical knowledge overlapped extensively with alchemical operations: the same furnaces, crucibles, bellows, and cupellation techniques used by miners and assayers were used in alchemical laboratories. Pamela Smith has argued that this artisanal knowledge was a major epistemological resource for the development of early modern natural philosophy, and Agricola's De Re Metallica is the most thorough surviving record of this knowledge.

Travels and Connections

Georgius Agricola (Georg Bauer, born Glauchau, 1494) followed a humanist's road before a metallurgist's. After studying at the University of Leipzig (1514-1518) under Petrus Mosellanus — who urged the latinizing of his name — and a rectorship at Zwickau, he crossed the Alps around 1523 to take a medical degree at Bologna (and, the sources say only probably, Padua), then joined the Aldine Press in venice (c. 1524-1526), editing Galen. The Italian per-city dates are not firmly fixed in the literature and are given here as best estimates.

The decisive turn came in 1527, when he settled as town physician and apothecary in the silver-mining boomtown of Joachimsthal in the Erzgebirge. There, among miners and smelters, he gathered the firsthand material for Bermannus, sive de re metallica (Froben, Basel, 1530) — a work that carried a commendatory preface from Erasmus, secured through the Joachimsthal schoolmaster Petrus Plateanus. By 1533 he had moved to Chemnitz, where the patronage of Duke Maurice of Saxony brought him a house and four terms as burgomaster, and where he completed his masterwork De re metallica (published posthumously, 1556).

Agricola is notable for his measured skepticism toward Transmutation: in De re metallica he judged the alchemists' claim that base metals could be turned to gold 'dubious', observing that their writings were obscure and that none of the reputed masters had grown rich. This is reported as a historical stance, not an endorsement.

Itinerary

  1. Glauchau 1494–1506 attested
    Born Georg Bauer (latinized Pawer) on 24 March 1494 in the small Saxon town of Glauchau; spent childhood there before entering Latin school around age twelve.
  2. Leipzig 1514–1518 attested
    Matriculated at the University of Leipzig (1514-1518), studying under the humanist Petrus Mosellanus, who encouraged the latinization of his name to Georgius Agricola. Returned to Leipzig 1522-1523 for further (medical) study before traveling to Italy.
  3. Zwickau 1519–1522 attested
    Appointed rector (Lektor) of the Greek school at Zwickau (c. 1519-1522); published a Latin grammar manual (1520) during this humanist phase before resuming study at Leipzig.
  4. Bologna 1523–1524 attested
    Traveled to Italy (c. 1523) and enrolled at the University of Bologna, and probably Padua, to study medicine; earned an M.D. The exact sequence and years among the Italian cities are not precisely fixed in the sources.
  5. Padua 1524 inferred
    Studied (probably) at the University of Padua during his Italian period; sources list Bologna, Padua, and Venice together for roughly three years (c. 1523/24-1526) without firm per-city dates. The Padua attribution is qualified as 'probable' in the literature.
  6. Venice 1524–1526 attested
    Worked in Venice on the editorial staff of the Aldine Press (c. 1524-1526), contributing to the Aldine editions of Galen (and Hippocrates). Returned to Germany in autumn 1526.
  7. Joachimsthal 1527–1533 attested
    Settled in the silver-mining boomtown of Joachimsthal (Sankt Joachimsthal / Jachymov, Bohemia) as town physician and apothecary from spring 1527; married Anna Meyner in autumn 1527. Direct exposure to working mines and smelters here grounded his metallurgical writing, including Bermannus (1530). Sources vary: some give his Joachimsthal post as 1527-1530, with mine-touring before he took up the Chemnitz post in 1533.
  8. Chemnitz 1533–1555 attested
    Moved to Chemnitz in 1533 as town physician (Stadtphysicus); under the patronage of Duke (later Elector) Maurice of Saxony received a house (1543) and was elected burgomaster four times (1546, 1548, 1551, 1553). Completed De re metallica here; died in Chemnitz 21 November 1555 (published posthumously 1556).
