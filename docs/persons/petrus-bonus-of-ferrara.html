Petrus Bonus of Ferrara

Role: ALCHEMIST | Era: MEDIEVAL

Petrus Bonus of Ferrara (fl. 1323–1330) was an Italian physician and alchemist who composed the Pretiosa Margarita Novella (New Pearl of Great Price), one of the most philosophically sophisticated defenses of alchemy written in the Latin medieval tradition. Little is securely known about his life beyond what can be inferred from his treatise, which he completed in Pola (modern Pula, Croatia) and dedicated to patrons whose identity is disputed. His name and title suggest training in medicine and natural philosophy, probably at one of the Italian universities, and the treatise displays thorough acquaintance with Aristotelian natural philosophy, the Arabic alchemical tradition transmitted through Latin translation, and the major Latin alchemical texts of the preceding two centuries.

The Pretiosa Margarita Novella is notable as an extended apologia for alchemy: rather than simply presenting alchemical recipes, Petrus Bonus engages philosophically with the objections raised against alchemy by natural philosophers, theologians, and physicians, and attempts to demonstrate that alchemical transmutation is consistent with Aristotelian principles of natural change and divine creation. He grapples with questions that would continue to occupy natural philosophers through the Renaissance: whether art can replicate or surpass what nature produces; whether the alchemical gold is identical to natural gold; and whether the practitioner requires divine illumination as well as natural knowledge. Lynn Thorndike, in his History of Magic and Experimental Science (1934), treated the Pretiosa Margarita Novella as a representative example of the philosophical ambitions of fourteenth-century Latin alchemy, situating it within the tradition of apologetic texts that sought to legitimate the discipline against scholastic critics. William R. Newman and Lawrence Principe, in Alchemy Tried in the Fire (2002), note that Petrus Bonus represents an important moment in the development of Latin alchemical theory: the attempt to ground transmutation claims in Aristotelian categories of form, matter, and natural process, rather than purely authoritative precedent or empirical report.

The Pretiosa Margarita Novella was printed at Venice in 1546 by Janus Lacinius, whose edition also included introductory material that significantly shaped Renaissance readings of the text. This printed edition circulated widely and influenced subsequent discussions of alchemy's philosophical status among humanist scholars. Petrus Bonus thus stands as a pivotal figure in the transition between the high medieval Latin alchemical tradition and the Renaissance engagement with alchemy as both a practical and a philosophical discipline.
