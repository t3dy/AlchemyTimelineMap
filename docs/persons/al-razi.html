Abu Bakr Muhammad al-Razi

Role: PHYSICIAN | Era: MEDIEVAL

Abu Bakr Muhammad al-Razi (854–925), known in the Latin West as Rhazes, was a Persian physician, natural philosopher, and alchemist whose contributions to practical chemistry and pharmaceutical preparation were internationally recognized. Based primarily in Baghdad and Ray (Rhages), al-Razi combined medical practice with systematic investigation of material transformation, positioning alchemy not as mystical transmutation but as the practical foundation of pharmacy and medicine.

Al-Razi's significance lies in his reorientation of alchemy toward medical and pharmaceutical applications. His surviving treatises document the preparation of medicinal compounds, the extraction of active principles from plants and minerals, and the distillation of spirits for therapeutic use. He described the classification of substances by their properties (hot, cold, dry, moist), the combination of ingredients to counterbalance excess, and the verification of pharmaceutical effectiveness through empirical observation. This pragmatic approach treated alchemy as a discipline essential to healing.

Al-Razi's laboratory knowledge was extensive. He described the preparation of mineral acids (sulfuric, nitric, and hydrochloric), the manufacture of corrosive and dissolving solutions, and the distillation of aromatic essences. His descriptions of apparatus and procedures were precise enough that modern chemists have successfully replicated his operations. His 'Book of the Secret of Secrets' outlined distillation techniques for spirit production and material separation—techniques foundational to later pharmaceutical chemistry.

Al-Razi's intellectual stance diverged from Jabir's theoretical ambition. Rather than proposing universal theories of matter composition, al-Razi focused on observable material properties and their practical applications. His work exemplifies what historians call 'experimental empiricism'—the systematic testing of materials, observation of results, and modification of procedure based on outcomes. This approach aligned alchemy more closely with craft practice and medical empiricism than with natural philosophy.

Al-Razi's medical reputation in the Islamic world was extraordinary. His clinical observations on diseases (particularly smallpox and measles) were medically innovative, and his integration of alchemy into pharmaceutical preparation made him an authority physicians across the Islamic world consulted. His transmission to the Latin West, particularly his medical treatises, established him as an authority European physicians referenced through the medieval and early modern periods. The association of alchemy with medicine became institutionalized partly through al-Razi's model.

Travels and Connections

Abu Bakr Muhammad ibn Zakariya Abu Bakr Muhammad al-Razi (Rhazes, 864/865–925 CE) took his name from his birthplace, ray (Rayy, near modern Tehran), the city to which his career repeatedly returned. As a young man he moved to baghdad to study and practice at its bimaristan; the sources record the move but give no year, so the dating here is approximate. He was later invited back to Rayy by its governor, Mansur ibn Ishaq, to head the local hospital—the patron to whom he dedicated the Kitab al-Mansuri—before being summoned again to Baghdad to direct a new hospital, a phase loosely fixed by association with the caliphs al-Mu'tadid (d. 902) and al-Muktafi (r. 902–908). The precise chronology of these moves is not securely attested.

In the laboratory al-Razi practiced an austerely operational alchemy. His Kitab al-Asrar (Book of Secrets) reads as a working manual: it catalogues raw materials—four volatile “spirits,” the metallic “bodies,” stones, vitriols, borates, and salts—then the furnaces, alembics, and crucibles needed to work them, and finally step-by-step procedures, largely free of the allegory that pervades the earlier Jabir ibn Hayyan corpus on which he drew. He accepted Transmutation as genuinely possible and wrote a refutation (now lost, attested by Ibn al-Nadim) of Al-Kindi's denial of alchemy—though he candidly admitted he had not himself learned how to turn copper into gold.

That confidence in transmutation set him against the later verdict of Avicenna, who a century afterward both mocked al-Razi for overreaching in metaphysics and, in his De congelatione, denied that the species of metals could truly be changed. Through the twelfth-century Latin translations associated with Gerard of Cremona, al-Razi's practical chemistry reached the West as the Liber secretorum Bubacaris, carrying his apparatus and procedures into the medieval European tradition.

    Born in Rayy (modern Rey, near Tehran), from which his nisba al-Razi derives; spent his youth there before relocating to Baghdad. Birth year given as 864 or 865 CE in the sources; year of departure for Baghdad is not attested and is supplied here as an approximate bracket.
  2. Baghdad 885–900 approximate
    Moved to Baghdad in his youth to study and practice at the local bimaristan (hospital). The exact years are not recorded in the sources; this leg is bracketed approximately before his recorded return to Rayy.
  3. Rayy 900–902 approximate
    Invited back to Rayy by Mansur ibn Ishaq (Abu Salih al-Mansur), then governor of Rayy, and appointed head (director) of its bimaristan. He dedicated 'The Book for al-Mansur' (Kitab al-Mansuri) to this patron. The years are not recorded; this return is placed before his Baghdad hospital directorship under al-Mu'tadid (d. 902).
  4. Baghdad 902–915 approximate
    Invited to Baghdad, where he directed a new hospital; sources associate this phase with the caliphs al-Mu'tadid (d. 902) and al-Muktafi (r. 902-908), under whom he was reportedly commissioned to plan a new hospital. Exact dates of his Baghdad directorship are not securely recorded.
  5. Rayy 915–925 approximate
    Spent the last years of his life in his native Rayy, where he reportedly suffered from glaucoma/blindness. Died in Rayy in 925 CE (some sources give 935 CE). The start of this final phase is inferred; the death is attested.
