Thomas Hobbes

Role: PHILOSOPHER | Era: EARLY_MODERN

Thomas Hobbes (1588–1679) is best known as a political philosopher — author of Leviathan (1651), the most influential work of political theory in the English language — and as a materialist natural philosopher who reduced mental events to motions of matter and dismissed incorporeal substances as conceptual confusions. His relationship to alchemy was primarily polemical: his materialism and his dismissal of occult qualities and hidden causes put him in explicit opposition to the natural philosophical frameworks that alchemy depended on.

Materialism and the Critique of Occult Philosophy

Hobbes's exchange with Robert Boyle over the interpretation of Boyle's air-pump experiments — analyzed by Steven Shapin and Simon Schaffer in Leviathan and the Air-Pump (1985) — was partly about the epistemology of experimental knowledge and partly about the politics of natural philosophical authority. Hobbes argued that Boyle's experimental demonstrations proved nothing about the nature of the vacuum (he denied the void on mechanical philosophical grounds); Boyle argued that careful experimental observation could produce reliable knowledge even without complete theoretical understanding. This dispute, which Shapin and Schaffer analyze as a foundational moment in the social construction of scientific knowledge, touched on the same questions about the relationship between theory and practice, and between demonstration and proof, that organized alchemical epistemology.

Hobbes's mechanist materialism — his reduction of all natural phenomena to motion and matter, his elimination of occult qualities, his denial of active principles that were not mechanical — was in tension with the theoretical frameworks of the alchemical tradition. The "active principles" that Isaac Newton sought to identify through his alchemical research, and that Robert Boyle hoped to discover through his chymical inquiries, were precisely the kind of non-mechanical powers that Hobbes's natural philosophy denied. The debate between mechanist and alchemical natural philosophy that Hobbes's work crystallizes was eventually resolved in favor of neither pure mechanism nor pure alchemy but in the synthesis of mathematical mechanics with chemical analysis that produced eighteenth-century physics and chemistry.

Legacy in the History of Science

Hobbes's relevance for the history of alchemy lies primarily in what his natural philosophy excluded. His thoroughgoing materialism — every mental event is a motion of material particles, every causal relation is a mechanical impact — left no room for the "active principles" that Isaac Newton sought in his alchemical research, no room for the "vital spirits" that van Helmontian chymistry invoked, and no room for the sympathies and correspondences that organized Paracelsian natural magic. In this sense, Hobbes represents the limiting case of the mechanical philosophy: a vision of nature from which everything that alchemical natural philosophy valued had been definitively excluded.

The fact that this vision failed to prevail — that Newton's active principles, Boyle's corpuscular chemistry with its unexplained properties, and eventually Lavoisier's oxygen and caloric retained aspects of active powers that Hobbes's strict mechanism denied — illustrates the limits of pure mechanism as a framework for natural philosophy. The history of natural philosophy between Hobbes and Lavoisier can be read as the negotiation of a middle position between pure mechanism (Hobbes) and the animate natural philosophy of the alchemical tradition (van Helmont, Paracelsus), a negotiation that was not resolved in favor of either extreme but in a series of provisional syntheses that eventually produced the disciplinary framework of modern chemistry and physics.

The Mechanist Challenge to Alchemical Natural Philosophy

Hobbes's challenge to the theoretical foundations of alchemy was more fundamental than the specific critiques that had been directed at alchemical practice throughout the medieval and early modern periods. The scholastic critics had questioned whether art could replicate the natural processes of metallic generation; the humanist satirists had attacked the fraudulent practitioners who deceived credulous clients; the early chemists had questioned whether transmutation was experimentally achievable. Hobbes questioned whether the theoretical framework of alchemical natural philosophy — with its vital principles, its occult sympathies, its animate nature — was philosophically coherent at all. His reduction of all causation to motion and matter left no explanatory role for the kinds of active, spiritual, or vital principles that alchemical theory required.

The failure of strict Hobbesian mechanism to prevail in seventeenth-century natural philosophy is historically significant. Isaac Newton's active principles, Robert Boyle's corpuscular properties that could not be reduced to size, shape, and motion, and eventually Lavoisier's caloric (heat as a material substance) all retained elements of explanatory irreducibility that Hobbes's strict mechanism denied. This persistent need for something beyond strict mechanism in natural philosophical explanation is part of what makes the history of the transition from alchemy to chemistry more complex than the standard narrative of progressive rationalization suggests: the concepts that mechanism was supposed to replace did not simply disappear but transformed into new forms that continued to do explanatory work.

Steven Shapin and Simon Schaffer's Leviathan and the Air-Pump (1985) is the foundational text for understanding the Hobbes-Boyle controversy and its significance for the history of scientific knowledge. Their analysis — which situates the dispute within the political and social context of Restoration England and argues that the epistemological norms of experimental science were established through social negotiation rather than purely epistemic merit — has been enormously influential in science studies and in the history of science, though it has also generated substantial critical response. For the history of alchemy, the Hobbes-Boyle controversy is significant because it marks the moment when the epistemological and ontological claims of the chymical tradition were explicitly challenged by a competing natural philosophical framework.

Hobbes's significance for the intellectual history of alchemy is sharpened by the contrast with his near-contemporary Robert Boyle. Both lived through the same period of English history; both were concerned with the nature of matter and causation; both engaged with the new mechanical philosophy. But where Boyle combined mechanical philosophy with alchemical inquiry in a way that William R. Newman and Lawrence M. Principe have shown was genuinely integrated rather than inconsistent, Hobbes's mechanical materialism excluded alchemy as decisively as any contemporary philosopher. The contrast between Boyle and Hobbes thus illustrates the different paths that seventeenth-century natural philosophy could take: toward an integration of mechanist and alchemical approaches (Boyle) or toward the exclusion of everything that mechanism could not explain (Hobbes). That Boyle's integrative approach — and Newton's even more ambitious synthesis — was historically more productive than Hobbes's strict mechanism reflects the degree to which the alchemical tradition's insistence on active principles, vital forces, and qualitative change pointed toward phenomena that mechanism alone could not explain.

The historical irony of Hobbes's position is that his thoroughgoing mechanism was ultimately less scientifically productive than the eclectic approaches of Robert Boyle and Isaac Newton, who retained active principles and non-mechanical powers alongside the mechanical framework. The history of physics from Newton through electromagnetic field theory and quantum mechanics has progressively revealed that the natural world contains phenomena that resist reduction to mechanical impact of particles, exactly the kind of non-mechanical powers that Hobbes had excluded and that the alchemical tradition had always insisted on. This does not validate alchemy's specific claims, but it suggests that Hobbes's exclusion of everything non-mechanical was premature, and that the chymical tradition's insistence on active principles was pointing toward genuine phenomena that a strict mechanism was incapable of explaining.

Shapin and Schaffer's analysis in Leviathan and the Air-Pump marks the Hobbes-Boyle controversy as a foundational moment in the social construction of scientific knowledge, where the epistemological norms of experimental science were established through social negotiation rather than purely epistemic merit. This analysis has been enormously influential in science studies even where its specific conclusions have been contested. For the history of alchemy, the controversy is significant because it marks the moment when the epistemological and ontological claims of the chymical tradition were explicitly challenged by competing natural philosophical frameworks. The outcome was not a simple victory of mechanism over alchemy but the beginning of a long and contested process through which the boundaries of acceptable natural philosophical inquiry were gradually redrawn.
