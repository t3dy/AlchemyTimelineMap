Nicolas Barnaud

Role: ALCHEMIST | Era: EARLY_MODERN

Nicolas Barnaud (ca. 1539 – ca. 1604) was a French physician, religious radical, and alchemical editor whose Quadriga aurifera (Leiden, 1599) published the first substantial Latin text of George Ripley's Liber duodecim portarum (Book of the Twelve Gates) as the second of four alchemical texts in the compilation. Barnaud had met Bernard Penot in Prague, and Barnaud's edition is closely related to Penot's earlier partial edition of 1595, drawing on the same underlying manuscript tradition; both editions excise Ripley's polemical sections on fraudulent alchemists and the 'Admonition' describing his own failed experiments. Jennifer M. Rampling, in 'Transmission and Transmutation' (Early Science and Medicine 17, 2012), demonstrates that Barnaud's text derives not from Ralph Rabbards' 1591 English print edition but from a Latin manuscript that had been in Barnaud's possession for some years.

Barnaud was also a religious dissenter with anti-trinitarian sympathies; Didier Kahn's study ('Between Alchemy and Antitrinitarianism: Nicolas Barnaud,' 2005) documents his position in the intersection of radical religious movements and alchemical practice in late sixteenth-century Europe. His dedication of the Quadriga edition of Ripley to the English courtier and poet Sir Edward Dyer (1543–1607) reflects his Prague connections: both Barnaud and Dyer had dealings with the alchemical milieu of Rudolf II's court, and Dyer had visited Prague repeatedly in 1588–1590 in attempts to secure the knowledge of Edward Kelley. Barnaud's explicitly trans-national preface — claiming personal acquaintance with philosophers in France, England, Germany, Italy, Poland, Bohemia, Prussia, Sweden, and the Dutch Confederacy — exemplifies the rhetorical appeal to the universality of alchemical knowledge that characterises the genre of pan-European alchemical compendia in this period.
