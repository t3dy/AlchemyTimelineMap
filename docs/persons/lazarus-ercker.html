Lazarus Ercker

Role: SCHOLAR | Era: MODERN

Lazarus Ercker (d. 1593) was a German metallurgist and mining administrator in the service of Rudolf II, Holy Roman Emperor, who served as chief superintendent of the mines in the kingdom of Bohemia and authored the most important sixteenth-century treatise on assaying and ore-testing. Ercker worked alongside Rudolf II's other court scholars and specialists, including the astronomers Tycho Brahe and Johannes Kepler, in the environment of Prague's intellectually cosmopolitan court. Erwin F. Lange, in Ambix (1966), identifies Ercker as the third of the major sixteenth-century metallurgists after Biringuccio and Agricola, and notes that his work 'might well be considered an important forerunner of books dealing with chemical quantitative analysis.' His approach was strictly empirical: Ercker declined to discuss alchemical transmutation beyond dismissing 'the cements used by the philosophers and alchemists to change copper to silver and silver to gold' with the terse observation that he would 'leave them for what they were worth' and deal only with 'processes and procedures which had been thoroughly tested and could be relied upon.'

His principal work, Beschreibung Allerfürnemsten Mineralischen Erzt-und Bergwerkarten (Description of the Foremost Mineral Ores and Mining Operations), first published in Prague in the German language, went through multiple editions and translations, with an early English translation by Sir John Pettus in 1683 considered unreliable. The modern scholarly translation by Anneliese G. Sisco and Cyril Stanley Smith, published by the University of Chicago Press in 1951 as Lazarus Ercker's Treatise on Ores and Assaying, made Ercker's work accessible to modern scholars. The work is notable for its practical instructions not only in the assaying of gold, silver, copper, and other metals, but also in the construction of precision instruments including the analytical balance — which Ercker described fashioning from a sword blade — and standardized sets of weights, reflecting the early modern concern with quantitative measurement in metallurgical and chemical practice.
