Dante Alighieri

Role: POET | Era: MEDIEVAL

Dante Alighieri (1265–1321) was a Florentine poet, political theorist, and philosopher whose Commedia (the Divine Comedy) represents the supreme literary achievement of medieval Italian culture and contains several passages relevant to alchemy, metallurgy, and natural philosophy. Born in Florence into a minor noble family, Dante was educated in rhetoric, theology, and natural philosophy; politically active as a White Guelph, he was exiled from Florence in 1302 and spent the remainder of his life in various northern Italian courts. While Dante was not himself an alchemist and the Commedia does not endorse alchemical practice, the poem places alchemists among the falsifiers in Hell (Inferno, Canto XXIX–XXX), provides a literary treatment of alchemical claims, and its broader natural philosophy drew on sources — particularly Aristotle and Avicenna — that were central to the intellectual framework within which alchemy was debated.

Alchemy in the Commedia

In Inferno XXIX–XXX, Dante encounters the falsifiers of metals (alchemists) in the tenth bolgia of the eighth circle of Hell, where they are afflicted with disease as punishment for corrupting matter. The two alchemists named — Griffolino d'Arezzo and Capocchio da Siena — were historical figures known in Dante's time; Capocchio is described as one who "counterfeited metals with good art," placing him alongside fraudulent metalworkers and counterfeiters as those who corrupt the natural order through deceptive imitation. This positioning reflects the dominant medieval theological view that alchemy, if fraudulent, was a form of deception and counterfeiting; it does not necessarily represent Dante's view that all alchemy was fraudulent, but it engages with the cultural association between alchemical practice and fraud that was pervasive in the period.
