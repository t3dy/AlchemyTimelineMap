Cesare Cremonini

Role: SCHOLAR | Era: EARLY_MODERN

Cesare Cremonini (1550, Cento – 19 July 1631, Padua) was an Italian Aristotelian philosopher and professor of natural philosophy at the University of Ferrara (from 1578) and then at the University of Padua (from 1591 until his death), where he was a colleague and adversary of Galileo Galilei. He is renowned as one of the last and most consistent champions of strict Aristotelianism in the Italian universities, maintaining the Aristotelian framework against both Galileo's mathematical natural philosophy and the new Baconian empiricism.

Cremonini is perhaps the most famous anecdote-figure in early modern philosophy: he allegedly refused to look through Galileo's telescope on the grounds that it could not be trusted as a philosophical instrument, though modern historians have qualified this story. His In quartum librum Aristotelis Meteorologicorum (manuscript, Padua, Biblioteca Universitaria), discussed by Craig Martin in 'Alchemy and the Renaissance Commentary Tradition on Meteorologica IV' (Ambix 51:3, 2004), reveals his position on alchemy: he was sceptical of transmutation on Aristotelian grounds, arguing that just as offspring can only be created by parents of the same species, so metals can only generate metals of their own kind. His rejection of chrysopoeia was thus based on Aristotelian reproductive biology rather than on chemical observation. Cremonini faced repeated accusations of impiety and heterodoxy before the Inquisition throughout his career, but the Venetian Republic protected him from prosecution as a valuable teacher.
