Anna Maria Zieglerin

Role: SCHOLAR | Era: MODERN

Anna Maria Zieglerin (d. 7 February 1575), known as Anna Zieglerin, was a female alchemist active at the court of Duke Julius of Braunschweig-Wolfenbüttel between 1571 and 1575, and is one of the most fully documented women practitioners in early modern European alchemy. Almost nothing certain is known of her origins: according to her husband, Heinrich Schombach, Anna was born by emergency caesarean section after her mother fell through ice and died; instead of a wet nurse, Anna was reportedly 'nourished entirely with a tincture and was specially purified thereby.' Whether or not these stories reflect actual events, they provided Anna with a narrative of alchemical purification from birth that she associated with her own inability to menstruate — a fact that she and her companion Philipp Sömmering explicitly compared to the purity of the Virgin Mary. Anna came to Wolfenbüttel in 1571 with her husband and the alchemist Philipp Sömmering, having previously been associated with the disaster of the Grumbach Feud at Gotha, in which Duke Johann Friedrich II had been persuaded by a peasant boy (Hänschen) claiming angelic inspiration to rebel against the Holy Roman Emperor, with catastrophic results. In Wolfenbüttel, Anna initially worked as an assistant to Sömmering and her husband. On 1 April 1573 she submitted to Duke Julius a twenty-page manuscript of alchemical recipes, including those for the production of a substance she called 'the lion's blood' — a Paracelsian tincture with claimed agricultural, medical, and alchemical powers. The lion's blood was, in Anna's framework, both an alchemical elixir and an eschatological substance: it would redeem metals 'just as Christ's blood redeemed sinners,' and produce the purified children who would usher in the Last Days. Tara Nummedal, in her monograph Anna Zieglerin and the Lion's Blood: Alchemy and End Times in Reformation Germany (University of Pennsylvania Press, 2019), demonstrated that Anna's sophisticated integration of Paracelsian alchemy with Lutheran apocalypticism and Marian imagery constituted a coherent theological and alchemical vision, not mere fraud or delusion. By spring 1574 the court's favor had turned against her; she was accused of sorcery, adultery with Sömmering, fraud, theft, and poisoning. Torture led to confessions and guilty verdicts. On 7 February 1575 Anna was executed at Wolfenbüttel; the first written record of the event, by Heinrich Bünting in 1584, describes her being 'burned to death as a sorceress.'
