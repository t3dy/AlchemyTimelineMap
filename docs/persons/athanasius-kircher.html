Athanasius Kircher SJ

Role: SCHOLAR | Era: EARLY_MODERN

Athanasius Kircher SJ (1602–1680) was a German Jesuit polymath and prolific author, based at the Collegio Romano in Rome from 1634 until his death, whose encyclopedic writings across geology, acoustics, Egyptology, optics, and medicine made him one of the most famous learned men of seventeenth-century Europe. Martha Baldwin, in her study of alchemy in the Society of Jesus (Ambix, 1993), identifies Kircher's published work as evidence of 'an active group of learned men pursuing alchemical projects in the laboratory of the Jesuit Collegio Romano' in the second half of the seventeenth century. Kircher's engagement with alchemy appears in his enormous two-volume Mundus Subterraneus (Amsterdam, 1665), where Baldwin notes he published 'a diatribe on alchemy' placed within a broader encyclopedic discussion of vulcanology, ocean currents, hydraulics, geology, metallurgy, and toxicology. This placement within a much larger work is characteristic of the pattern Baldwin identifies among Jesuit alchemical writers: 'Hoping to present their alchemical studies as part of much broader works on natural philosophy suggests that these Jesuits seemed acutely uncomfortable with being known as alchemists.'

Kircher collaborated with the German Jesuit Gaspar Schott (1608–1666), who 'later transported his interest to Germany when his superiors reassigned him' and who made 'frequent references to alchemical projects, both his own and those of his correspondents' in his published writings. Kircher's Collegio Romano laboratory served as a hub for European correspondence networks in the natural sciences and in alchemy, with Schott transmitting information about Kircher's experiments and conclusions to a broad readership through his own encyclopedic publications. Beyond alchemy, Kircher was centrally responsible for the development of European Egyptology through his (ultimately erroneous) attempts to decode Egyptian hieroglyphics, and his many publications exercised enormous influence on learned culture across Europe in the mid-seventeenth century.
