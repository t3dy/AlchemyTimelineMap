Samuel Hartlib

Role: SCHOLAR | Era: EARLY_MODERN

Samuel Hartlib (c. 1600–1662) was a Prussian-born English educational reformer, intelligencer, and patron of natural philosophy who served as the central node of a vast correspondence network connecting natural philosophers, alchemists, chemical physicians, and reformers across England and Europe in the middle decades of the seventeenth century. Betty Jo Dobbs, in her study of Kenelm Digby and alchemy (Ambix, 1973), describes Hartlib as 'primarily interested in educational and religious reform but was perhaps more socially and practically oriented than many reformers,' who 'undertook to be a sort of communications centre for the collection and dissemination of information of all sorts' and attempted unsuccessfully to institutionalize this role as a state-supported Office of Address. He entered England as a student in 1625 and by the 1640s had built up a correspondence that included men of every intellectual persuasion; his daybook (the 'Ephemerides,' 1634–1660) records chemical references as early as 1640. Among his close chemical associates were Robert Boyle, George Starkey, Frederick Clodius (who married Hartlib's daughter and set up a laboratory in Hartlib's back kitchen), and Sir Kenelm Digby. Hartlib maintained contact with Continental chemical developments through the chemist-divine Johann Moriaen in Holland.

Hartlib's circle hoped that Boyle and Starkey would achieve major breakthroughs in chemical medicine and alchemy through the 1640s and early 1650s, but Hartlib's faith in Starkey declined sharply by 1653–4, while his hopes transferred to Frederick Clodius and later Digby. Hartlib corresponded with the Royal Society's first Secretary, Henry Oldenburg, who was the son-in-law of Hartlib's co-worker John Dury and who after Hartlib's death converted Hartlib's correspondence model into the public format of the Philosophical Transactions (1665). Hartlib's extensive papers — which include inventories of Digby's Gresham College laboratory, records of alchemical processes, correspondence with Boyle, and a daybook tracking developments in chemistry, alchemy, husbandry, and educational reform — are preserved at Sheffield University.
