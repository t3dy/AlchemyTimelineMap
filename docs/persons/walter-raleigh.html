Sir Walter Raleigh

Role: PATRON | Era: EARLY_MODERN

Sir Walter Raleigh (c. 1552–1618) was an English courtier, soldier, colonial adventurer, historian, and natural philosopher whose chemical investigations, particularly during his imprisonment in the Tower of London (1603–1616), made him one of the most famous lay practitioners of alchemy and iatrochemistry in early modern England. P. M. Rattansi, in his study of alchemy and natural magic in Raleigh's History of the World (Ambix, 1966), reconstructs Raleigh's chemical activities from contemporary sources: Sir William Wade, lieutenant of the Tower, reported to Robert Cecil in 1605 that Raleigh had converted 'a little hen-house in the garden into a still-house; and here he doth spend his time all day in distillations.' The Diary of the Rev. John Ward (1661) records that Ward met a Mr. Sampson who had served as 'operator to Sir Walter Raleigh twelve years, whilst he was in the Tower' — an account that attests both the sustained duration of Raleigh's chemical work and the institutional character of his laboratory with its dedicated assistants.

Raleigh's most lasting chemical legacy was 'Raleigh's Cordial,' a complex pharmaceutical preparation whose recipe circulated throughout the seventeenth century. John Evelyn recorded in 1662 accompanying King Charles II to the laboratory of the royal chemist Nicolas Le Febvre to witness 'his accurate preparation of Sir Walter Raleigh's Cordial'; Le Febvre published a Discours sur le Grand Cordial de Sr. Walter Rawleigh in 1665, describing its preparation and virtues. Raleigh's chemical interests were not incidental to his broader intellectual life: his enormous History of the World (1614), composed during his imprisonment, explicitly connected chemical methods of analysis — 'as a skilful and learned Chymist can as well by separation of visible elements draw helpful medicines out of poison, as poison out of the most helpful herbs and plants' — to his historical method of separating truth from legendary accretion. Rattansi analyzes the History as a document of Raleigh's engagement with Hermeticism, neo-Platonic natural magic, and the alchemical tradition, demonstrating that his imprisonment transformed Raleigh into one of the most intellectually ambitious natural philosophers of the Jacobean period.

Raleigh was released from the Tower in 1616 for an expedition to South America in search of the legendary gold of El Dorado; when the expedition failed and an attack on Spanish settlements violated treaty terms, he was executed in 1618 under his original sentence from 1603. His chemical work in the Tower thus represents the most sustained phase of his natural-philosophical activity, conducted under conditions of enforced leisure that gave him time for systematic experimentation and historical writing.
