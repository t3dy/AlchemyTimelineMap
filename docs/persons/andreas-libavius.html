Andreas Libavius

Role: CHEMIST | Era: EARLY_MODERN

Andreas Libavius (ca. 1555–25 July 1616) was a German physician, chemist, and schoolmaster whose Alchemia (Frankfurt, 1597) is widely regarded as the first systematic chemistry textbook in the European tradition. Born in Halle, Saxony, Libavius studied at Wittenberg and Jena, subsequently taking appointments as a schoolmaster and district physician before serving from 1607 until his death as rector of the Gymnasium Casimirianum in Coburg. Lawrence M. Principe and William R. Newman have emphasised his significance as a chymist who sought to systematise chemical practice as a craft discipline, separating it from the more speculative and mystical currents of Paracelsian medicine and alchemical philosophy, while nonetheless engaging critically with both.

The Alchemia organised chemical practice into two principal branches — encheria (operations performed by hand, covering laboratory techniques, apparatus, and manipulations) and chymia (the theoretical and practical preparation of medicines, metals, and stones) — providing what Bruce T. Moran describes as 'the most comprehensive survey of practical chemical knowledge available at the close of the sixteenth century.' Libavius was also a prolific polemicist: he attacked Paracelsian medicine in his Rerum chymicarum epistola (1595), criticised the Rosicrucian manifestos as dangerous enthusiasm, and engaged the anti-alchemical arguments of Thomas Erastus (1524–1583). His commentary on George Ripley's Liber duodecim portarum, the 'Analysis duodecim portarum', written as a systematic explication of the Twelve Gates, drew on the Latin editions of Bernard Penot (1595) and Nicolas Barnaud (1599). Jennifer M. Rampling, in 'Transmission and Transmutation' (Early Science and Medicine 17, 2012), notes that Libavius was unaware of Ripley's prologue and 'Admonition' sections — which had been excised by Penot and Barnaud — and his Analysis accordingly reflects this incomplete textual tradition.
