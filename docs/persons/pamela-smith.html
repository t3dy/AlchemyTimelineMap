Pamela H. Smith

Role: SCHOLAR | Era: MODERN

Pamela H. Smith is Seth Low Professor of History at Columbia University and founding director of the Centre for Science and Society, whose research on artisanal knowledge, material culture, and the relationship between craft practice and natural philosophy in early modern Europe has fundamentally shaped the historiography of the Scientific Revolution and the history of alchemy and chemistry. Her major work The Body of the Artisan: Art and Experience in the Scientific Revolution (University of Chicago Press, 2004) argued that the practical, embodied knowledge of craftsmen and artisans — metallurgists, painters, goldsmiths, potters — was not merely background to but constitutive of the new natural philosophy of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. Her more recent "Making and Knowing Project" at Columbia has developed these ideas through a digital edition and reconstruction of the anonymous French manuscript Ms. Fr. 640, a late sixteenth-century workshop manual.

Central Thesis

Smith's central argument is that the epistemological claims of early modern natural philosophy — that knowledge must be grounded in direct engagement with the material world, in observation and experiment — were not invented by university-trained philosophers but were already embedded in the practices and self-understanding of craftsmen. Artisans had always claimed that their knowledge was "real" knowledge, derived from hands-on engagement with materials rather than from books; what changed in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries was that natural philosophers began to claim this artisanal epistemology for themselves, creating a new genre of natural knowledge that combined observational empiricism with literary and institutional authority. This argument positions Artisanal Epistemology as a central concept in the history of science.

Smith's approach draws on the concept of tacit knowledge — knowledge embedded in practice and embodied skill that cannot be fully articulated in language — to analyze how artisans understood and transmitted their craft expertise. Her analysis of sixteenth-century German artist-craftsmen, particularly those working in metallurgy and casting, shows how their engagement with the material properties of metals, pigments, and mineral substances generated a form of natural knowledge that was simultaneously practical and theoretical, operational and philosophical. This artisanal epistemology, she argues, was a major resource for the new natural philosophy of the Scientific Revolution.

Key Works

The Business of Alchemy: Science and Culture in the Holy Roman Empire (Princeton University Press, 1994) examined the intersection of alchemy, patronage, and politics at the court of Rudolf II and in the Holy Roman Empire more broadly. The Body of the Artisan (2004) developed the artisanal epistemology thesis. The Making and Knowing Project has produced a digital critical edition of Ms. Fr. 640, providing access to this unique source document for artisanal knowledge in the sixteenth century.

Artisanal Knowledge and the Body of the Artisan

Smith's The Body of the Artisan: Art and Experience in the Scientific Revolution (2004) develops an argument about how practical craft knowledge — the knowledge of metalworkers, apothecaries, painters, potters, printers — contributed to the development of early modern natural philosophy. The "body of the artisan" in her title refers both to the artisan's physical body as the primary instrument of craft inquiry (skilled hands, calibrated senses, embodied judgment) and to the corpus of practical knowledge accumulated through generations of workshop practice. Smith's argument is that this knowledge was not simply absorbed passively by natural philosophers but actively challenged them: the recalcitrance of matter in the workshop — the way gold refused to be extracted from ore exactly as theory predicted, the way colors shifted in the kiln in unexpected ways — forced investigators to revise theoretical accounts of material properties.

Her research on Ms. Fr. 640, a sixteenth-century French manuscript of workshop recipes and craft techniques (the "Book of Secrets from the Workshop of a French Artist"), has been developed into a major collaborative digital humanities project at Harvard and MIT: Secrets of Craft and Nature in Renaissance France. The project combines scholarly transcription, translation, and annotation with laboratory reconstruction of the manuscript's recipes — the same kind of "lab and library" methodology that Lawrence M. Principe has applied to alchemical texts. The results demonstrate that the manuscript's recipes are operationally coherent — they actually work — and that the author was a sophisticated observer of material processes who had developed systematic knowledge of pigments, molds, metalworking, and natural history through direct experimental inquiry.

Smith's broader contribution to the historiography of early modern science lies in her insistence that the boundary between "science" and "craft" — between theoretical natural philosophy and practical workshop knowledge — was permeable and contested in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries in ways that standard accounts of the Scientific Revolution have obscured. Her approach converges with William R. Newman and Principe's revision of the alchemy-chemistry boundary: both arguments locate early modern natural knowledge production in laboratory and workshop settings where theory and practice were inseparable, and both require attending to sources (laboratory notebooks, recipe manuscripts, workshop records) that fall outside the canonical texts of the Scientific Revolution narrative.

Material Culture and the Senses

Smith's contribution to the history of alchemy lies in her insistence that the material culture of the workshop — the specific tools, materials, and physical environments in which early modern artisans worked — was a form of knowledge, not merely a context for knowledge. Her concept of "artisanal epistemology" (distinct from but related to Pamela O. Long's "openness, secrecy, authorship" framework) argues that what artisans knew was embodied in their skilled practice and their sensory attunement to materials, not merely in the recipes and formulas they could articulate. This creates interpretive challenges for historians working from texts alone: the most important knowledge may be precisely what was not written down because it could not be adequately expressed in words.

The Making and Knowing project (the Ms. Fr. 640 reconstruction) has demonstrated how much can be recovered through reconstructive experiment even when written records are incomplete. The project's multi-disciplinary approach — bringing together historians of science, archaeologists, materials scientists, and practicing artisans — models a collaborative methodology that parallels Lawrence M. Principe's laboratory history of alchemy. The convergence of these two programs — both emphasizing material practice as the site of historical knowledge production — represents a significant reorientation of the history of early modern science toward embodied, sensory, workshop-based inquiry.

Smith's broader scholarship includes work on the history of the body, on gender and natural knowledge, and on the relationship between artistic and scientific practice in early modern Europe. Her edited volumes and collaborative projects have been as significant as her individual monographs in shaping the field. Her approach to material culture — treating objects, workshops, and the physical traces of practice as primary historical evidence — has influenced a generation of historians of science who now routinely incorporate material analysis alongside textual analysis in their research. The convergence of her work with William R. Newman and Lawrence M. Principe's laboratory history of alchemy represents a broader methodological shift in the history of early modern science toward "hands-on" historical inquiry.
