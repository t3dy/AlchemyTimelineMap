Nicolas Guibert

Role: PHYSICIAN | Era: EARLY_MODERN

Nicolas Guibert (ca. 1547–ca. 1620) was a physician from Lorraine whose Alchymia ratione et experientia ita demum viriliter impugnata (Strasbourg, 1603) constituted one of the most sustained anti-alchemical treatises of the early seventeenth century. Writing after forty years of unsuccessful alchemical practice, Guibert attacked alchemy on both philosophical and textual grounds: he challenged the legitimacy of the foundational alchemical authors (arguing that Hermes Trismegistus and Aristotle had not written the texts attributed to them, that the works of Albertus Magnus and Thomas Aquinas were spurious, and that Raymond Lull had been deluded by demons); he denied the practical possibility of metallic transmutation; and he sought to unmask the entire tradition as a compound of fraud, delusion, and false attribution.

Guibert's attack prompted immediate and extensive rebuttal: Andreas Libavius responded in 1604 and again in 1615; the Dutch alchemist Ewald von Hoghelande published a defence in 1604; and Bernard Penot responded in 1608. As late as 1649, Ludwig Combach's preface to the first collected edition of Ripley's works was in part a belated response to Guibert — partly because Guibert's criticisms had been revived by Guerner Rolfinck (1641) and Hermann Conring (1648). Jennifer M. Rampling, in 'Transmission and Transmutation' (Early Science and Medicine 17, 2012, pp.493-495), traces this apologetic thread through the continental Ripley tradition, showing how editors and defenders of alchemy repeatedly assembled the authority of 'international adepts' to counter Guibert's accusation that the alchemical tradition was built on false attribution. Bruce T. Moran discusses the Libavius–Guibert exchange at length in Andreas Libavius and the Transformation of Alchemy (Sagamore Beach, MA, 2007), and William R. Newman analyses Guibert's arguments in Atoms and Alchemy (Chicago, 2006).
