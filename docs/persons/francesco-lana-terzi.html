Francesco Lana Terzi SJ

Role: SCHOLAR | Era: EARLY_MODERN

Francesco Lana Terzi (1631, Brescia – 22 February 1687, Brescia) was an Italian Jesuit natural philosopher and encyclopedist, celebrated for his speculative designs for a lighter-than-air flying ship (an airship supported by evacuated copper spheres, described in his Prodromo of 1670) and for his ambitious attempt to compile an encyclopedic synthesis of natural philosophy and experiment in the Magisterium Artis et Naturae (2 vols., Brescia, 1684–86, with a third volume published posthumously).

Lana Terzi studied with Athanasius Kircher at the Collegio Romano in Rome from 1652 to 1654, where he was introduced to Kircher's laboratory-based natural philosophy and alchemical experiments. After teaching rhetoric and grammar at Jesuit gymnasia in Terni and Parma, he returned to his native Brescia in 1665, where he remained for the rest of his life pursuing physical, optical, and alchemical experiments. He taught mathematics at the University of Ferrara and the Jesuit college there before returning permanently to Brescia. He founded a local scientific society, the Accademia dei Filesotici, modelled on the Royal Society of London, and oversaw the publication of its journal, the Actae novae academiae philexeticorum naturae et artis, though both society and journal survived only briefly beyond his death in 1687. He corresponded with Leibniz, who published his letters in the Acta Eruditorum Lipsiae, and with the Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society.

Martha Baldwin, in 'Alchemy and the Society of Jesus in the Seventeenth Century' (Ambix 40:2, 1993), documents Lana Terzi's sustained alchemical interests. He distinguished between alchemy and chemistry while accepting the three Hermetic principles of salt, sulphur, and mercury alongside the four Aristotelian elements. His Prodromo and Magisterium included recipes for chemical and medical preparations, and alchemical projects — including plant palingenesis experiments — engaged him throughout his career. His eclectic, Baconian approach to natural philosophy, combining Aristotelian, Hermetic, corpuscular, and magnetic concepts, typifies the intellectual syncretism of seventeenth-century Jesuit natural science.
