Kleopatra the Alchemist

Role: SCHOLAR | Era: MODERN

Kleopatra the Alchemist (fl. ca. 3rd–4th century CE) was one of the earliest female practitioners identified in the Greek alchemical corpus and the reputed author of the Chrysopoeia of Kleopatra (Gold-Making of Kleopatra) and the Dialogue of Kleopatra and the Philosophers. She should not be confused with the historical Cleopatra VII, queen of Egypt (69–30 BCE); F. Sherwood Taylor, in the founding article of Ambix (1937), identifies her as one of a group of early female alchemists — alongside Maria the Jewess, Theosebeia the sister of Zosimos, and Paphnutia the Virgin — active in Alexandria and Egypt in the first three centuries of the Common Era.

The Chrysopoeia of Kleopatra is among the oldest surviving Greek alchemical texts and consists principally of a page of symbolic drawings transmitted in the Greek alchemical manuscript Marcianus 299 and its derivatives. Its central symbols — the Ouroboros (the self-devouring serpent) inscribed with hen to pan (The All is One), and symbols for gold, silver, and mercury — have been the subject of extensive scholarly commentary. The Dialogue of Kleopatra and the Philosophers is a more substantial mystical-alchemical text in which Kleopatra is interrogated by Ostanes and other philosophers about the nature of the sacred art. Taylor identifies this text as among the most important sources for the mystical-regenerative symbolism of Greek alchemy: it describes the 'blessed waters' visiting 'the corpses lying in Hades,' the 'medicine of Life' rousing them from sleep, and the cloud of vapors rising from the sea — imagery that simultaneously describes alchemical distillation and spiritual resurrection. The attribution of the Chrysopoeia and the Dialogue to a single historical author named Kleopatra cannot be verified, but Taylor regards both texts as representative of an early stratum of Greek alchemy that predates Zosimos of Panopolis (fl. ca. 300 CE), to whom they were known. The Chrysopoeia is preserved in the Greek alchemical manuscript tradition edited by Berthelot, Collection des Alchimistes Grecs (Paris, 1888).
