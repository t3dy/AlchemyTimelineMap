Cleopatra the Alchemist

Role: ALCHEMIST | Era: LATE_ANTIQUE

Cleopatra the Alchemist is a legendary female alchemist associated with the earliest stratum of Greek alchemical tradition, not to be confused with the Egyptian queen Cleopatra VII. She is known primarily through two extraordinary documents preserved in the Greek alchemical corpus: a philosophical dialogue on the nature of gold-making, and the "Chrysopoeia of Cleopatra" (Gold-Making of Cleopatra), one of the most visually remarkable documents in the entire alchemical tradition. These texts, likely dating to the third or fourth century CE, place Cleopatra among the small group of female practitioners — alongside Mary the Prophetess and Theosebeia — who appear in the earliest Greek alchemical sources.

The Chrysopoeia of Cleopatra

The "Chrysopoeia of Cleopatra" (preserved in a tenth-century Byzantine manuscript) is one of the most extraordinary documents in the history of early alchemy. It consists of a series of allegorical images and inscriptions that encode alchemical principles: a serpent eating its tail (the ouroboros, symbol of the cyclical, self-sufficient nature of the alchemical work), a series of concentric circles representing the stages of the work, and the Greek inscription "hen to pan" (one the all), which became one of the most cited Hermetic aphorisms. The images are accompanied by brief enigmatic texts that resist straightforward interpretation but clearly encode a sophisticated alchemical philosophy.

Garth Fowden's analysis in The Egyptian Hermes situates the Chrysopoeia within the context of late antique Egyptian religious and philosophical culture, noting its connections to Hermetic philosophy, Gnostic cosmology, and the material culture of gold-working in the Greco-Egyptian milieu. The ouroboros, which became one of the most pervasive symbols in the subsequent Western alchemical tradition, appears here in one of its earliest datable occurrences, and its specific alchemical significance — the cyclical return of matter to its primary state and its regeneration in a new form — is closely tied to the Chrysopoeia's account of the alchemical process.

The Philosophical Dialogue

A second text attributed to Cleopatra is a dialogue between "the philosophers" (represented as a group seeking the secret of gold-making) and Cleopatra herself, who reveals the principles of the art in characteristically allegorical language. The dialogue form was common in late antique philosophical literature and was used in alchemical texts to frame the transmission of esoteric knowledge from a master to disciples: the dialogue represents the encounter between those who seek alchemical wisdom and one who possesses it.

The attribution of alchemical wisdom to a female authority figure — Cleopatra, Mary, Theosebeia — in the earliest Greek alchemical tradition is a historiographically significant feature that has attracted scholarly attention. Karin Figala and other scholars have noted that the association of female figures with alchemical wisdom reflects broader patterns in late antique religious culture in which women could occupy authoritative roles as prophets, mystics, and repositories of esoteric knowledge, and that the specific female alchemists of the Greek corpus were likely historical figures rather than purely legendary constructions, though their precise biographies are irrecoverable.

Legacy and Transmission

Cleopatra the Alchemist's texts were transmitted as part of the Greek alchemical corpus compiled and preserved in Byzantine manuscripts, the same corpus that includes works by Zosimos of Panopolis, Olympiodorus, and other early Greek alchemists. The Chrysopoeia's imagery — particularly the ouroboros — was widely reproduced and adapted in subsequent alchemical literature, and it became one of the most recognized visual symbols of the Western alchemical tradition. The Theatrum Chemicum anthology and other early modern collections of alchemical texts drew on the Greek corpus, ensuring that the imagery attributed to Cleopatra circulated among learned readers across Europe. Modern scholarship on the Chrysopoeia, including the work of Miriam Meijer and more recently Matteo Martelli, has focused on establishing the text's historical context and analyzing its complex visual and textual symbolism.

Women in Early Greek Alchemy

The presence of female figures — Cleopatra, Mary the Prophetess, Theosebeia, and others — in the earliest stratum of Greek alchemical texts has attracted considerable scholarly attention as evidence of women's participation in late antique intellectual and practical culture. While the specific historical details of these figures' lives and work are largely irrecoverable, their prominent roles in foundational alchemical texts suggests that women occupied genuine intellectual and practical roles in the Alexandrian milieu within which Greek alchemy developed. Scholars including Karin Figala and, more recently, Matteo Martelli have analyzed the literary and textual dimensions of these female figures' roles, distinguishing between the legendary and possibly historical dimensions of their representation.

The association of female figures with alchemical wisdom in the Greek corpus reflects broader patterns in late antique religious culture in which women could occupy authoritative roles as prophets, mystics, and repositories of esoteric knowledge. The Gnostic and Neoplatonist traditions within which Greek alchemy was embedded both accorded significant roles to female wisdom figures — Sophia, the divine feminine principle, played a central role in Gnostic cosmology — and the association of alchemical wisdom with female figures like Cleopatra can be read as a specific instantiation of this broader cultural pattern.

The ouroboros image from the Chrysopoeia of Cleopatra has had an extraordinary afterlife in the history of alchemy, occultism, and modern popular culture. As a symbol of cyclical self-sufficiency — the serpent eating its own tail represents the self-contained, self-renewing nature of the alchemical process, which requires no external input once properly initiated — it became one of the most widely recognized visual emblems of alchemical thought. Its modern prevalence in tattoo culture, graphic design, and popular occultism reflects how profoundly the imagery of early Greek alchemy has permeated Western visual culture, even when its alchemical origins are no longer recognized. Historians of visual culture including Alexander Roob have traced this transmission, situating the ouroboros within the longer history of alchemical imagery from late antiquity through the present.

Cleopatra and the Ouroboros Tradition

The ouroboros image in the Chrysopoeia of Cleopatra represents one of the earliest appearances of this symbol in an explicitly alchemical context, and its subsequent development in the Western alchemical tradition illustrates how visual imagery can carry intellectual content across centuries and across disciplinary boundaries. The symbol's core meaning in the alchemical context — the cyclical self-sufficiency of the alchemical work, its return to beginning, the identity of end and origin — is expressed in the Greek inscription "hen to pan" (one the all) that accompanies the image, and this philosophical claim about the unity of the alchemical process became one of the most frequently cited aphorisms in the tradition.

The gender politics of attributing alchemical wisdom to female figures in the early Greek tradition has been discussed by scholars including Hannah Marcus and Barbara Obrist. The prominent role of women as alchemical authorities in the earliest stratum of the tradition — Cleopatra, Mary the Prophetess, Theosebeia — stands in interesting contrast to the predominantly male authorship of the Arabic and Latin alchemical traditions that followed. Whether this contrast reflects genuine female participation in late antique Alexandrian alchemical practice, a specifically late antique cultural pattern that attributed certain forms of practical wisdom to female figures, or some combination of these factors, has been debated by scholars who acknowledge the difficulty of recovering historical reality from mythologized texts.

The Chrysopoeia's significance for the history of alchemy extends beyond its specific content to its status as a document of the intersection between visual culture and natural philosophical inquiry in late antiquity. The use of images — rather than purely verbal description — to communicate alchemical knowledge reflects the late antique conviction that some forms of knowledge could not be conveyed in words alone, that certain truths required visual representation to become fully intelligible. This conviction became a defining feature of the Western alchemical tradition through the emblematic alchemy of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, where the most sophisticated practitioners communicated through the combination of image, word, and music rather than through any single medium alone.
