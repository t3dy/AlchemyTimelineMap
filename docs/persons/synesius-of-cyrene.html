Synesius of Cyrene

Role: PHILOSOPHER | Era: LATE_ANTIQUE

Synesius of Cyrene (ca. 373–414 CE) was a Neoplatonist philosopher, rhetorician, and bishop of Ptolemais who occupies a distinctive place in the history of Greek alchemy as the author of a brief but significant commentary on the Physika kai Mystika attributed to Pseudo-Democritus. Born in Cyrene in the Libyan Pentapolis to a family of ancient lineage, Synesius studied in Alexandria under the philosopher Hypatia and absorbed the full breadth of late antique Platonic learning, including interest in the physical and mystical sciences.

His alchemical commentary, transmitted under the title On the Book of the Democritean Philosopher to Dioscorus, takes the form of an explanatory dialogue addressed to a student named Dioscorus. Synesius engages the cryptic technical vocabulary of Pseudo-Democritus and attempts to clarify the operations of tincturing, colouring, and the preparation of the divine water central to Hellenistic chrysopoeia. F. Sherwood Taylor, in his foundational article on the origins of Greek alchemy in Ambix (1937), identifies Synesius as one of the principal transmitters of the Pseudo-Democritean tradition, noting the text's importance for understanding how practical chemical operations were embedded within a Platonic-mystical framework. Taylor argues that commentators like Synesius demonstrate the fusion of artisanal practice and Neoplatonic theory that characterizes the Graeco-Egyptian alchemical corpus.

Synesius is better known in general history as a prolific letter-writer, an ambivalent convert to Christianity, and an occasional political figure who defended his native region against Berber raids. His election as bishop of Ptolemais in 410 CE required him to hold certain Christian doctrines conditionally. H. J. Sheppard, in his study of Gnosticism and alchemy (Ambix, 1957), situates commentators like Synesius within the larger context of late antique syncretism, in which Platonic, Gnostic, and craft-technical traditions interpenetrated in the intellectual milieu of Alexandria. The alchemical commentary thus reflects the broader cultural environment in which alchemy took shape as a learned discipline with both practical and speculative dimensions.

Modern scholars treat Synesius's commentary with caution as a source for practical alchemical operations: the text is brief, often obscure, and the attribution to the historical bishop is not universally accepted. Nevertheless, it remains one of the relatively few named commentaries on the Pseudo-Democritean corpus and provides important evidence for how literate late antique readers received and interpreted the earliest stratum of alchemical writing.
