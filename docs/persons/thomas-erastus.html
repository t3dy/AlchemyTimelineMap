Thomas Erastus

Role: PHYSICIAN | Era: EARLY_MODERN

Thomas Erastus (7 September 1524 – 31 December 1583), born Thomas Lieber or Liebler in Baden, Switzerland, was a Swiss physician, theologian, and professor of medicine at Heidelberg (1558–1580) and later Basel, whose four-part Disputationum de medicina nova Philippi Paracelsi (Basel, 1571–1573) mounted one of the most systematic early attacks on both Paracelsian medicine and alchemical transmutation. His argument against chrysopoeia — the making of gold — and argyropoeia — the making of silver — was grounded in Aristotelian natural philosophy: metallic species, he contended, were defined by their substantial forms, which art could not reproduce or transmute. The Explicatio quaestionis (in Disputationum, part I, 1571) directly denied the possibility of transmutation, arguing that even if alchemists could produce a substance indistinguishable from gold in its sensible properties, it would not be 'true and natural gold.'

Erastus's attack provoked a substantial apologetic literature from defenders of alchemy, most notably the response of Gaston DuClo (Claveus) in the Apologia argyropoeiae et chrysopoeiae adversus Thomam Erastum (Nevers, 1590), re-edited by Bernard Penot in 1598. The controversy helped define the intellectual terms of debate over the legitimacy of alchemy for several decades. Jennifer M. Rampling, in 'Transmission and Transmutation' (Early Science and Medicine 17, 2012), notes that continental editors of English alchemical texts such as George Ripley implicitly positioned their compilations as witnesses to the truth of alchemy against Erastus's critique, mobilising the accumulated authority of international adepts for apologetic ends. William R. Newman discusses the Erastus controversy in Atoms and Alchemy (Chicago, 2006) as a key episode in the epistemology of early modern chemistry. Erastus's name later became associated with political doctrines of ecclesiastical subordination to civil authority ('Erastianism'), a legacy of his disputes with Calvinist church discipline at Heidelberg.
