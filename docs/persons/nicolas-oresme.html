Nicolas Oresme

Role: SCHOLAR | Era: MEDIEVAL

Nicolas Oresme (ca. 1320–11 July 1382) was a French philosopher, theologian, mathematician, and natural philosopher who studied and taught at the University of Paris under Jean Buridan and later served as Bishop of Lisieux (from 1377). He is among the most intellectually distinguished figures of the scholastic tradition: he developed early geometrical and graphical representations of motion; translated Aristotle's Politics, Ethics, Economics, De Caelo, and De Anima into French for Charles V; and contributed to theories of money and coinage in his Tractatus de mutatione monetarum. His mathematical and philosophical work anticipated in several respects the kinematics of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries.

Oresme's relevance to the history of alchemy lies in his systematic critique of divinatory astrology and alchemy in his Livre de Divinacions (ca. 1361–1365), written in French for a lay audience. In this work he attacked astrology for violating the doctrine of free will and for providing a specious intellectual legitimacy for fraudulent practitioners; he extended his critique to alchemy, which he treated as the archetypal example of a futile art pursued by those who sought hidden secrets of nature through misguided means. Noel L. Brann, in 'Alchemy and Melancholy in Medieval and Renaissance Thought' (Ambix 32:3, 1985), cites Oresme as representative of the learned scholastic opposition to alchemy, associating both alchemists and diviners with the accusation that 'they attempt foolishly to search out the secrets of nature ... urged by the craft of the Devil.' Lynn Thorndike's History of Magic and Experimental Science discusses Oresme's broader engagement with occult ideas, noting that despite his critique of astrology and alchemy he was genuinely interested in natural philosophy and the explanation of unusual phenomena.
