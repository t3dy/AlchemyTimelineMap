Martin Del Rio

Role: SCHOLAR | Era: MODERN

Martin Del Rio (Martín Antonio Del Río, 1551–1608), a Belgian Jesuit, was one of the most prolific and widely read demonologists of the Counter-Reformation. Born in Antwerp of Spanish descent, Del Rio studied law and received his doctorate before entering the Society of Jesus in 1580, having previously served as a senator of Brabant and as procurator general of the army of the Spanish Netherlands. His publication record within the Society was exceptional, including commentaries on Scripture, classical texts, and moral philosophy, but he is best remembered for his three-volume Disquisitionum Magicarum Libri Sex (Six Books of Investigations into Magic), first published at Louvain in 1599. The work went through numerous editions throughout the seventeenth century and was widely read by confessors, inquisitors, and natural philosophers across Catholic Europe. Martha Baldwin, in Ambix (1993), identifies Del Rio as having made a more careful and accepting argument for alchemy's legitimacy than was recognized by his contemporaries: he carefully argued that alchemical transmutation was not demonic, that historical claims of successful chrysopoeia (gold-making) by Arnald of Villanova, Raymond Lull, and Bernard Trevisano were credible, and that alchemy was entirely licit for pious and wealthy men. Del Rio served as professor of sacred literature at Jesuit colleges at Rouen, Liège, Graz, and Louvain.
