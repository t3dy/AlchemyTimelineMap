Gaspar Schott SJ

Role: SCHOLAR | Era: EARLY_MODERN

Gaspar Schott (baptized 5 February 1608, Königshofen im Grabfeld, Bishopric of Würzburg – 22 May 1666, Würzburg) was a German Jesuit mathematician, natural philosopher, and prolific encyclopedist, known for his multi-volume works on natural magic, hydraulics, and experimental science. He is one of the principal intermediaries through whom the experimental and alchemical investigations of Athanasius Kircher were disseminated in mid-seventeenth-century Catholic Europe.

Schott entered the Society of Jesus and was forced to flee Germany during the Thirty Years' War when the political and confessional upheavals in his native Bishopric of Würzburg made life impossible for the Jesuit community. He spent nearly twenty years as a professor of mathematics and natural philosophy at the Jesuit college in Palermo, Sicily, before being transferred to Rome in 1652, where he lived at the Collegio Romano for three years and became a lifelong friend and devoted disciple of Kircher. He was initiated into Kircher's alchemical and natural-philosophical projects at the Collegio Romano, where he also met Francesco Lana Terzi. In 1655 he returned to Germany and settled at Würzburg, where he spent the remaining eleven years of his life in prolific publication.

Schott's published output was immense and encyclopedic. His major works include the Magia Universalis Naturae et Artis (4 vols., Würzburg, 1657–59), an exhaustive compendium of natural magic covering optics, acoustics, mathematics, and physics; the Mechanica Hydraulico-Pneumatica (Würzburg, 1657); the Thaumaturgus Physicus (1654), which devoted a chapter to pyrotechnic magic and the inextinguishable fires and fulminating gold of the alchemists; and the Cursus Mathematicus (1661). He also edited several of Kircher's works, including the Iter Exstaticum Coeleste (1660). Martha Baldwin, in 'Alchemy and the Society of Jesus in the Seventeenth Century' (Ambix 40:2, 1993), documents Schott's engagement with alchemy: he equivocated on the question of metallic transmutation, but was a firm believer in and experimenter on 'palingenesis' — the resurrection of plants from their calcined ashes — following recipes derived from Kircher and others. He also corresponded extensively with Leibniz, Otto von Guericke, and other leading natural philosophers of his day.
