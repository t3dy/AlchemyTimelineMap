Arthur Dee

Role: PHYSICIAN | Era: EARLY_MODERN

Arthur Dee (13 July 1579–1651) was an English physician and alchemist, the eldest son of John Dee and Jane Fromonds, who spent a significant portion of his career in Russia at the courts of Tsar Mikhail Fyodorovich and later in England. His biography has been reconstructed from scattered documentation, most extensively by N. A. Figurovski, in 'The Alchemist and Physician Arthur Dee (Artemii Ivanovich Dii)' (Ambix 13, 1965, pp.35–51).

Arthur grew up in a household immersed in alchemical, mathematical, and occult inquiry: he was present during his father's famous sessions with Edward Kelley at Prague and elsewhere in the 1580s–90s, and was reportedly present when 'an ounce and a quarter of common quicksilver' was transmuted in the presence of Emperor Rudolf II. He studied medicine and practised briefly in England, before departing for Russia ca. 1621 to serve as court physician to Tsar Mikhail Fyodorovich in Moscow, a post he held until 1633. He then returned to England, where he continued his medical and alchemical work. He later attempted to employ Johannes Banfi Hunyades for alchemical operations, and maintained connections with the English alchemical community. His sole published work was the Fasciculus Chemicus (London, 1650), a compilation of alchemical extracts — the name 'Arthur Dee' itself serving as a signal of transmission of the Dee family's interest in the hermetic arts into the next generation. Betty Jo Dobbs, in 'Studies in the Natural Philosophy of Sir Kenelm Digby: Part II' (Ambix 20:3, 1973), briefly notes that Arthur Dee attempted to employ Hunyades for alchemical work, placing him in the network of mid-seventeenth-century English alchemical practitioners.
