Guido de Montanor

Role: SCHOLAR | Era: MODERN

Guido de Montanor (fl. ca. 15th century) was a medieval alchemical authority — one of the most obscure yet influential figures in the Latin alchemical tradition — whose work formed the primary structural and doctrinal source for George Ripley's Compound of Alchymy (1471), the most celebrated English alchemical poem. Jennifer M. Rampling, in Ambix (2008), describes Guido as 'one of the most elusive of alchemical authorities, of whom virtually nothing is known, from the country of his birth to the century in which he was born.' His name — sometimes given as Guidonis Magni de Monte — was noted in a single papal letter of 1458-1459 involving Ripley, and he may well have been roughly contemporaneous with the English Canon, although a marginal note in a 1588 manuscript states only that 'Guido erat coetaneus Riplaii' (Guido was a contemporary of Ripley). Ripley himself, citing him in both the Compound and the Medulla Alchimiae, described Guido as 'a philosopher of Greece,' while Michael Maier (1568–1622), writing in the Symbola aurae mensae (1617), concluded he was 'without doubt a Frenchman,' and Condeesyanus's 1625 edition of his De arte chymica took the same view.

The works attributed to Guido include the Scala philosophorum (Ladder of the Philosophers), also known as the Scalae Sapientiae or the 'Twelve Steps,' which Rampling demonstrates was the immediate source for Ripley's 'Twelve Gates'; the Notabilia Guidonis Montaynor (British Library MS Sloane 3744), a collection of extracts; and the De arte chymica, a longer work of which only fragments survived until Hermann Condeesyanus printed a version in the Harmoniae imperscrutabilis Chymico-Philosophicae (Frankfurt, 1625). A German-language text, Thesaurus chymiatricus, oder lange verborgener Schatz der Chymie (Halle, 1623), attributed to Guido Magni de Monte, may represent a translation of a lost practica. The manuscripts of Guido's works circulated in England during the second half of the fifteenth century alongside pseudo-Lullian alchemical compendia, and were annotated by scholars including John Dee (who owned Oxford, Corpus Christi College MS 136, a compendium containing Guido's recipes). Despite his enormous influence on Ripley — and through Ripley on the entire English alchemical tradition — Guido's reputation was thoroughly eclipsed by that of his most famous reader, and it was only through Rampling's close analysis of manuscript relationships that the structural debt of the 'Twelve Gates' to the twelve grados of the Scala philosophorum was established.
