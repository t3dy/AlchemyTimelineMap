Fama Fraternitatis Rosae Crucis

Type: PRIMARY_SOURCE | Language: None | Composed: 1614 CE

Fama Fraternitatis R.C., oder Brüderschaft des Hochlöblichen Ordens des Rosenkreutzes (Fame of the Fraternity of the Rosy Cross) is the first of the three foundational Rosicrucian documents, published at Kassel in 1614 as an appendix to a German translation of Trajano Boccalini's Italian satire Ragguagli di Parnaso (Dispatches from Parnassus). Written in German, the Fama announces the existence of a secret Brotherhood of the Rosy Cross founded, it claims, by the fictional Christian Rosenkreutz (C.R.C.), who had traveled to the Middle East, Morocco, and Spain in the early fifteenth century, learning wisdom from Islamic sages, before returning to Germany to establish the Brotherhood with a small group of disciples. The Fama calls upon the learned men of Europe to recognize the dawning of a new age of universal reform encompassing religion, science, and society, and invites sympathizers to make contact with the Brotherhood — while providing no means of actually doing so. Carlos Gilly, in Cimelia Rhodostaurotica (Amsterdam, 1995), documents that the Fama circulated in manuscript versions from about 1610 onward, generating an enormous response before its publication; hundreds of pamphlets, responses, and letters were written by people hoping to make contact with the Brethren. The alchemical dimension of the Fama is present but not primary: Christian Rosenkreutz is described as having acquired knowledge of the Mysterium Magnum and of the correspondence between macrocosm and microcosm — characteristically Paracelsian formulations — and the Brotherhood is oriented toward healing and spiritual regeneration rather than transmutation of metals. The Fama is deeply Lutheran in character: it attacks the Pope as Antichrist and the alchemists who 'boast of the great name of alchemy' without true understanding. Lawrence Principe, in The Secrets of Alchemy (2013), notes that the Rosicrucian manifestoes drew on Paracelsian natural philosophy while subordinating chemical practice to spiritual and religious goals. Frances Yates, in The Rosicrucian Enlightenment (1972), placed the Rosicrucian movement within a broader Hermetic and neoplatonic current that she traced from the Italian Renaissance through John Dee and Bruno to the early seventeenth century; this reading has been substantially qualified by subsequent scholars including Principe, Gilly, and Hanegraaff, who emphasize the Lutheran-Paracelsian rather than Hermetic character of the manifestoes. The authorship of the Fama and Confessio remains uncertain; Gilly and others credit Johann Valentin Andreae's circle at Tübingen, particularly Tobias Hess and Christoph Besold, as the likely authors or co-authors.
