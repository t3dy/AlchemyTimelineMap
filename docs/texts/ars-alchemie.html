Ars Alchemie (The Art of Alchemy)

Type: COMPILATION | Language: None | Composed: ca. 1230–1240

Ars Alchemie (The Art of Alchemy) is a collection of alchemical recipes composed in the first half of the thirteenth century, associated in its prologue with the Arabic-Latin translator Michael Scot. Antony Vinciguerra, in his study published in Ambix (2009), identifies the Ars Alchemie as 'one of the most ancient Latin alchemical texts that is not translated directly from Arabic,' making it a pivotal document in the emergence of an autonomous Latin alchemical tradition. The text survives in four manuscripts from the late thirteenth to early fourteenth centuries: Palermo, Biblioteca Comunale, Qq A 10 (AD 1320); Oxford, Corpus Christi College 125 (end of 13th century); Cambridge, Gonville and Caius College 181 (AD 1275); and Göttingen, Universitätsbibliothek, Hist. Nat. 75 (14th century). The four manuscripts show so many variants that none appears to be a direct copy of another.

Content and Theory. The Ars Alchemie consists of a theoretical part and seven long practical recipes, followed by a detailed list of products and processes. Vinciguerra analyzes the theoretical part as a compilation: the first prologue is an 'itinerant text' found in multiple other manuscripts attached to different works; the second prologue, which includes the attribution to Michael Scot, is adapted from Hugo of Santalla's Ars Geomantie (12th century) with alchemical substitutions made for the original astrological terms — a 'cut and paste' procedure that changed 'astrological expressions to alchemical ones.' The text also draws on the De Aluminibus et Salibus, the Liber Hermetis blchkmkb, and the Liber Dedali. The definition of alchemy offered in the theoretical part is 'short and confusing,' describing it as 'a kind of hot and moist substance possessing the same virtues as the poison of a snake' — evidently taken from the De Aluminibus et Salibus. In contrast, the recipes — seven extended practical procedures for chemical operations on metals, salts, and alums — have attracted more scholarly interest for their specificity and practical orientation.

Composition and Tradition. Robert Halleux, in a key scholarly assessment cited by Vinciguerra, identified the Ars Alchemie as 'one of the first Latin attempts to compose an alchemical text divided into theory and practice.' Its mention of the alum of Aleppo as a trade commodity has been connected by David Abulafia to documented thirteenth-century trade routes for that material, providing independent corroboration of its compositional date and geographic context. The text's association with Michael Scot, one of the most productive translators of the Arabic-Latin translation movement, situates it within the broader context of the Toledan and Sicilian translation milieu that conveyed Arabic scientific knowledge to Latin Europe in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries. Scot's dedication of the work to a 'Theophilus of Tunis' — the 'master of the Saracens' — reflects the Mediterranean cross-cultural network in which the text was produced.

Modern Scholarship. The three principal manuscripts were edited separately by Charles Homer Haskins (Isis, 1928), Dorothea Singer (Isis, 1929), and Samuel Harrison Thomson (Osiris, 1938). Vinciguerra's 2009 analysis in Ambix represents the most thorough scholarly study of the text's compositional history, sources, and significance as the earliest Latin practical alchemy text produced independently of direct Arabic translation.
