Scala philosophorum (Ladder of the Philosophers)

Type: TREATISE | Language: None | Composed: ca. 15th century (first print: 1550)

The Scala philosophorum (Ladder of the Philosophers), also transmitted under the titles Scalae Sapientiae (Twelve Steps) and attributed at various times to Ramon Lull ('Raymundi Lulli Scala Sapientiae') or anonymously, is a Latin alchemical treatise divided into twelve grados (steps) whose structure and doctrine formed the direct source for George Ripley's Compound of Alchymy (1471), the most celebrated English alchemical poem. Jennifer M. Rampling, in her foundational study in Ambix (2008), demonstrates that the twelve 'gates' of Ripley's Compound — from Calcination through to Projection — correspond exactly to the twelve steps of the Scala, and that Ripley 'translated chunks of his exemplar's Latin prose more or less directly into Middle English verse, struggling at times to tailor his text to the straitjacket of rhyme royal.' Ripley attributed the Scala to Guido de Montanor, and this attribution was subsequently confirmed by Michael Maier in the Symbola aurae mensae (1617), though the connection between Guido and the Scala had previously gone unnoticed because manuscript copies of the text are either anonymous or attributed to Lull.

The Scala philosophorum opens with a lengthy preface quoting chapter 64 of the pseudo-Lullian Testamentum in full (beginning 'Ut dicit venerabilis Raymundus Lulii'), which explains why many manuscript copies were falsely attributed to Lull; Ripley did not use this Lullian preface, turning directly to the main body of twelve alchemical steps. The work treats, in sequence, all twelve standard operations of medieval Latin alchemy — calcination, dissolution, separation, conjunction, putrefaction, congelation, cibation, sublimation, fermentation, exaltation, multiplication, and projection — using the framework of sulphur-mercury theory, the four elements and their qualities, and the concept of philosophical mercury as the universal medium of transmutation. The fermentation doctrine of the Scala differed significantly from pseudo-Lullian teaching (which required gold or silver as ferment), and Rampling shows that Ripley's handling of this doctrinal tension — ultimately following Raymond over Guido on fermentation, while following the Scala's structure for everything else — is one of the key features of the Compound's intellectual originality.

The Scala philosophorum was first printed anonymously in 1550 (De Alchimia opuscula, Frankfurt) and again in 1610 (Artis Avriferae, Basel, vol. II), and was incorporated in full into Johann Daniel Mylius's Philosophia reformata (Frankfurt, 1622), where it appears as Liber I, Pars IV, accompanied by a famous sequence of engravings depicting the twelve alchemical processes. The earliest surviving manuscript copy known to Rampling is Cambridge, Trinity College MS O.8.9; the full unabridged text also survives in three manuscripts at the Bibliotheca Universitaria di Bologna. The work was edited with attribution to Guido in Jean-Jacques Manget's Bibliotheca chemica curiosa (Geneva, 1702), vol. II, p.134.
