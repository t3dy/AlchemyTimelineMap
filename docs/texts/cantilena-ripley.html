Cantilena (George Ripley)

Type: PRIMARY_SOURCE | Language: None | Composed: late 15th century (printed 1649)

The Cantilena (also known as the Carmen perpulchrum totum opus enucleans, 'Very Beautiful Song Setting Forth the Entire Work,' and De lapide philosophico seu de phoenice, 'On the Philosopher's Stone, or the Phoenix') is a late fifteenth-century Latin allegorical poem in thirty-eight stanzas attributed to George Ripley, Canon of Bridlington (d. ca. 1490). Jennifer M. Rampling, in her foundational 'Catalogue of the Ripley Corpus' (Ambix 57:2, 2010), describes the Cantilena as 'one of the most artistically accomplished works in the corpus.' The poem survives in two textual redactions: Type I, associated with Giles Du Wes (Aegidius de Vadis, d. 1535), French-born librarian to Henry VII and Henry VIII, who transcribed the poem twice in the early sixteenth century and who may have been an original transmitter of Ripley's Latin texts; and Type II, which was printed by Ludwig Combach in the Opera Omnia Chemica (Kassel, 1649). The poem was also translated into English in at least four versions during the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, and into German by Georg von Welling in his Opus Mago-Cabbalisticum (1735).

The Cantilena allegorises the alchemical process as a narrative of filial regeneration: a king (gold) regresses to the body of his mother (the prima materia or philosophical mercury) and is reborn in perfected form. The quintessence appears as a crowned maiden within the wheel of the four elements who drinks the blood of the 'Green Lion' (a Deckname for the sericonian starting material) and, in return, feeds it with her milk — an allegory of the circulation of the 'Lion' with the quintessence of wine. The underlying alchemy is closely compatible with the doctrines of Ripley's Medulla Alchimiae (1476) and Compound of Alchymy (1471), describing the preparation of the sericonian vegetable stone through the interaction of philosophical mercury with the imperfect metallic body. The phoenix symbolism (from the alternative title) reinforces the theme of death and regeneration that runs through the colour-stage framework of the magnum opus, since the phoenix dies in fire and rises reborn, just as the alchemical king undergoes nigredo before achieving the rubedo of the Philosopher's Stone. One of the two earliest copies of the Type I Cantilena, Cambridge, Trinity College MS O.2.16, was owned by Thomas Charnock (1526?–1581), who added English annotations; another (Trinity College MS O.8.5) was owned by John Dee.
