Breviary of Natural Philosophy (Thomas Charnock)

Type: TREATISE | Language: None | Composed: finished 1 January 1557/8

The Breviary of Natural Philosophy (finished 1 January 1557/8) is the principal alchemical work of Thomas Charnock (1524/6–1581), an English vernacular poem in the tradition of Thomas Norton's Ordinal of Alchemy (1477) and the most important English alchemical verse composition of the mid-sixteenth century. Jennifer M. Rampling, in The Experimental Fire: Inventing English Alchemy, 1300–1700 (University of Chicago Press, 2020), describes it as a poem 'that differs from Blomfield's Blossoms in its autobiographical and discursive style, which takes Norton's Ordinal as its model rather than Ripley's more doctrinally focused Compound.' Charnock's preference for 'anecdote and racy narrative' distinguishes the Breviary from earlier English alchemical verse: it recounts his alchemical education, his practical experiments, his encounters with patrons and rivals, and his relationship to the tradition of Norton and Ripley in a manner more akin to confession and autobiography than to systematic instruction in alchemical theory. F. Sherwood Taylor, in his foundational article in Ambix (1946), identified the Breviary as a key document for understanding mid-sixteenth-century English alchemical practice and the social networks within which it operated.

The Breviary was composed in Somerset and circulated in manuscript before being published posthumously by Elias Ashmole (1617–1692) in the Theatrum Chemicum Britannicum (London, 1652), which served as the primary vehicle for Charnock's posthumous reputation. Ashmole's edition gave the Breviary canonical status alongside the works of Ripley and Norton as a monument of English alchemical verse. In the Breviary, Charnock positions himself as the latest in a distinguished English alchemical lineage, drawing on Norton's Ordinal as a literary and intellectual model while claiming personal experience of practical alchemical operations. Like Norton, Charnock was preoccupied with the reliability of alchemical texts and the problem of fraudulent practitioners; his autobiographical method allows him to frame these concerns through personal narrative rather than abstract instruction.
