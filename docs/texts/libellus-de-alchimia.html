Libellus de Alchimia

Type: PRIMARY_SOURCE | Language: Latin | Composed: Medieval

Libellus de Alchimia (Little Book of Alchemy), attributed in tradition to Hermes Trismegistus (clearly fictitious), is a medieval Latin alchemical treatise that addresses foundational alchemical principles and operations. The work exemplifies the tradition of framing alchemical knowledge as ancient wisdom revealed by legendary authorities. The Libellus synthesizes various alchemical traditions—Greek, Islamic, and Latin medieval—into a coherent presentation. While not one of the most influential texts, the work demonstrates how alchemical knowledge was assembled and transmitted through compilations attributed to authoritative figures.

Content and Theory

The Libellus de Alchimia addresses the fundamental principles of alchemy: the nature of matter, the operations through which transformation is achieved, and the goal of the philosopher's stone. The work discusses distillation, sublimation, calcination, and other operations in systematic fashion. It emphasizes the importance of the prima materia and the successive color stages (nigredo, albedo, rubedo) marking progress toward completion. The text presents alchemy as a rational discipline grounded in natural philosophy rather than as magical or mystical practice.

Composition and Textual Tradition

The Libellus de Alchimia is a medieval anonymous compilation, likely 13th or 14th century in origin. The attribution to Hermes Trismegistus is conventional; many alchemical texts bore this attribution to claim ancient authority. The work circulated in manuscript form and was printed in some early modern editions. The textual tradition shows variations across different manuscripts.

Modern Scholarship

The Libellus de Alchimia remains relatively understudied in modern scholarship, partly because it is less distinctive than other major alchemical texts and partly because it survives in limited manuscript witnesses. The work serves primarily as evidence of how alchemical knowledge was organized and transmitted in medieval Europe rather than as a text containing unusual or innovative ideas.
