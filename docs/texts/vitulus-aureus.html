Vitulus Aureus (Helvetius)

Type: PRIMARY_SOURCE | Language: None | Composed: 1667 CE

The Vitulus Aureus, Quem Mundus Adorat et Orat (The Golden Calf which the World Adores and Worships; Amsterdam, 1667) by the Dutch physician Johann Friedrich Helvetius (1630–1709) is one of the most famous transmutation accounts published in the seventeenth century. The text describes in detail Helvetius's alleged experience of witnessing a metallic transmutation — the projection of a tiny quantity of a yellow sulphurous substance upon molten lead in a crucible, with the result being approximately two drams of gold — performed by an anonymous adept who visited him twice at his home in The Hague around the turn of 1667. The work was immediately influential: the adept's claimed connection to Rosicrucianism was asserted by later writers; Baruch Spinoza investigated the transmutation and reported his findings to Jarig Jelles (Epistola XL, March 1667); Isaac Newton excerpted the account in his alchemical notebooks; and Leibniz received secondhand reports about it. Mike A. Zuber, in Ambix (2018), notes that later printed sources from 1701 onward identified Helvetius's adept as 'an adept philosopher, of the society of the Rosicrucians, by the name of Gottmann, in Leiden' — an identification that was spread orally by the itinerant alchemist Johann Heinrich Vierordt at least two decades earlier, though Leibniz dismissed these claims as 'nonsense.' The Dutch edition of 1667 was followed by a German translation (Nürnberg, 1668, as Guldenes Kalb) and a Latin edition. The title alludes to the biblical golden calf — Helvetius's joke at the expense of those who, like the Israelites worshipping the golden idol in the wilderness, placed too much trust in the power of gold.
