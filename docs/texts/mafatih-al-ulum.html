Mafatih al-Ulum (Keys to the Sciences)

Type: ENCYCLOPEDIA | Language: None | Composed: ca. 976–980 CE

Mafatih al-Ulum (Keys to the Sciences) is a tenth-century Arabic encyclopaedia composed between 976 and 980 by Abu Abdallah Muhammad ibn Ahmad al-Khwarizmi (d. ca. 997), a court scribe working for the Samanid dynasty at Bukhara. Karin C. Ryding, in her study published in Ambix (1994), identifies the Mafatih al-Ulum's chapter on alchemy as 'a unique and valuable cultural artifact': an objective description of medieval Islamic alchemy written by a non-alchemist, designed to explain alchemical vocabulary and practice to court secretaries and scribes who required 'definitions and explanations of the burgeoning number of technical terms being coined and employed within the various disciplines of the time.' The work was dedicated to Abu al-Hasan Ubayd Allah ibn Ahmad al-Utbi, vizier to the Samanid ruler Nuh II ibn Mansur (r. 976–997).

Context and Encyclopedic Tradition. Ryding situates the Mafatih al-Ulum within a cluster of three major tenth-century Islamic encyclopedic projects that chronicled and categorized the extraordinary intellectual achievement of the Abbasid era: alongside the Fihrist of Ibn al-Nadim (d. 995) and the collective epistles of the Ikhwan al-Safa' ('Brethren of Purity'). Of the three, Ryding describes the Mafatih al-Ulum as 'most closely resembling a practical handbook,' with its focus on definitions and taxonomy rather than historical narrative or philosophical speculation. Stapleton, Azo, and Husain, in their 1927 study published in the Memoirs of the Asiatic Society of Bengal, established that al-Khwarizmi based his alchemical chapter primarily on the works of Muhammad ibn Zakariya al-Razi (d. 925), specifically al-Razi's al-Madkhal al-Talimi (The Instructive Introduction).

The Alchemy Chapter. The ninth chapter of the second discourse covers alchemy in three sections: (1) apparatus, listing and describing fifteen instruments including the furnace, crucible, cucurbit, alembic, and athanor (the self-ventilating oven); (2) substances, covering the seven metals (gold, silver, iron, copper, lead, tin, quicksilver), four 'spirits' (sulfur, arsenic, mercury, ammonia), various salts, naturally-occurring stones including gems, and man-made compounds; and (3) thirteen operations, including sublimation/distillation, solution, coagulation, calcination, roasting, oxidation, lixiviation, amalgamation, fixing, and descension. Al-Khwarizmi notes that 'practitioners of this craft call it the Absolute Wisdom' and that they 'speak in symbols' using planetary-metal correspondences while maintaining consistency about the use of the sun to represent gold and the moon to represent silver. The chapter concludes with a description of the elixir and an identification of the forms of the philosophers' stone.

Significance. As an 'objective description of medieval alchemy written by a non-alchemist,' the Mafatih al-Ulum's alchemical chapter provides unmediated evidence for the material culture and operational vocabulary of Islamic alchemy at the height of the Samanid renaissance, without the allegorical obscurity that renders most alchemical texts opaque to outsiders. Ryding's 1994 translation in Ambix provides an updated version of the single earlier Western translation by Stapleton et al. (1927), which has remained largely inaccessible.
