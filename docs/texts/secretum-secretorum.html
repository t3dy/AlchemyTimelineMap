Secretum Secretorum

Type: PRIMARY_SOURCE | Language: Arabic; Latin translation | Composed: Medieval

Secretum Secretorum, a medieval Latin alchemical and magical work attributed spuriously to Aristotle, is one of numerous pseudo-Aristotelian treatises that circulated in the medieval and early modern periods. The work contains alchemical doctrines, magical procedures, and practical recipes attributed to Aristotle (the attribution is clearly fictitious) to claim ancient authority. The Secretum Secretorum exemplifies the medieval practice of pseud epigraphic authorship: claiming the authority of a major thinker (Aristotle) to lend weight to new or controversial material. The work demonstrates how alchemy was understood within medieval intellectual culture and how alchemical ideas were integrated with magical and philosophical thought.

Content and Theory

The Secretum Secretorum addresses alchemical operations, magical procedures, and practical recipes, presenting them as secret wisdom revealed by Aristotle to his student Alexander. The work promises power through knowledge: mastery of the operations described will allow the practitioner to accomplish transformations of matter and effects in the material world. The text integrates practical recipes (for producing dyes, medicines, and other useful substances) with more speculative or magical procedures (incantations, talismanic magic, astrological timing). The work does not sharply distinguish between what a modern reader might categorize as chemistry, medicine, or magic; all are understood as related forms of practical wisdom about manipulating nature.

Composition and Textual Tradition

The Secretum Secretorum is a medieval anonymous compilation whose original authorship is unknown. The attribution to Aristotle is clearly fictitious; no evidence suggests Aristotle wrote such a work. The text may draw on earlier sources—Islamic magical texts, Greek alchemical fragments, medieval European craft knowledge—but the specific compilation as we know it is medieval. The work circulated in manuscript form throughout the medieval and early modern periods and was printed in some editions. The textual tradition shows considerable variation, suggesting continuous adaptation and supplementation.

Modern Scholarship

Modern scholarship on the Secretum Secretorum has focused on understanding it as evidence of how medieval Europeans understood and applied alchemical and magical knowledge. The work's pseudo-Aristotelian attribution has been analyzed as a strategy for lending authority to material that might otherwise be questioned. Contemporary scholars recognize the work as a representative example of medieval magical and alchemical thought rather than as a unique or particularly influential text.

The Encyclopedia of Practical Wisdom and Its Alchemical Content

The Secretum Secretorum (Secret of Secrets) is one of the most widely read texts of the medieval period, translated into at least twenty-six languages and surviving in hundreds of manuscripts. The text presents itself as a letter from Aristotle to Alexander the Great, containing advice on statecraft, health, physiognomy, astrology, and natural philosophy. Its alchemical content is not the primary focus of the text but appears within the broader framework of natural philosophical knowledge that the text claimed to transmit: minerals and their properties, the effects of celestial influences on terrestrial substances, and the practical arts that depended on natural philosophical understanding.

The Arabic original of the Secretum Secretorum (known as Kitab Sirr al-Asrar) was composed or compiled in the tenth century and drew on a complex tradition of Arabic encyclopedic wisdom that combined Greek philosophical material (attributed to Aristotle to give it authority) with Arabic additions. The attribution to Aristotle was a characteristic strategy of medieval Arabic and Latin encyclopedic texts, paralleling the attribution of alchemical texts to Hermes Trismegistus, Democritus, and other ancient authorities. The Aristotelian attribution gave the text enormous authority in both the Islamic world and, through Latin translation, in medieval Europe.

The Latin translation by Philip of Tripoli (c. 1230) made the text available to European readers at roughly the same time as the major Aristotelian natural philosophical translations were arriving. Roger Bacon wrote a commentary on the Secretum Secretorum that expanded its natural philosophical content and connected it to his own program of experimental science. This commentary is significant for the history of alchemy because it demonstrates Bacon's engagement with a text that combined practical natural knowledge (including alchemical procedures) with the authority of the most prestigious ancient philosopher, and because it illustrates how Bacon sought to integrate different streams of practical and theoretical knowledge into a unified natural philosophical program.

The Secretum Secretorum's treatment of health, astrology, and the properties of substances reflects the same framework of elemental qualities and planetary influences that organized alchemical theory. The physician-philosopher who understood how to manipulate these influences could maintain health, prolong life, and produce powerful effects in the material world — goals that were continuous with those of alchemical iatrochemistry even when expressed in a different generic form.

The Secretum Secretorum (Secret of Secrets) is a pseudo-Aristotelian compilation of practical wisdom — on kingship, physiognomy, health, astrology, and natural lore — purportedly written by Aristotle as a letter of advice to Alexander the Great. Scholars have established that the text originated as an Arabic compilation, the Kitab Sirr al-Asrar, probably composed in the eighth or ninth century and drawing on a diverse range of Hellenistic and Persian sources. It was translated into Latin twice in the twelfth century, the more influential version by Philip of Tripoli, and rapidly became one of the most widely read texts in medieval Europe, with hundreds of manuscripts surviving.

Roger Bacon's engagement with the Secretum Secretorum was decisive for his own natural philosophical program. Bacon edited and commented on the text extensively, inserting discussions of optics, mathematics, and experimental method that reveal his conviction that the Aristotelian-Hermetic tradition of practical wisdom could be systematized and expanded through the application of mathematical and optical science. Bacon saw in the Secretum's emphasis on practical knowledge — including its alchemical sections on the transformation of metals — a confirmation of his own view that natural philosophy should issue in practical mastery over nature. His commentary thus constitutes a significant document in the history of the relationship between speculative and operative knowledge.

Sections of the Secretum Secretorum dealing with the preparation of medicines and the properties of minerals and metals circulated alongside explicitly alchemical texts throughout the Middle Ages and Renaissance. Lynn Thorndike catalogued the text's contents and traced its influence on later medieval natural philosophy, while Steven Williams's monograph provided the most comprehensive account of the text's manuscript tradition and reception. The work's generic hybridity — at once mirror for princes, medical handbook, and compendium of occult natural knowledge — made it unusually adaptable to a wide range of intellectual contexts.
