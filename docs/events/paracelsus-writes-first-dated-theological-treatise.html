Scholarly Context

Urs Leo Gantenbein established that Paracelsus's first dated works were theological treatises written at Salzburg in 1524 — predating his medical reform career — in 'Cross and Crucible: Alchemy in the Theology of Paracelsus' in Ambix vol. 67 no. 1 (2020) pp.1-12.

In 1524, while residing in Salzburg, Paracelsus (Theophrastus Bombastus von Hohenheim, 1493–1541) composed his earliest dated works — theological treatises dealing with the then-urgent questions of church reformation. Urs Leo Gantenbein, in Ambix (2020), draws on this fact to make a crucial historiographic intervention: 'Historians of science have deliberately overlooked the fact that the first dated works of Paracelsus were written in 1524, when he was in Salzburg, and do not concern medical studies, but theological treatises dealing with the then highly topical questions of church reformation.' Paracelsus, Gantenbein argues (following Andrew Weeks's earlier research), appears to have begun engaging with theological questions around 1520, when Martin Luther's reformatory pamphlets had spread throughout the German-speaking world. Paracelsus would only emerge as a medical reformer in Basel three years later (1527). Based on personal testimony in his own writings, Gantenbein concludes that 'Paracelsus was only inspired to reform medicine by the previous church reform' — the theological came first. In his theological writing, Paracelsus combined the major issues of the Radical Reformation (social justice, the requirements of true Christian life, and a special Eucharistic doctrine) with his developing natural-philosophical framework of the tria prima (mercury, sulphur, salt) and the four pillars of medicine. The 1524 Salzburg theological writings remained unpublished until the modern era; every early printer hesitated because 'in the age of hardening religious positions they contained explosive heretical doctrines that would have led to serious problems for any denomination.'
