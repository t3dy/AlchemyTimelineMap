Scholarly Context

William Newman proved Starkey's authorship through comparison of the Introitus with Starkey's letters to Boyle in 'Prophecy and Alchemy: The Origin of Eirenaeus Philalethes' in Ambix vol. 37 no. 3 (1990) pp.97-115.

Between 1651 and 1654, George Starkey (1628–1665) composed the Introitus Apertus ad Occlusum Regis Palatium (Open Entrance to the Shut Palace of the King) under the pseudonym 'Anonymous Philalethes,' a work William Newman in Ambix (1990) identifies as the same text later published in 1667 under the name Eirenaeus Philalethes and known as the Introitus Apertus. Newman's proof of Starkey's authorship rests on source-critical comparison of the Introitus with Starkey's Latin letters to Robert Boyle (3 January and 3 February 1651/2, Royal Society Library): the antimony-mercury procedures described in those private letters are identical to the procedures encoded in the allegorical language of the Introitus. Hartlib's Ephemerides of summer 1652 already contain references to 'The Chym(ical) MS. of Stirk's Adeptus,' showing that at least one manuscript attributed to Starkey's fictional 'adept' was in circulation by that date, and by summer 1653 Friedrich Clodius reported multiple manuscripts. The Introitus's processes center on the preparation of the 'regulus martis' — metallic antimony reduced by iron — as the starting material for the Philosopher's Stone; these processes were drawn from Starkey's own laboratory practice, grounded in his reading of Alexander von Suchten's Antimonii Mysteria Gemina and his antimony work with Boyle. The Introitus thus represents the culmination of Starkey's transformation of laboratory practice into pseudonymous literary persona.
