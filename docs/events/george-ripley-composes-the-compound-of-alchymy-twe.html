Scholarly Context

Jennifer M. Rampling analyzed the Compound of Alchymy's sources and identified Guido de Montanor's previously unrecognized role in 'Establishing the Canon: George Ripley and his Alchemical Sources' in Ambix vol. 55 no. 3 (2008) pp.189-208.

George Ripley (ca. 1415–ca. 1490), Canon Regular of St. Mary's Priory at Bridlington in Yorkshire, composed his Compound of Alchymy, divided into Twelve Gates in 1471 — the date given by its colophon — making it the primary dated text by the most influential English alchemist of the late medieval period. Jennifer M. Rampling, in Ambix (2008), establishes that while Ripley is widely perceived as primarily an expositor of pseudo-Lullian alchemy, the central structure of the Compound — the 'Twelve Gates' — is actually based not on pseudo-Lull but on a Latin treatise by the obscure alchemist Guido de Montanor (also named Guidonis Magni de Monte), whom Ripley himself praised as 'Guido de Montayno whose fame goyth wyde.' Rampling demonstrates that the Compound's Preface and associated prefatory material show heavy pseudo-Lullian influence, while the twelve chapters themselves draw primarily on Guido's practical framework. Both sources are present throughout — balancing references to Raymond Lull with those of Guido — but Guido's influence, Rampling argues, 'outweighs even that of Raymond in determining the structure and content of the Twelve Gates.' The Compound attracted intense study by later practitioners including Thomas Charnock (16th century), George Starkey (writing as Eirenaeus Philalethes, 17th century), John Dee, Robert Boyle, and Isaac Newton. It was first printed in Ralph Rabbards's 1591 edition, with subsequent publication in Elias Ashmole's Theatrum Chemicum Britannicum (1652). In the seventeenth century, Nicolaus Maius translated the Twelve Gates into Latin verse for Emperor Rudolf II, attesting the text's sustained cross-European prestige.
