Scholarly Context

Jennifer M. Rampling identifies Thomas Charnock as finishing the Breviary of Natural Philosophy on 1 January 1557/8, describes its autobiographical and discursive style as distinct from Ripley and closer to Norton's Ordinal, and notes its publication in the Theatrum Chemicum Britannicum (1652), in The Experimental Fire: Inventing English Alchemy, 1300–1700 (Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 2020), pp.230–252. See also F. Sherwood Taylor, 'Thomas Charnock,' Ambix 2:3–4 (1946) pp.148–176.

On 1 January 1557/8, Thomas Charnock (1524/6–1581) of Faversham, Kent and Somerset finished his Breviary of Natural Philosophy, the most important English alchemical verse composition of the mid-sixteenth century. Jennifer M. Rampling, in The Experimental Fire: Inventing English Alchemy, 1300–1700 (University of Chicago Press, 2020), describes the Breviary as a poem 'that differs from Blomfield's Blossoms in its autobiographical and discursive style, which takes Norton's Ordinal as its model rather than Ripley's more doctrinally focused Compound.' F. Sherwood Taylor, in his foundational article in Ambix (1946), provided the first systematic study of Charnock and the Breviary. Unlike the formal doctrinal verse of George Ripley's Compound of Alchymy (1471) or even Norton's own Ordinal, the Breviary is characterized by anecdote, personal reminiscence, and what Rampling calls 'racy narrative' — a distinctive style that recounts Charnock's alchemical education, his practical experiments, and his encounters with patrons and rivals. Charnock described himself as an 'unlettered scholar' and an unlicensed medical practitioner, positioning himself below the socially elevated alchemical philosophers while claiming direct practical experience. His deepest intellectual debt was to Norton, whose Ordinal shaped both the literary model and the biographical framework of the Breviary. The poem circulated in manuscript and was eventually published posthumously by Elias Ashmole (1617–1692) in the Theatrum Chemicum Britannicum (London, 1652), where it acquired canonical status alongside Ripley and Norton as a monument of the English alchemical tradition. Charnock's personal copy of fifteenth-century alchemical manuscripts, including his furnace drawings (now Cambridge, Trinity College Library, MS O.2.16) annotated with the marginal note 'Charnocke defieth Charcole,' survive as evidence of his practical laboratory work.
