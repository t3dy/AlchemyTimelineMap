Scholarly Context

Erwin F. Lange identifies Lazarus Ercker and his Beschreibung Allerfürnemsten Mineralischen Erzt-und Bergwerkarten (Prague, ca. 1574) as the third of the major sixteenth-century metallurgists and their work as representative of an empirical tradition opposed to alchemical transmutation, in 'Alchemy and the Sixteenth Century Metallurgists,' Ambix 13:2 (1966) pp.92-95. See also Anneliese G. Sisco and Cyril Stanley Smith, Lazarus Ercker's Treatise on Ores and Assaying (Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1951).

Around 1574, Lazarus Ercker (d. 1593), chief superintendent of the mines in the kingdom of Bohemia under Holy Roman Emperor Rudolf II, published his Beschreibung Allerfürnemsten Mineralischen Erzt-und Bergwerkarten (Description of the Foremost Mineral Ores and Mining Operations) in Prague. Erwin F. Lange, in Ambix (1966), identifies Ercker's treatise as the third of the major sixteenth-century metallurgical works, alongside Biringuccio's Pirotechnia (1540) and Agricola's De re Metallica (1556), that together established an empirical tradition of mining and metallurgical knowledge distinct from the transmutational claims of alchemy. Like Biringuccio and Agricola, Ercker dismissed alchemical gold-making with terse practicality, confining himself to 'processes and procedures which had been thoroughly tested and could be relied upon.' The work's particular contribution was in quantitative assaying and analytical procedure: it provided detailed instructions for the assaying of gold, silver, and copper; for making precision instruments including the analytical balance (which Ercker described fashioning from a sword blade); and for constructing and calibrating standardized sets of weights. Sisco and Smith, in their English translation (University of Chicago Press, 1951), identify the work as a forerunner of modern quantitative chemical analysis. Ercker worked at the court of Rudolf II alongside Tycho Brahe and Johannes Kepler, in a milieu that combined empirical metallurgical expertise with the astrology, Paracelsian medicine, and alchemical speculation that Rudolf famously patronized. The Beschreibung went through multiple editions and translations, including an early English version by Sir John Pettus in 1683.
