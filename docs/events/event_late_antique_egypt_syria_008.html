c. 320 CE Late Antiquity (3rd–7th c.)

Alexandria

Egypt

c. 320 CE, Alexandria: In the early fourth century, as Christianity becomes increasingly influential in the Mediterranean world, Zosimos of Panopolis's writings continue to attract serious attention from scholars and practitioners. His work on Distillation and Operational Chemistry transcends the religious and philosophical divisions of his era, appealing to pagans and Christians alike who seek to understand material transformation. Zosimos's empirical approach to chemical practice suggests that the study of matter can yield genuine knowledge independent of theological commitments. This universality of chemical method may help explain why Zosimos's works survive the transformation of the Mediterranean world from pagan to Christian dominance. Unlike philosophical schools tied to particular metaphysical or religious systems, alchemy—as Zosimos has framed it—focuses on repeatable operations observable by anyone who follows the instructions carefully. This portability and independence from doctrinal claims helps secure alchemy's place in the Byzantine intellectual tradition and, later, in Islamic scholarship.
