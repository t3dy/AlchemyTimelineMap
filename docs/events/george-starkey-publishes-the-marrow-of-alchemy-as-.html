Scholarly Context

William Newman established Starkey's authorship of The Marrow of Alchemy in Prophecy and Alchemy: The Origin of Eirenaeus Philalethes in Ambix vol. 37 no. 3 (1990) pp.97-115.

In 1654 and 1655 The Marrow of Alchemy was published in two parts attributed to Eirenaeus Philoponos Philalethes with prefaces bearing anagrams of the name George Stirk -- the name under which George Starkey (1628-1665), a Harvard-educated New England alchemist, had emigrated to London in 1650 and joined the Samuel Hartlib circle. William Newman, in Ambix (1990), establishes that Starkey was the actual author: in various other texts Starkey signed his name Philoponos Philalethes or Eirenaeus Philoponos Philalethes, and an unpublished manuscript (British Library Sloane 633) has Starkey claiming authorship. The Marrow presents Philoponos Philalethes as a pupil who received the white elixir from a mysterious English-born Cosmopolite -- a figure Newman shows to be a literary invention based on the Sendivogian legend. Newman's analysis shows that Starkey's antimonial processes, derived from Alexander von Suchten's Mysteria gemina, underlie both the Marrow and the Introitus apertus. The narrative that Philalethes received the elixir, tried to multiply it, and lost most of it, echoes the contemporaneous account of Sendivogius squandering Seton's transmutational powder (published in Pierre Borel's Tresor de recherches, 1655). The Marrow influenced Isaac Newton's alchemical reading; William Cooper's 1669 English printing of the Introitus created the canonical name Eyrenaeus Philaletha Cosmopolita for Starkey's fictional adept.
