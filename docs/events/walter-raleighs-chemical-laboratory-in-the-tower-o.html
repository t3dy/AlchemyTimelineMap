Scholarly Context

P. M. Rattansi analyzed Raleigh's chemical activities in the Tower from primary sources in 'Alchemy and Natural Magic in Raleigh's History of the World' in Ambix vol. 13 no. 3 (1966) pp.122-138.

During his imprisonment in the Tower of London (1603–1616), Sir Walter Raleigh (c. 1552–1618) maintained a systematic chemical laboratory, making him one of the most thoroughly documented lay alchemical-chemical practitioners in Jacobean England. P. M. Rattansi, in Ambix (1966), reconstructs the laboratory's activities from contemporary sources: Sir William Wade, lieutenant of the Tower, reported to Robert Cecil in 1605 that Raleigh had converted 'a little hen-house in the garden into a still-house; and here he doth spend his time all day in distillations.' The Reverend John Ward's Diary (1661) records his meeting with Mr. Sampson, who 'was operator to Sir Walter Raleigh twelve years, whilst he was in the Tower' — establishing that the laboratory operated with a dedicated professional assistant for over a decade and constituted a genuine research enterprise rather than casual experimentation. Raleigh's laboratory produced the preparation known as 'Raleigh's Cordial,' which became famous throughout the seventeenth century: John Evelyn recorded in 1662 accompanying King Charles II to witness Nicolas Le Febvre's preparation of the Cordial, and Le Febvre published a formal treatise on its preparation and virtues in 1665. Raleigh himself connected his chemical method to his historical method in the History of the World (1614), comparing a 'skilful and learned Chymist' who 'by separation of visible elements' draws medicine from poison to the historian who extracts truth from legend. Rattansi situates Raleigh's chemical activity within his broader engagement with Hermeticism, Paracelsian natural philosophy, and the tradition of natural magic, identifying the History of the World as a key document of early seventeenth-century English attitudes toward alchemy and its relationship to legitimate natural knowledge.
