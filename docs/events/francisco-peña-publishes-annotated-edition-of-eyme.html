Scholarly Context

Andrew Campbell, Lorenza Gianfrancesco, and Neil Tarrant identified Francisco Peña's 1578 annotated edition of the Directorium Inquisitorum as the pivotal document in reshaping the Church's institutional stance on alchemy during the later sixteenth century, in 'Alchemy and the Mendicant Orders of Late Medieval and Early Modern Europe' in Ambix vol. 65 no. 3 (2018) pp.201-209.

In 1578, the Spanish canonist and Roman Rota judge Francisco Peña (ca. 1540–1612) published a heavily annotated edition of Nicholas Eymerich's fourteenth-century Directorium Inquisitorum at Rome. Andrew Campbell, Lorenza Gianfrancesco, and Neil Tarrant, in Ambix (2018), identify Peña's edition as playing a 'pivotal role' in reshaping the Catholic Church's institutional position toward alchemy during the later sixteenth century — more consequential, in this respect, than the Directorium itself, which had been relatively little known during the century and a half following its first printing at Barcelona (1503). Peña's commentary, drawing on the framework Eymerich had created to identify diabolical magic and distinguish it from natural operations, explicitly extended the inquisitorial framework to alchemical practice — in particular to those aspects of alchemy that appeared to promise impossible or supranatural results, such as the transmutation of base metals into gold. The 1578 edition was distributed throughout Counter-Reformation Europe and read by inquisitors, theologians, and confessors. The result was a significant tightening of ecclesiastical suspicion toward alchemy among Catholic authorities. This context helps explain the defensive postures adopted by later alchemical authors in Catholic Europe — the Venetian priest Giovanni Pantheus had already felt obliged in 1530 to rename alchemy 'Voarchadumia' to avoid prosecution; Martin Del Rio SJ, writing in 1599, would carefully argue that alchemy was licit in principle even while distancing it from demonic invocation; and the Franciscan Girolamo Trimarchi, in 1637, would defend alchemy within a strictly Aristotelian natural-philosophical framework specifically to avoid the association with diabolism that Peña's commentary had reactivated.
