Scholarly Context

Antony Vinciguerra analyzed the Ars Alchemie and argued for its status as the first Latin practical alchemy text in 'The Ars Alchemie: the First Latin Text on Practical Alchemy' in Ambix vol. 56 no. 1 (2009) pp.57-67.

Michael Scot (ca. 1175–ca. 1232), the Scottish Arabic-Latin translator serving at the court of Holy Roman Emperor Frederick II, is associated in the prologue of the Ars Alchemie with the compilation of the text that Antony Vinciguerra, in Ambix (2009), identifies as 'one of the most ancient Latin alchemical texts that is not translated directly from Arabic.' Vinciguerra analyzes the Ars Alchemie's second prologue — which opens 'Ego vero magister Miccael scottus interpretationem aggredior et tibi magno Theophilo gayto saracenorum tecum Tunixe...' ('I, Master Michael Scot, undertake this translation and bring to you, great Theophilus, master of the Saracens at Tunis...') — as an adaptation of Hugo of Santalla's twelfth-century Ars Geomantie, with astrological terms systematically replaced by alchemical ones. The Ars Alchemie consists of a theoretical part (drawing on the De Aluminibus et Salibus, the Liber Hermetis, and other Arabic-derived texts) and seven practical recipes for chemical operations on metals, salts, and alums, including a reference to the alum of Aleppo that David Abulafia has connected to documented thirteenth-century trade routes. Robert Halleux has identified the text as 'one of the first Latin attempts to compose an alchemical text divided into theory and practice.' The text survives in four manuscripts from the late thirteenth to early fourteenth centuries at Palermo, Oxford, Cambridge, and Göttingen. Its compilation under Scot's name at the Sicilian or Toledan translation milieu represents a landmark in the establishment of a Latin alchemical tradition independent of Arabic models, providing the procedural and theoretical framework that subsequent Latin alchemists would adapt and elaborate.
