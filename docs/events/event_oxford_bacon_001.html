c. 1260 Medieval (7th–15th c.)

Oxford

England

c. 1260 CE, Oxford: The Franciscan friar and natural philosopher Roger Bacon, lecturing at Oxford, advocates for the centrality of Operational Chemistry to natural philosophy and theological understanding. Bacon insists that true knowledge of God's creation requires direct engagement with natural operations through experimentation. His Opus Majus champions alchemy as a legitimate philosophical pursuit grounded in observable results and reproducible procedures. Bacon's advocacy emerges from his engagement with translated Arabic texts, particularly the Jabir corpus and al-Razi's works, which demonstrate that alchemical operations produce verifiable effects. Bacon argues that alchemists possess knowledge unavailable through purely theoretical philosophy, knowledge gained only through laboratory engagement and craft mastery. His insistence that experimentation constitutes a valid path to natural philosophy elevates alchemy from marginal pursuit to central intellectual concern. Though Bacon himself conducts limited laboratory work, his philosophical argument—that operational knowledge is real knowledge—legitimizes alchemy within the university. Bacon establishes the intellectual framework within which European alchemy can be pursued as serious natural philosophy rather than fraud or esotericism. His voice carries particular authority due to his Franciscan standing and his evident learning in multiple languages and traditions.
