Scholarly Context

William Newman analyzed Seton's legend and its literary consequences in 'Prophecy and Alchemy: The Origin of Eirenaeus Philalethes' in Ambix vol. 37 no. 3 (1990) pp.97-115.

Alexander Seton, a Scottish alchemist known as 'the Cosmopolite,' performed a series of transmutation demonstrations before learned witnesses across continental Europe between 1602 and 1604, claiming to transmute base metals into gold and silver. William Newman, in his study of the origin of Eirenaeus Philalethes in Ambix (1990), reconstructs these events primarily from the account of Johann Dienheim, a physician of Freiburg, who described a transmutation of lead to gold performed by Seton in Basel in the summer of 1603 in the presence of himself and Jacob Zwinger, a Basel physician. Contemporary sources describe similar demonstrations under various assumed names — Hirschberger, Gustenhofer — across different cities of the Holy Roman Empire. According to the most widely circulated version of the story, Seton was eventually imprisoned and tortured by the Duke of Saxony after revealing possession of the Philosopher's Stone, and died shortly after being rescued by Michael Sendivogius. Newman's analysis demonstrates that Seton's demonstrations became the nucleus of a complex legend that, circulated through Pierre Borel's Tresor de recherches (1655) and other intermediaries, directly shaped George Starkey's fictional construction of the 'Cosmopolite' figure in The Marrow of Alchemy (1654–1655) and, through Starkey, the broader mythology of the anonymous adept in seventeenth-century alchemical culture.
