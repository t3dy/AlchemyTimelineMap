1571 Early Modern (15th–18th c.)

Basle (Basel)

Switzerland

1571, Basel: The Swiss physician and theologian Thomas Erastus (1524–1583) publishes the first part of his Disputationum de medicina nova Philippi Paracelsi (Basel, 1571), including the Explicatio quaestionis, utrum ex metallis ignobilibus arte conflari aurum posit verum et naturale, which mounts a systematic philosophical attack on alchemical transmutation. Drawing on Aristotelian natural philosophy, Erastus argues that the substantial forms of metals cannot be reproduced by art and that any substance produced by alchemical procedures — however similar in sensible properties to gold — would not be 'true and natural gold.' The attack provokes a sustained apologetic response from defenders of chrysopoeia, most notably Gaston DuClo's Apologia argyropoeiae et chrysopoeiae adversus Thomam Erastum (Nevers, 1590), and helps shape the terms of debate over alchemy's legitimacy for several decades. Jennifer M. Rampling, in 'Transmission and Transmutation' (Early Science and Medicine 17, 2012, p.486), documents how continental editors of English alchemical texts — including Bernard Penot and Nicolas Barnaud — implicitly positioned their compilations of Ripley and other authorities as witnesses against the Erastian critique. William R. Newman discusses the controversy in Atoms and Alchemy (Chicago, 2006) as a central episode in the epistemology of early modern chymistry.
