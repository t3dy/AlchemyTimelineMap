Scholarly Context

Carlos Gilly documents the publication of the Fama Fraternitatis (Kassel, 1614) and its manuscript transmission from ca. 1610, in Cimelia Rhodostaurotica: Die Rosenkreuzer im Spiegel der zwischen 1610 und 1660 entstandenen Handschriften und Drucke (Amsterdam: In de Pelikaan, 1995). See also Frances Yates, The Rosicrucian Enlightenment (London: Routledge & Kegan Paul, 1972), and Lawrence Principe, The Secrets of Alchemy (Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 2013), pp.147-151.

In 1614, the Fama Fraternitatis R.C., oder Brüderschaft des Hochlöblichen Ordens des Rosenkreutzes (Fame of the Fraternity of the Rosy Cross) was published at Kassel by the printer Wilhelm Wessel, appended to a German translation of Trajano Boccalini's Italian satire Ragguagli di Parnaso. The Fama announced the existence of a secret Brotherhood of the Rosy Cross founded by the legendary Christian Rosenkreutz (C.R.C.), who was said to have traveled to Arabia, Morocco, and Spain in the early fifteenth century to acquire universal wisdom, before returning to Germany and founding the Brotherhood with seven disciples. The Brotherhood had reportedly kept its existence secret for 120 years, but now called upon the learned men of Europe to recognize the dawning of a new age of universal reform and to signal their readiness to join. Carlos Gilly, in Cimelia Rhodostaurotica (Amsterdam, 1995), documents that the Fama had circulated in manuscript from about 1610 onward and had already generated hundreds of responses before its publication; after 1614 the number of pamphlets, treatises, and letters responding to the Rosicrucian call exploded across Germany and the surrounding territories. Frances Yates, in The Rosicrucian Enlightenment (1972), interpreted the manifesto as the culmination of a Hermetic-neoplatonic tradition stretching from the Italian Renaissance through John Dee and Giordano Bruno; this thesis has been substantially qualified by subsequent scholars who emphasize the manifesto's Lutheran-Paracelsian rather than Hermetic character. The Fama attacks Aristotelian natural philosophy, promises a new universal science grounded in the macrocosm-microcosm correspondence, and frames its reform program within a Lutheran Protestant theology that identifies the Pope as Antichrist. Lawrence Principe, in The Secrets of Alchemy (2013), notes that while the manifesto draws on Paracelsian natural philosophy — particularly the vocabulary of the Mysterium Magnum and the doctrine of signatures — it subordinates chemical practice to spiritual and religious goals. The authorship of the Fama is uncertain; Gilly and others attribute it to the Tübingen circle of Johann Valentin Andreae, Tobias Hess, and Christoph Besold. The immediate result of the Fama's publication was a wave of speculation, imitation, and controversy — an intellectual event of the first order in European history.
