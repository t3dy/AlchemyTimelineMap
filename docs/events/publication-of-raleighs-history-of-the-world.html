Scholarly Context

P. M. Rattansi analyzed the History of the World's engagement with alchemy and natural magic in 'Alchemy and Natural Magic in Raleigh's History of the World' in Ambix vol. 13 no. 3 (1966) pp.122-138.

Sir Walter Raleigh's History of the World, composed during his imprisonment in the Tower of London and published in April 1614, contained a systematic engagement with alchemy, Hermeticism, and neo-Platonic natural magic that P. M. Rattansi, in Ambix (1966), analyzes as a major document of early seventeenth-century English attitudes toward the occult sciences. Rattansi demonstrates that the History presents Raleigh's engagement with the Florentine Platonic tradition — particularly Ficino's neo-Platonism with its postulate of a universe 'shot through with secret sympathies and antipathies' — as the natural-philosophical framework within which chemical investigations made sense. Raleigh defends the secrecy with which alchemists guarded their knowledge, refers to the 'secret virtues hidden in nature which the chemists sought to put to human use,' and explicitly uses the figure of the chemist as an analogy for the historian's method: as a skilled chemist draws medicine from poison by 'separation of visible elements,' so the historian extracts truth from legend. James I suppressed the first edition of the History — commanding it to be called in 'for divers exceptions, but specially for beeing too sawcie in censuring princes' — but the book continued to circulate and was reissued in a second edition (1628) and subsequent printings throughout the seventeenth century. Rattansi situates the History in relation to James I's very different views on natural magic and demonology (expressed in his Daemonologie, 1597) and to Francis Bacon's contemporaneous programme for the reform of natural philosophy, arguing that Raleigh's Hermetic-Paracelsian framework represents an intellectual position distinct from both Jacobean demonology and Baconian empiricism.
