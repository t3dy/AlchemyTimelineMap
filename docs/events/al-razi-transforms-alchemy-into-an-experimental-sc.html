Scholarly Context

Gerard Heym analyzed al-Razi as the creator of experimental alchemy, drawing on Julius Ruska's 1937 German translation of the Kitab Sirr al-Asrar, in Al-Razi and Alchemy in Ambix vol. 1 no. 3 (1938) pp.184-191.

Abu Bakr Muhammad ibn Zakariya al-Razi (865-925 CE), the Persian physician and natural philosopher known in Latin as Rhazes, composed the Kitab al-Asrar (Book of Secrets) and related alchemical treatises during the height of the Islamic Golden Age, and in doing so became -- in the judgment of Gerard Heym, in Ambix (1938) -- the first to transform theoretical alchemy into a strictly experimental system. Where earlier Greek alchemy (especially the Jabir corpus) had been dominated by Isma'ili speculative philosophical frameworks, al-Razi's alchemical work rejects that speculative philosophy entirely and replaces it with a book of experiments. Al-Razi described specific procedures -- calcination as depriving bodies of their association with one another and burning off any sulphur or oil they contain; sublimation; distillation; crystallization -- and assigned each chemical agent a specific role in transformation. Heym identifies in al-Razi's system the origin of the Mercury-Sal-Sulphur triad of later European alchemy: mercury, ammonium chloride, sulphur, and arsenic (Zarnikh) are treated as four spirits that modify the colour, texture, and transformability of metals, through processes of sublimation, distillation, washing, roasting, and boiling. Al-Razi's theory of matter posited five eternal principles (jauhar): the Creator, soul, matter, time, and space; bodies were composed of indivisible atoms separated by empty space, with the characteristics of the four Aristotelian elements (earth, water, air, fire) determined by atomic density. According to al-Biruni, al-Razi composed 21 alchemical works in total. The Kitab al-Asrar was translated into German by Julius Ruska in 1937. Heym argues that al-Razi's system, though theoretical, is as internally consistent as any other scientific system when judged on its own terms. His influence was acknowledged at Dutch universities as late as the early seventeenth century.
