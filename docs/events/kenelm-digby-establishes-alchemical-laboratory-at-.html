Scholarly Context

Betty Jo Dobbs reconstructed Digby's Gresham College activities from Aubrey's Lives, Digby's own Of the Vegetation of Plants (1660/1669), and the Hartlib Papers laboratory inventory in 'Studies in the Natural Philosophy of Sir Kenelm Digby Part II: Digby and Alchemy' in Ambix vol. 20 no. 3 (1973) pp.143-163.

Following the death of his wife Venetia in 1633, Sir Kenelm Digby (1603–1665) withdrew from public life for approximately two years and 'diverted himselfe with his chymistry, and the professors' good conversation' at Gresham College in London. Betty Jo Dobbs, in Ambix (1973), reconstructs the period from John Aubrey's brief lives and Digby's own later recollections: Digby had at Gresham 'a fair and large Laboratory... erected under the Lodgings of the Divinity-Reader' and his lodgings were 'at the end of your great Gallery.' An inventory of the laboratory's contents, dated 1 August 1648 and preserved in the Hartlib Papers at Sheffield University, lists a substantial and technically sophisticated installation across multiple rooms: a great glass case with several large glasses, an anatomy board with drawers, a drying oven, an oven with iron retorts, a deliquum stone and cellar, a reverberating calcining oven, a balneum mariae (water bath), sand and retort furnaces, a furnace for retorts in open fire, an athanor or digestive furnace, twenty alembic bodies of several sizes, glass basins, glass funnels, iron trivets, earthenware vessels, a water cistern, grinding stones of speckled marble, a large screw press, stone mortars, and three vaporatories. During this period Digby employed the Hungarian laboratory operator Johannes Banfi Hunnyades (1576–1650), who arrived in England in 1632 and became a favourite among gentlemen alchemists; Digby also conducted experiments on palingenesis, attempting to revivify plants and animals from their calcined ashes — including successful experiments with calcined crayfishes.
