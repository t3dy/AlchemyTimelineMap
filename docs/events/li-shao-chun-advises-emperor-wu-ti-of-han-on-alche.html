Scholarly Context

F. Sherwood Taylor analyzed the earliest Chinese alchemical records and their relation to Greek alchemy, citing the Han Shu XXV passage translated by Arthur Waley, in The Origins of Greek Alchemy in Ambix vol. 1 no. 1 (1937) pp.30-47.

The wizard Li Shao-chun advised the Han Emperor Wu Ti (r. 141-87 BCE) that by sacrificing to the kitchen god Tsao, spirits could be summoned, and that cinnabar powder could then be transmuted into yellow gold. Vessels made from this gold, Li Shao-chun claimed, would prolong life. His speech is preserved in the Han Shu (History of the Former Han), chapter XXV, as translated by Arthur Waley: Sacrifice to the stove (tsao) and you will be able to summon things (spirits). Summon spirits and you will be able to change cinnabar powder into yellow gold. With this yellow gold you make vessels to eat and to drink out of. You will then increase your span of life. F. Sherwood Taylor, in Ambix (1937), draws on this passage to conclude that Chinese alchemy was probably in existence at least as early as 200 BCE, antedating the earliest Greek alchemical writings by about two centuries. The central doctrine visible here -- that artificially prepared gold prolongs life because gold is especially rich in the positive Yang principle -- is, Taylor emphasizes, totally absent from Western alchemy of the first millennium CE. The idea that gold could be made from cinnabar (mercury sulphide) reflects the Taoist principle that certain substances rich in Yang can sustain life. This text represents the earliest historically dated alchemical episode in the record: the application of alchemical theory to royal longevity. Although the two great traditions -- Chinese and Hellenistic Egyptian -- developed independently, the Chinese tradition arrived independently at the conception of alchemical gold as a medicine for prolonging life, which would only enter Western alchemy via Arabic intermediaries in the medieval period.
