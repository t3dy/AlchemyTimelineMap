Scholarly Context

Martha Baldwin analyzed van Helmont's testimony about Aguilon's alchemy in 'Alchemy and the Society of Jesus in the Seventeenth Century: Strange Bedfellows?' in Ambix vol. 40 no. 2 (1993) pp.41-64.

Jan Baptist van Helmont, in a manuscript written ca. 1624 defending himself against heresy charges, reported witnessing alchemy experiments conducted by the Jesuit mathematician François Aguilon (1566–1617) at the Jesuit College in Antwerp in 1596. Martha Baldwin, in Ambix (1993), analyzes this testimony as the 'earliest evidence of Jesuit involvement with alchemical projects in the seventeenth century,' predating even the formal Jesuit educational documents that excluded alchemy from the curriculum. According to van Helmont's account, Aguilon had enlisted an artisan goldsmith to teach him manual operations for transmutation and had 'directed repeated experiments attempting the transmutation of gold from various forms of silver,' using 'glass vessels imprinted with the yellow seal of the Society of Jesus' — a detail that led van Helmont to conclude that the experiments 'had not happened without the approval of the Jesuit superiors.' Aguilon ultimately abandoned alchemy after failing to achieve transmutation and turned to optics, publishing the major Opticorum Libri Sex (Antwerp, 1613). Baldwin notes that van Helmont's account, made as part of a legal self-defense against a Jesuit-backed Inquisition charge in 1634, was 'never refuted by those ecclesiastics involved in his trial' — a silence that lends some credibility to his testimony. The episode documents the presence of chrysopoeic experimentation within the institutional structure of the Society of Jesus despite its official exclusion from the Jesuit curriculum under the Ratio Studiorum of 1599.
