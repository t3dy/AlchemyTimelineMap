1317 Medieval (7th–15th c.)

Avignon

France

1317, Avignon: Pope John XXII (r. 1316–1334), in the course of a broader campaign against magic in all its forms, issues a decretal Spondent quas non exhibent ('They promise what they do not deliver') condemning the practice of alchemy. The decretal addresses fraudulent gold-making, prescribing that alchemists who defraud buyers with false gold be required to pay into the public treasury a quantity of true gold equal to the amount of false gold they have produced; those who cannot pay are to be imprisoned. Noel L. Brann, in 'Alchemy and Melancholy in Medieval and Renaissance Thought' (Ambix 32:3, 1985), notes that the decretal was issued at a moment of widespread alchemical activity and that its existence testifies to the art's popularity even as it attempted to regulate it. The decretal was not a condemnation of alchemy per se — it did not deny the theoretical possibility of transmutation — but targeted fraudulent practitioners. It was incorporated into canon law and was repeatedly cited in later legal and theological discussions of alchemy. William R. Newman and Lawrence M. Principe discuss the legal and institutional context of the papal condemnation in Alchemy Tried in the Fire (Chicago, 2002), and in Promissory Notes on the Treasury of Merits and related studies.
