1603 Early Modern (15th–18th c.)

Strasbourg

France/Alsace

1603, Strasbourg: The Lotharingian physician Nicolas Guibert (ca. 1547–ca. 1620) publishes Alchymia ratione et experientia ita demum viriliter impugnata (Alchemy manfully attacked by reason and experience, Strasbourg, 1603), a comprehensive denunciation of alchemy that targets both the authenticity of its foundational texts and the physical possibility of metallic transmutation. Drawing on forty years of his own failed alchemical practice, Guibert challenges the alchemical attributions of Hermes Trismegistus, Aristotle, Albertus Magnus, Thomas Aquinas, and Raymond Lull (the last he claims was deluded by demons), and denies that the substantial forms of metals can be reproduced or transmuted by art. The work provokes a series of published rebuttals: Andreas Libavius responds in 1604 and 1615; Ewald von Hoghelande replies in 1604; Bernard Penot publishes a counter in 1608. Guibert's arguments were subsequently revived by Guerner Rolfinck in Chimia in artis formam redacta (1641) and Hermann Conring in De Hermetica Aegyptiorum vetere (1648), making Guibert's critique a persistent touchstone for the anti-alchemical tradition well into the mid-seventeenth century. Jennifer M. Rampling, in 'Transmission and Transmutation' (Early Science and Medicine 17, 2012, pp.493-495), analyses the enduring influence of the Guibert controversy on the editorial strategy of continental alchemical publishers.
