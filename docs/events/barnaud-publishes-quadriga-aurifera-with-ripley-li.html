1599 Early Modern (15th–18th c.)

Leiden

Netherlands

1599, Leiden: The French physician and editor Nicolas Barnaud (ca. 1539–ca. 1604) publishes the Quadriga aurifera (Four-wheeled golden chariot), a compendium of four alchemical texts: the first is an anonymous tract on the philosophy of metals; the second — the centerpiece — is George Ripley's Liber duodecim portarum (Latin translation of the Compound of Alchymy), presented as the most complete Latin version yet available; the third is Ripley's Liber de mercurio et lapide philosophorum; and the fourth is a Paracelsian tract on the elixir of the sun. The edition is dedicated to the English poet and courtier Sir Edward Dyer (1543–1607), whom Barnaud had likely encountered in Prague during Dyer's repeated visits in 1588–1590 in pursuit of Edward Kelley's alchemical knowledge. Jennifer M. Rampling, in 'Transmission and Transmutation' (Early Science and Medicine 17, 2012, pp.488-495), establishes that Barnaud's edition derives from a manuscript that had long been in his possession — not from the English print tradition — and is closely related to Penot's 1595 compilation; both editions omit Ripley's controversial satirical verses on fraudulent alchemists and the 'Admonition' describing his experimental failures. The Quadriga aurifera was reprinted in the Theatrum chemicum (Strasbourg, 1602, 1613, 1659-61), disseminating the Ripleian Latin text widely through continental Europe and providing Andreas Libavius with the primary basis for his own 'Analysis duodecim portarum' commentary.
