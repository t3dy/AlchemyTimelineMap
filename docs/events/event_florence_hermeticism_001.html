c. 1460 Early Modern (15th–18th c.)

Florence

Italy

c. 1460 CE, Florence: The Neoplatonic philosopher Marsilio Ficino, working under the patronage of Cosimo de' Medici, begins translating the Corpus Hermeticum, a collection of Hellenistic philosophical and mystical texts attributed to the sage Hermes Trismegistus. Ficino's translation, completed in 1463, presents the Hermetic texts as repositories of ancient wisdom prefiguring Christian truth, establishing the doctrine of Prisca Theologia (Ancient Theology)—the claim that pagan antiquity possessed revealed truth. This intellectual framework elevates alchemy, as practiced in the Hermetic tradition, from marginal craft to expression of timeless wisdom. Ficino's Neoplatonism, with its emphasis on the unity of all being and the possibility of spiritual ascent through contemplation of cosmic principles, provides philosophical ground for alchemy's integration of material operations with spiritual aspiration. The Florentine Academy, centered on Ficino's work, becomes the intellectual epicenter of Renaissance engagement with Hermeticism and alchemy. Though Ficino himself engages alchemy primarily as philosophical and theological tradition rather than operational craft, his translation and commentary establish the intellectual authority within which Renaissance alchemists conduct their work. Ficino's Florence marks the transition from medieval alchemy toward Renaissance alchemy's synthesis of operational practice with Hermetic philosophy and Neoplatonic theology.
