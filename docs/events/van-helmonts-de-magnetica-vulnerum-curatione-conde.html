Scholarly Context

Georgiana D. Hedesan identified the condemnation of De magnetica vulnerum curatione by the Mechelen curia as the beginning of Van Helmont's conflict with Catholic authorities, drawing on archival sources in the Mechelen Archbishopric Archives, in 'The Influence of Louvain Teaching on Jan Baptist Van Helmont's Adoption of Paracelsianism and Alchemy' in Ambix vol. 68 nos. 2-3 (2021) pp.231-246.

In 1621, Jan Baptist van Helmont (1579–1644) published De magnetica vulnerum curatione (On the Magnetic Cure of Wounds), a treatise defending the weapon salve — a Paracelsian remedy that involved treating a wound by applying ointment to the weapon that caused it rather than to the wound itself. The underlying theory was that the weapon retained a vital sympathetic connection to the wound through the Paracelsian concept of Blas (vital force) and that alchemical seminal principles could act at a distance. Georgiana D. Hedesan, in Ambix (2021), identifies this publication as the immediate cause of Van Helmont's collision with Catholic ecclesiastical authorities. The Mechelen ecclesiastical curia condemned the treatise (along with Otto Van Veen's Physicae et Theologicae Conclusiones) as having been written uno eodemque daemonis spiritu (in one and the same demonic spirit), and characterized both authors as disciples instructed in Paracelsi schola (in the school of Paracelsus). The former Louvain professors Thomas Feyens and Gerard de Villers were among the signatories of a 1623 letter intended to censure Van Helmont's writings. The weapon salve controversy had broader European antecedents: Rudolf Goclenius of the University of Marburg and the Jesuit Jean Roberti of Douai had already been engaged in a polemic over the topic. Van Helmont's defense drew him into this controversy and triggered a long legal battle with the Inquisition that eventually resulted in house arrest ca. 1634, and delayed the publication of his major work until the posthumous Ortus medicinae (Amsterdam, 1648).
