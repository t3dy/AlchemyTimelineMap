Scholarly Context

Jennifer M. Rampling identified the 1591 Rabbards edition as the first vernacular alchemical text printed in England and analyzed the Elizabethan expansion of the Ripley corpus, in 'The Catalogue of the Ripley Corpus: Alchemical Writings Attributed to George Ripley (d. ca. 1490)' in Ambix vol. 57 no. 2 (2010) pp.125-201.

In 1591, Ralph Rabbards published the first vernacular alchemical work printed in England: George Ripley's The Compound of Alchymy, divided into Twelve Gates, a long alchemical poem composed ca. 1471 in late Middle English rhyme royal, in a quarto volume of approximately one hundred pages. Jennifer M. Rampling, in Ambix (2010), identifies this publication as a landmark event in English alchemical history: Rabbards dedicated the work to Queen Elizabeth I and promoted Ripley as an authority who 'lived in the time of king Edward the 4 ... in great fame and estimation, for his rare knowledge in these secrets.' The publication brought into print, for the first time, a work that had circulated widely in manuscript since the 1470s — a Middle English alchemical poem that Rampling describes as the single most influential alchemical text in the English language, 'the flower of all the books of the philosophers' in the words of a manuscript note from 1525. The 1591 Rabbards edition was subsequently reprinted in Elias Ashmole's Theatrum chemicum Britannicum (London, 1652), which added dozens of shorter Ripleian works with scholarly notes. Rampling's research demonstrates that the 1591 printing marks the high-water point of a process that had been underway throughout the Elizabethan period: the dramatic expansion of the corpus of works attributed to Ripley, as both genuine and pseudoepigraphic texts circulated in increasing numbers of manuscript copies, were translated from Latin to English and English to Latin, and were read across Europe from Venice to France. The printed Compound cemented Ripley's reputation as the pre-eminent English alchemical authority, a status he held through the seventeenth century.
