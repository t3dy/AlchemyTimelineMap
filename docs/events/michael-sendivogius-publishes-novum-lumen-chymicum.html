Scholarly Context

Zbigniew Szydlo analyzed the Novum Lumen Chymicum and Sendivogius's nitre theory in 'The Alchemy of Michael Sendivogius: His Central Nitre Theory' in Ambix vol. 40 no. 3 (1993) pp.129-146. William Newman discussed the Seton legend and the Sendivogian context in 'Prophecy and Alchemy' in Ambix vol. 37 no. 3 (1990) pp.97-115.

Michael Sendivogius (1566–1636), a Polish nobleman and court alchemist serving Emperor Rudolf II, published his De Lapide Philosophorum Tractatus Duodecim — soon known as the Novum Lumen Chymicum — simultaneously in Prague and Frankfurt under the anagram 'Divi Leschi Genus Amo.' Zbigniew Szydlo, in Ambix (1993), identifies the work's central chemical contribution as its theory of aerial nitre: the doctrine that the atmosphere contains a hidden vital nourishment, a 'central nitre' or 'food of life,' that sustains organic growth and drives chemical processes — a theoretical framework that Szydlo situates as a significant step between Paracelsian salt-sulphur-mercury theory and the eighteenth-century chemistry of atmospheric gases. Szydlo notes that the Novum Lumen Chymicum went through more than fifty editions in five languages before 1797, making it one of the most widely read alchemical texts of the early modern period. The work's enormous reputation rested partly on the legend — recorded by Pierre Borel in 1655 and widely believed — that Sendivogius had obtained the manuscript from the dying Scottish adept Alexander Seton; William Newman (Ambix, 1990) demonstrates that this attribution is almost certainly apocryphal. Sendivogius served at the courts of King Sigismund III Vasa of Poland and Emperors Rudolf II and Ferdinand II at Prague and Vienna, and the Novum Lumen Chymicum was one of several works connected to these Rudolfine courtly contexts that made Prague a centre of alchemical publishing at the turn of the seventeenth century.
