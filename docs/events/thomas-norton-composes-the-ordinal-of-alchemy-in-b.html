Scholarly Context

Jennifer M. Rampling describes the Ordinal of Alchemy as 'the only English work of the fifteenth century to rival Ripley's Compound in posthumous celebrity,' identifies Thomas Norton as a Bristol alchemical practitioner (d. 1513), and notes that the Ordinal was 'probably also intended for presentation' to a patron, in The Experimental Fire: Inventing English Alchemy, 1300–1700 (Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 2020), pp.4, 63, 105–106. See also Rampling, 'The Englishing of Medieval Alchemy,' Ambix 63:3 (2016) pp.268–272.

In 1477, the Bristol alchemical practitioner Thomas Norton (d. 1513) completed the Ordinal of Alchemy, a Middle English poem in seven chapters that would become, alongside George Ripley's Compound of Alchemy (1471), one of the two canonical texts of fifteenth-century English alchemy and a touchstone for English alchemical practice for the next two centuries. Jennifer M. Rampling, in The Experimental Fire: Inventing English Alchemy, 1300–1700 (University of Chicago Press, 2020), describes the Ordinal as 'the only English work of the fifteenth century to rival Ripley's Compound in posthumous celebrity,' and observes that, like Ripley's poems, it 'was probably also intended for presentation' to a high-ranking patron — though the documentary context has not survived. Norton's poem opens with a 'Prohemium' warning against the corruption of alchemical texts: 'And changing of som oone sillable / May make this boke vnprofitable.' The Ordinal is notable for its sophisticated integration of Chaucer's 'Canon's Yeoman's Tale' as an authority on alchemical fraud, and for its sustained dialogue with a fictional interlocutor named Tonsile, whose failed alchemical career serves as a cautionary example. Norton drew on a wide range of earlier authorities including pseudo-Arnold of Villanova and the pseudo-Lullian corpus. The Ordinal circulated widely in manuscript; at least three manuscript copies were associated with Henry VII. Rampling and Rampling (2016) both identify Thomas Norton alongside Ripley as providing 'the authoritative backbone of fifteenth-century English alchemy.' Norton's great-grandson, Samuel Norton (1548–1604), would become a prominent alchemical practitioner in Elizabethan England and drew heavily on the Ordinal in his own alchemical Key (1577). The text was printed by Elias Ashmole in the Theatrum Chemicum Britannicum (London, 1652) and received its modern critical edition from John Reidy for the Early English Text Society (Oxford, 1975).
