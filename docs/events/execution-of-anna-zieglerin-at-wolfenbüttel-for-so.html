Scholarly Context

Tara Nummedal established the historical record of Anna Zieglerin's execution at Wolfenbüttel in February 1575 and analyzed its historiographic significance for the intersection of gender and alchemy in the Holy Roman Empire, in Anna Zieglerin and the Lion's Blood: Alchemy and End Times in Reformation Germany (Philadelphia: University of Pennsylvania Press, 2019), reviewed by Bruce Janacek in Ambix vol. 67 no. 4 (2020) pp.1-8.

On 7 February 1575, Anna Maria Zieglerin (born ca. 1545?), an alchemical practitioner who had worked at the court of Duke Julius of Braunschweig-Wolfenbüttel since 1571, was executed at Wolfenbüttel for charges that included sorcery, fraud, theft, adultery, and infanticide. Tara Nummedal, in Anna Zieglerin and the Lion's Blood: Alchemy and End Times in Reformation Germany (University of Pennsylvania Press, 2019), establishes Anna's execution as a landmark case in the intersection of gender, alchemy, and judicial power in the early modern Holy Roman Empire. The first written record of her death is from 1584, when the chronicler Heinrich Bünting described Anna's end simply as: '[Heinrich's] wife Anna Maria was burned to death as a sorceress.' However, Nummedal argues that the iron chair displayed in the Landesmuseum in Braunschweig as the instrument of her execution — 'the witch's chair' in which, according to tradition, she was burned alive — has no documentary support in the trial records, and that her execution probably took the form of 'death by fire' without such theatrical apparatus. Anna's case forms a paradigmatic instance of what Nummedal calls the 'gendered assault on spiritual and sexual integrity' deployed against female alchemical practitioners: the accusations against her were not purely technical (failed experiments, fraudulent claims) but systematically personal, targeting her sexuality, her body, and her religious claims. Anna had participated with her husband Heinrich Schombach and the alchemist Philipp Sömmering in alchemical experiments sponsored by the Duke's court; all three were implicated in the disastrous Grumbach Feud at Gotha (where Duke Johann Friedrich II had been inspired by a peasant boy claiming angelic visitations to rebel against the Emperor), and that association haunted them throughout their time at Wolfenbüttel. Torture led to confessions; confessions led to execution.
